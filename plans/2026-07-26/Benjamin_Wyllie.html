1

The one-line verdict

Why four of six didn't flip

He ratifies the wait instead of reframing it.

What actually happened

On YACOVELLI the customer had already handed him the reason to act — I've got a baby coming on the way, so I'm not letting my kid have no air conditioning in their house — and then chose to run the system until it failed. Ben laid out proactive versus reactive fairly, accepted the answer, and referred them to the home warranty. On PARRISH the same customer position produced the same ending.

What the FSG model calls for

A customer choosing to wait is not a closed door; it is a different door. Stop selling the repair and sell the preparation: Totally fair — most people don't fix an old system on a maybe. So let's not talk repair at all. With a baby coming, the thing that would actually hurt is finding out the cost and the lead time on the day it quits. Our advisor comes out free and gets you the number now. Thursday morning, or is afternoon better? That converts a no-close into a booked appointment without arguing with anybody.

4

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • The expectations block, word for word, every time. Six beats including the one almost nobody says: This all for a five star experience? If at any point I'm not doing that, please correct me on it. He asks customers to hold him to a standard.
  • The best analogy of the day. To a second-year medical student: the compressor is like the heart of the system. I'm testing its blood pressure right now. He read the customer and built the explanation for that person.
  • He got a customer to close himself. On RAGHEB the homeowner reached the conclusion out loud — it wouldn't be financially smart to keep on dumping money in — and the flip followed from there. That is the hardest behavior in the rubric and he did it.
  • Honest about what he doesn't know. On YACOVELLI he could not pinpoint the noise and said so, rather than inventing a cause. Customers can tell.
  • He protects them from bad advice. A handyman had topped off a leaking system; Ben explained the EPA position and why it was not a fix, without running the other guy down.

6

This week's drill — the fallback that costs nothing

When they say wait, offer the free number

Trigger: any version of "we'll wait" / "run it till it dies" / "let me check the warranty."

Do not agree and do not argue. Say:

"Totally fair — most people don't fix an old system on a maybe. So let's not talk repair at all. The thing that would actually hurt is finding out the cost and the lead time on the day it quits. Our advisor comes out free and gets you that number now. Thursday morning, or is afternoon better?"

Then stop. Ben reached this exact moment three times in one day. Three booked advisor slots was available to him for the price of one sentence.

3-week training plan

Every "we'll wait" gets the free-advisor fallback offered with a specific day and time. Manager counts offers made versus waits heard.
Week 2
Write down the emotional line
Note the personal reason the customer gives — baby, health, tenants, budget — and bring it back at the first hesitation.
Week 3
Frame first, diagnose second
The expectations block before any finding is voiced, on every call. Plus harvest the five-star seed he already plants six times a day.
