1

The one-line verdict

What the tape shows

Elite trust-building, and no third door.

What actually happened

On a 2006 system he found an overheated compressor, a seized blower motor and a coil impacted with debris — and framed it this way: it's fixable. Just want to give you that peace of mind that we can fix it, get you back up online… just know that there are going to be repairs that will be coming down the line. On the second call, a dead compressor on a discontinued refrigerant got the same treatment: capacitor and fan now, compressor and blower motor at some point down the line.

What moves it

Both customers were told the truth about the equipment and then given a menu with one aisle. On a twenty-year-old system with three failed components, the third option is not optional: "I can get you running today, and I want you to see the replacement number next to it — not because you have to do it, but because you shouldn't be choosing between repairs without knowing what the alternative costs." He earned the right to say that on both calls and never used it.

5

What Joe did right — real strength, build on it

  • He beat the handyman without saying a word against him. Having someone like that is very beneficial — I'm that for a lot of my family members. But when we're called, unfortunately family tends to take a backseat until we can talk to you guys. Empathy, no attack, and a reason. Best objection handling recorded this day.
  • He repaired the company's reputation, not just the unit. A customer believed a previous tech had skipped points on the checklist. Joe explained why an older coil doesn't get a chemical wash, and got he did not explain it to me the way you explain it back. That is a save most techs turn into an argument.
  • Discovery that presses for a real answer (Required Questions — Strong). And how often are we really changing the filters? Then he refused the vague answer and offered ranges until he got a number — and used it later against the impacted coil.
  • He gave away work and told them so, honestly. Recovering the compressor from thermal overload for free, and naming what other companies would have charged for it, buys more credibility than any discount.

The three gaps

each one in Joe's own calls
Moderate

Gap 1 — Two end-of-life systems, and neither customer saw a replacement number

Joe"The unit is original to the house, which is from 2006… so system's 20 years old. It's fixable. Just want to give you that peace of mind that we can fix it."
Joe, second call"The refrigerant that runs through the system, similar to cars, has been discontinued. That's what makes this price what it is." — and the conversation stayed on repairs.

The fix Both customers were being asked to spend real money on equipment with a short future, without ever being shown the alternative. That isn't restraint — it's an incomplete option set, and it's the one thing standing between Joe's trust-building and a flip. "Here's what gets you cold today. And because this system is twenty years old on a refrigerant they've stopped making, I want a specialist to give you the replacement number too — free, no obligation. I'd rather you compare than guess." He has more permission to say that than anyone on this board.

Moderate

Gap 2 — He asked about the power bill and never heard the answer

Joe"80's that sweet spot for you guys? How does that reflect on your guys' power bill? How are the power bills?"
What followedThe conversation moved to the service disconnect key and never came back to it. On the other no-cool call the question was never asked at all.

The fix He asks it and then lets it go. On a system running a dead compressor and an overheating fan, the bill is the number that makes future repairs feel urgent instead of optional. Ask it, wait for the figure, write it down, and use it: "You said around two-fifty in July. A chunk of that is this system fighting itself — that's what we're actually fixing."

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Always a third door
Drill: Any system over fifteen years old, or on a discontinued refrigerant, gets a replacement option on the report — every time, framed as a comparison rather than a recommendation.
Proof it worked: Every aging-system report this week contains three tiers, not two.
Week 2
Get the bill figure
Drill: Ask the power-bill question, then stay silent until a number comes back, and write it down. Use it in the presentation.
Proof it worked: A customer-supplied bill figure appears in every assessment on tape.
Week 3
Both ends of the five
Drill: Five-star seed added to the expectation set; five-star ask added to every close, including no-closes.
Proof it worked: Six-for-six expectations on two pulled tapes; a review ask on every completed visit.
8

What we owe Joe Mendoza

  • Record his handyman answer and teach it. That thirty seconds is the best competitive-objection handling on this board and it should be a training clip, not a private skill.
  • Back him on free work. He recovered a compressor at no charge and told the customer plainly that others would have billed for it. That builds enormous trust — he needs to know it's supported, not tolerated.
  • Recording that runs past the presentation. Three tapes, three cut-offs before the numbers. The most important twenty minutes of his day are invisible.
  • An easy path to a same-day replacement estimate. His hesitation to bring up replacement may be about not wanting to seem like a salesman. Give him a no-obligation, no-cost specialist offer that doesn't feel like one.
Bottom line

Joe is the best trust-builder on this board and it isn't close — he neutralised a competing handyman and a complaint about Sierra's own work on the same day, and both customers ended up believing him more, not less. The single thing holding him back is that he keeps aging systems in repair-land. Give both of those customers a replacement number next to the repair number and his close rate looks very different, without him changing anything else he does.
