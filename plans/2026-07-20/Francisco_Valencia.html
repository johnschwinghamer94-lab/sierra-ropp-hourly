The one-line verdict

Why it flipped

He offered the third door first, and made it safe to take.

What actually happened

Before any number was quoted, Francisco framed the real decision: because of what we're seeing out there and just the age, one of our options is going to be to talk to one of our equipment specialists and comparing between patch-up work and more of a permanent fix. Because for either one we have payment programs… The least thing we want is for you guys to commit to something and then it's going to put you guys in a burden.

What moves it

And when the customer said he needed to talk to his wife and process it, Francisco didn't push — before you do anything, of course. Let's try to get informed as much as possible — then converted that into a time: a specialist slot the following afternoon after five, checked against the schedule rather than promised. No pressure, and still a booking.

What Francisco did right — real strength, build on it

  • Option C offered before the first price (Building Options — Strong). Most techs reach for the specialist after the repair number lands badly. Francisco set it up as one of three planned options from the start, which is why it did not feel like a fallback.
  • Financing raised without being asked. On both paths, unprompted, with the reason stated plainly: the least thing we want is for you guys to commit to something and then it's going to put you guys in a burden. That sentence is why the customer trusted the rest.
  • He let the customer build the argument (Get Customer to Diagnose — Strong). So I can spread it out a little bit? came from the customer, not from him. That is the objective in the rubric, achieved cleanly.
  • Shoe covers and a partner who wears them too. Small, visible, and he narrated it so the customer knew it was deliberate.

The three gaps

each one in Francisco's own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — Zero of the three critical questions, on both calls

What he asked"How long have you guys been in the house?" · "Is this the first time you guys have issues with it?" · "Have you guys had any maintenance or service?"
What he never askedHow often are you changing your filters? · Which rooms get hotter or colder than the rest? · What do you keep the thermostat at in the summer — and what does a standard bill look like at that setting?

The fix This is the automatic fail on an otherwise excellent call, and it cost him a real weapon. He was selling replacement against repair on a sixteen-year-old system to a household on a budget with a new baby — and the single strongest argument for replacement is the monthly bill, which he never got a number for. Ask the setting, then the bill, before the tools come out. Then the specialist conversation has arithmetic in it, not just age.

Gap 2 — The customer had to ask him to speak plainly

Francisco"It's almost at 24 amps when this thing only calls for 25… your fan motor is rated for 1.5, and your fan motor was running at almost 1.7."
Customer"That's kind of like super hard to understand. So it's like, just tell me like, hey, this is wrong."

The fix He can do plain English — he proved it in the very next sentence, and the spark-plug analogy later is outstanding. The habit to change is the order: verdict first, evidence second. "Your fan motor has failed — that's why you have no cooling. Here's how I know: it's pulling more power than it's rated for." Same facts, and the customer never has to interrupt to get the answer.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Three questions
Drill: Filters, hot and cold rooms, thermostat setting plus the bill follow-up — asked on every call before tools come out. Written on the tablet if needed.
Proof it worked: A customer-supplied bill figure and a filter answer in every assessment on tape.
Week 2
Verdict first
Drill: Every finding starts with the plain-English conclusion, then the evidence. Manager role-plays five findings; Francisco has to lead with the verdict each time.
Proof it worked: No tape where a customer has to ask him to simplify.
Week 3
Finish the expectations
Drill: Approval promise, same-day offer and five-star seed added to the sequence he already runs. Review ask on every completed visit.
Proof it worked: Six-for-six on two pulled tapes; review asks on all completed visits.

What we owe Francisco Valencia

  • Teach his Option C framing to the board. Raising the specialist comparison before the first price — with financing on both paths — is the single most transferable behaviour recorded this day.
  • Protect the no-pressure close. He told a customer to go talk to his wife and still got the appointment. Nobody should coach that instinct out of him; the coaching is about the front of the call, not the back.
  • A printed question card. His decision step is the strongest on this board and his discovery is the weakest. That gap closes with a card, not a course.
  • Real financing terms he can quote. He was asked about rates and fees by an accountant and had to defer on most of it. Give him the current terms so the strongest part of his pitch doesn't stall on a detail.
Bottom line

Francisco runs the best decision step on this board — options before pricing, financing on every path, honest analogies, and a flip earned without a single push. The whole gap is at the front of the call: he never asks the three questions, so he arrives at the most important conversation of the visit without the customer's own numbers to use. Fix the first five minutes and nothing else about his call needs to change.
