1

The one-line verdict

Why both flipped

He sold the uncertainty, and the customers bought the certainty.

What actually happened

On the first call he told the homeowner the repair might not work before quoting it: we could just be putting good money in a bad place. The customer's response was immediate — I think I would like to do what we need to do to get this up and running today, but I'd also like a bid to what it would take to replace the whole system. Andrew's answer: I can make a call and have someone come on by while I'm writing my report… they can be here within the hour.

What moves it

On the second call the same honesty produced the same result, and this time he offered the specialist explicitly — you have the option where you can talk to one of our specialists. Completely for free — and had it booked for eight the next morning before he left. Two for two, both earned by being straight about what he could not promise.

5

What Andrew did right — real strength, build on it

  • Two flips in one day, both customer-initiated (Overcoming Objections — Strong). Nobody else on this board did that. Both came after he told the customer what he could not promise.
  • He kills his own sale before he makes it (Check Your Tone — Strong). I can get this part installed, I have it in my van right now. But I can't guarantee that the compressor is going to roll over after we install this. That sentence costs him a same-day repair and buys him the replacement conversation.
  • He asked about the membership instead of pitching it. What's keeping you away from being on our membership? — then listened to a real answer about auto-renewals and a tankless water heater making part of the plan useless, and didn't argue with any of it. That is how you learn something worth passing back to the company.
  • Advice that cost him money. Telling a customer with five thermostats to keep the zones close together, so the system runs less, is straightforwardly against his own short-term interest. It is also exactly why both of these customers said yes to the next step.

The three gaps

Gap 1 — The expectation set is two sentences long

What he says"I'd let you know in terms of what you can expect while I'm here. So I'm doing a visual inspection of both the systems."
What is never said"I'll do a thorough evaluation." · "I won't do anything without your approval." · "If you approve it we can start today." · "I'm here for a five-star experience."

The fix This is the automatic fail on the best-converting day on the board, and it is pure upside: everything downstream of his welcome already works. The approval promise in particular would do real work for him — both of these customers were braced for a sales pitch, and hearing "I won't do anything without your approval" at minute two removes the guard before the findings land. Four sentences. Nothing else about his call needs to change.

Gap 3 — The pricing arrived in pieces, and the paperwork got messy

Across the visitThe wiring repair, then "upwards of 6,500, if not over $7,000 for repairs when it comes to that system alone," then the leak search separately — spread across the call rather than presented together.
At the closeSignal problems forced two rounds of re-signatures, and the invoice explanation came out as "everything is 79 whatever."

The fix The work was excellent and the last ten minutes undercut it. Give the numbers once, as one picture — immediate, upcoming, and the comparison — so the customer can hold all three at the same time. And when the tablet fails, say what the number is out loud before asking for a signature: "It's seventy-eight dollars for today's diagnostic, and tomorrow's visit is free." Precision at the end protects everything that came before it.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Four sentences
Drill: Thorough evaluation, options, approval, today, five stars — added to the front of every call. Manager pulls two tapes and checks specifically for the approval promise.
Proof it worked: Six-for-six expectation beats on both pulled tapes.
Week 2
Discovery on demand calls too
Drill: The four questions asked on no-cool calls, not just maintenance visits. Filters, setting, hot and cold rooms, bill.
Proof it worked: All four answers present on every tape, demand and maintenance alike.
Week 3
One clean number, and the ask
Drill: Present immediate, upcoming and comparison together in one sitting. State every charge out loud before requesting a signature. Add the five-star ask at the close.
Proof it worked: No scattered pricing on tape; a review ask on every completed visit.

What we owe Andrew Trujillo

  • Say the two flips out loud in the huddle. Two for two, both customer-initiated, both earned by refusing to overpromise. That is the behaviour the rest of the board is being coached toward.
  • Pass the membership feedback up. A customer explained precisely why the plan doesn't work for them — auto-renewal friction and a tankless water heater making part of it worthless. Andrew surfaced it; somebody should act on it.
  • Fix the connectivity. He lost signal on both calls, had to redo signatures twice, and couldn't display a form the customer wanted to read. That is a customer-experience problem the company owns, not the tech.
  • An expectation card. His decision step is the strongest on this board and his welcome is the weakest. That is a printing job.
Bottom line

Andrew converted both of his calls, and he did it by telling customers what he could not guarantee before asking them for anything. That instinct is rare and it should be protected exactly as it is. What is missing sits entirely at the front of the visit: an expectation set that is two sentences long, and discovery questions that appear on one call and vanish on the other. Fix the first five minutes and this is the model call on the board.
