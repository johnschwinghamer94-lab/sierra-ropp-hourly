The one-line verdict

Why nothing sold

Great evidence, no ask.

What actually happened

Andrew found a drained start device, an oil-leaking condenser fan motor — the oil is running across it and spraying as it spins and runs. So this is my biggest concern outside, because that thing's going to overheat — and a contactor so far gone he said you can even see that it's starting to snap apart from wear and tear. He photographed all of it and pulled the husband in to see it. Then the visit ended on I look forward to seeing you next year.

What moves it

Three findings, two of them near-failure, on a ten-year-old system on a 108-degree day. Every ingredient of a close was already in the room and on the tablet. The missing beat is the simplest one in the model: go to the van, build the report, come back, and present options. He priced nothing, asked for nothing, and left the customer with photos and no decision to make.

5

What Andrew did right — real strength, build on it

  • Discovery most techs skip (Required Questions — Strong). Thermostat setting, the summer power bill, filter frequency and filter condition, all asked conversationally. He does the hard part of the assessment step well.
  • He brings in the second decision-maker on purpose. Do you want me to stop in with your husband as well, show him these? That way you guys could discuss while I'm stomping around up there. That is a deliberate, advanced move and it worked — the husband came out and engaged with the photos.
  • Photo-first evidence (Show Pictures — Strong). Every finding was shown, not described. The burnt contactor, the oil spray line, the drained start device — all on screen, in the homeowner's hands.
  • Genuine, easy rapport. Both homeowners clearly enjoyed the visit and the husband opened up completely. The trust required to sell three repairs was unquestionably in the room.

The three gaps

each one in Andrew's own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — Three failing components and not one price

Customer, directly"Fix that. The motor would have to be replaced? … Really? How much is that?"
Andrew"I'll get you the price for that at the end because I've only looked at half the system so far." — and the end of the visit came without it.
Andrew, on the contactor"This is one of the most burnt, burnt power switches I think I've ever seen. You can even see that it's starting to snap apart from wear and tear." — never priced, never offered.

The fix This is the whole call. The homeowner asked to be sold and the answer was "later." Deferring the price until you have the full picture is correct — but then you have to go build the report and come back. Non-negotiable sequence: finish the evaluation, go to the van, write it up, sit down, present. Twenty minutes of paperwork would have turned this visit into three approved repairs on a system the customer already agreed was at the end of its life.

Weak

Gap 2 — "My number one guy is my buddy" went unanswered

Customer, at the door"You got a running list of AC guys? My number one guy is my buddy. He's the big dog for [the builder]. You guys hit my neighbor. He's the one."
Andrew"All right, bro. You take care. Hey, you have a great day, man. I look forward to seeing you next year."

The fix That is a customer telling you exactly who he calls when something breaks, and it is not you. It is also the softest objection you will ever get — he likes you, he just has a habit. Empathy, question, help: "Makes sense — everybody's got a guy. Can I ask, does your guy carry the start device and the contactor on his truck? Because those two are the ones that strand you at midnight, and I've got both in the van right now." Answer the objection while you are still standing in the driveway.

Weak

Gap 3 — The expectations were never set, so nothing at the end had a place to land

What was said, mid-inspection"If I do notice concerns with the system, like genuine ones, you want to bring that to your attention?"
What was never said"I'll bring you back everything I find and show you your options." · "I won't do anything without your approval." · "My goal is to take care of it today." · "I'm here for a five-star experience." · "Do you need me out by any particular time?"

The fix The missing options promise and the missing presentation are the same failure seen from two ends. If the customer had been told at minute two that the visit ends with a report and options, the visit could not have ended on a handshake. Set all six beats in the first five minutes and the close builds itself into the schedule.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Always present
Drill: Every call ends in the van with a written report and a sit-down presentation — including maintenance visits with nothing urgent. No exceptions, no farewells at the attic ladder.
Proof it worked: A written report exists for every call on tape this week.
Week 2
Full LTOAT5
Drill: All six expectation beats in the first five minutes, plus the time-constraint check. Manager pulls two tapes and checks for the options and approval promises specifically.
Proof it worked: Six-for-six on both pulled tapes.
Week 3
Answer the objection
Drill: "I have my own guy" and "I'll wait until it breaks" rehearsed ten times with a manager — empathy, question, help. Plus the five-star ask on every completed visit.
Proof it worked: Every objection on tape gets a question; review asks on all completed visits.

What we owe Andrew Alonso

  • A clear standard: no visit ends without a report. Andrew's assessment work is genuinely good and it is being thrown away at the last step. This needs to be stated as a rule, not a suggestion.
  • Recognition for the discovery questions. He asks the power-bill follow-up naturally, which most of this board does not. Name it so he keeps it while the rest gets fixed.
  • Working recording equipment. Three of his four recordings this day captured almost nothing across four hours of field time — he cannot be coached on calls that do not exist. This needs checking before it costs him another week.
  • A printed expectation card. Six beats on the dash. His welcome is warm and personable already; it just needs the structure attached.
Bottom line

Andrew ran a strong assessment and a warm visit, and then walked out of a house holding photographs of three failing parts without ever naming a price. Nothing about his diagnosis, his questioning, or his rapport needs to change. One habit does: the report gets written and presented on every single call, whatever the visit was booked as. That single change is the whole difference between this day and a good one.
