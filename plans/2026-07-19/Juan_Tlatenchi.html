1

The one-line verdict

Why it didn't flip

The customer named the deadline. Juan wrote it on the form instead of using it.

What actually happened

The homeowner explained he was out of his house, in a hotel only through Thursday, and that his insurer had told him to find someone himself and send the estimate. Juan priced the leak repair and the refrigerant honestly, named the future work, and offered the specialist — Ellos pueden venir hoy (they can come today). The customer said he would think about it or call. Juan: déjame sacar tu firma y le voy a poner que quieres pensarlo por ahorita — let me get your signature and I'll note that you want to think about it for now.

What moves it

Thursday was the whole call. A man in a hotel with an insurance claim open is not deciding whether to buy — he is deciding who to call. The FSG move is to make the deadline the reason: "You're out Thursday and the insurer wants a number from someone. My specialist can be here today and you'd walk out of this with the number in writing tonight — that costs you nothing and it's the one thing your claim is missing." Then stop talking.

5

What Juan did right — real strength, build on it

  • He draws the system by hand (Draw the Picture — Strong). I'm gonna sketch out the system… I just like to draw it so that way it's easier to follow along. Makes my job a little easier. The customer's response — I appreciate it — is what buy-in sounds like before a price is ever mentioned.
  • A full call in the customer's language. Ninety minutes of diagnosis, teaching, and pricing in Spanish, including the refrigerant-transition problem. That is a service this customer could not have gotten from most techs, and it should be recognised as the asset it is.
  • The complete expectation set (Setting Expectations — Strong). All six beats, plus the time-constraint check, plus the permission-to-report question. He sets the call up correctly every time.
  • Honest about the limits of his own diagnosis (Check Your Tone — Strong). with the system being down, there's no telling what the pressures are doing. He refused to sell a certainty he didn't have — that is exactly the credibility the close needed.

The three gaps

each one in Juan's own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — A hotel deadline and an open insurance claim, and neither one made it into the close

Customer, unpromptedHe is out of the house, the hotel is paid only through Thursday, and the insurer told him to find someone himself and send in the estimate — the person they were sending couldn't come until Thursday either.
Juan, at the close"Si no está bien, déjame sacar tu firma y le voy a poner que quieres pensarlo por ahorita." (Fine — let me get your signature and I'll note that you want to think about it for now.)

The fix This is the whole call. The customer wasn't weighing whether to spend money — he was under a deadline with a claim open and no number in hand. Bring the deadline back and attach it to the specialist he had already offered: "You're out Thursday and your insurance needs a number from somebody. My guy can be here today and you'd have that number in writing tonight. It costs you nothing and it's the one piece your claim is missing — should I get him started this way?" Same offer, one sentence, aimed at the thing the customer actually said.

Moderate

Gap 2 — Two of the three critical questions are missing on both calls

What he asks every time"Where do you guys have the thermostat at? … Is that what you guys normally keep it at? … How are the power bills looking?" (thermostat ✓ power-bill follow-up ✓)
What is never askedHow often are you changing your air filters? · Which rooms tend to get hotter or colder than other rooms?

The fix He already does the hardest one — most techs skip the power-bill follow-up entirely and he asks it naturally. The two he's missing are the cheap ones, and on a leak call the hot/cold-room question is diagnostic gold: it tells him where the airflow problem is before he opens a panel. Add both to the same breath as the thermostat question.

Weak

Gap 3 — He promises a five-star experience and never asks whether he delivered one

Juan, in the welcome"And then overall, I'm here to deliver a five star experience. So if at any point I'm not meeting that expectation, you just let me know."
At the close, both callsEmail collected, report and photos promised, warm thanks exchanged — and no review ask on either visit.

The fix He opens the loop correctly and never closes it. On a call where the customer thanked him repeatedly and asked for the photos, the ask is the easiest sentence of the visit: "I said at the start I was going for five stars. If that's how it went, a review is the biggest thing you could do for me." Ask on the no-closes too — those are the reviews that come back as callbacks.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Use the deadline
Drill: Write the customer's stated constraint at the top of the report page before pricing anything — the move-out date, the claim, the guests coming, the newborn. Then say it back in the close. Manager role-plays three deadline scenarios.
Proof it worked: Every presentation on tape names the customer's own constraint out loud before the options.
Week 2
Question, don't document
Drill: Banned move for the week: taking a signature on a deferral without asking one question first. Empathy, then "can I ask what's holding you back?", then silence.
Proof it worked: No deferral signature on tape that isn't preceded by a genuine question.
Week 3
Two more questions, one more ask
Drill: Filters and hot/cold rooms added to the thermostat sequence; five-star ask on every completed visit including no-closes.
Proof it worked: All three critical questions on every tape; a review ask on every visit.
8

What we owe Juan Tlatenchi

  • Recognise the bilingual work as skilled work. He ran a full technical presentation in Spanish, including refrigerant regulation and pricing. That is a capability worth naming in the huddle and worth routing calls toward deliberately.
  • A specialist he can put in the driveway today. He said they can come today and the customer still walked. Give him a same-day slot he can actually name, and that sentence becomes a booking instead of a concept.
  • The deadline script, in both languages. One rehearsed sentence for "the customer just told me they're under time pressure" — written out, in Spanish and English, practiced with a manager until it is reflex.
  • Say the hand-drawn diagram out loud to the team. Sketching the system before pricing is a technique other techs on this board should be copying.
Bottom line

Juan does the parts of this job that are hardest to teach — he explains a system so clearly the customer follows along, in whichever language the customer needs — and then loses the call in a single sentence at the end. He is not missing a skill; he is missing a reflex. When a customer hands him a deadline, that deadline is the close. Practice one move: say it back to them before they say "let me think about it."
