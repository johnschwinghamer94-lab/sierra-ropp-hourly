1

The one-line verdict

Why the second one didn't flip

He earned the appointment, then offered it as a favour.

What actually happened

Nathan did everything right up to the ask: energy analysis, honest framing — I'm not like other companies that tell you, like, scare tactic — the busy-season reason, and a genuine offer to hold a slot: I can put you on the schedule and if you guys decide that you don't want to get the estimate, you can always move it to another date. The customer replied the coat and estimate things, let's differ for now and Nathan answered No problem. I just like to make sure I cover every base for you guys.

What it was

The customer had already said the quiet part out loud — sooner or later, I mean, when time comes, I need to do anyway, so the question is do I want to do estimate now or when there's something broken. He had answered his own objection. That is the moment to stop presenting and close on his own logic: you just said it yourself — now or broken. Now is free and takes twenty minutes. Nathan reassured instead.

What Nathan did right — real strength, build on it

  • A confirmed flip on a long, difficult visit. ServiceTitan shows a lead created off the Polster job the same day. Whatever happened in the ninety minutes the recorder missed, it ended with a real opportunity on the board.
  • The best empathy on this board, and it isn't technique. He stayed with a widow talking about her husband's illness instead of steering back to the thermostat, then offered to clean her filters and use her late husband's ladder. That is why customers ask for him by name.
  • He refuses fear selling out loud. I'm not like other companies that tell you, like, scare tactic. It's gonna fail tomorrow. Be afraid. Said to a customer facing a five-figure future repair — and it landed as credibility.
  • The band-aid analogy got the customer to conclude it himself. Nathan explained why a hard-start component only postpones the failure and the customer finished the sentence: It's a band aid. That is the hardest behaviour in the rubric, done right.
  • He asked for the review and got a commitment. The only rep on the day who closed that loop, and he explained why it matters rather than just requesting it.

The three gaps

each one in Nathan's own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — the customer answered his own objection and Nathan agreed with him

CustomerSooner or later, I mean, when time comes, I need to do anyway, so the question is do I want to do estimate now or when there's something broken.
CustomerThe coat and estimate things, let's differ for now.
NathanNo problem. I just like to make sure I cover every base for you guys.

The fix The customer built the case for the appointment out loud and then waited to be told to take it. "You just said it — now or when it's broken. Now is free, takes twenty minutes, and you get to pick the day. Broken means whoever can get here first. Let's do Thursday morning and you can move it if life happens." He had already earned the right to say that. He just needed to say it.

Moderate

Gap 2 — the big number arrived before the frame

NathanJust for the compressor alone when it fails, is going to be around $8,500 for that compressor.
CustomerJust make sure we have two condensers. So you're talking about 17,000 on compressors.
Customer, shortly afterI need to save a lot of money for that one. Like, you know. Wow.

The fix The number was correct and the delivery was honest, but it landed before any context, so the customer spent the rest of the call in sticker shock rather than in a decision. Frame first, price second: "Before I give you the number, here's the thing that matters — a part this expensive on a system this age is usually the moment people compare repair against replacement. Now the number." Same figure, completely different room.

Moderate

Gap 3 — the welcome stops halfway on short calls

Nathan, one callI'll do a couple checks for you. I'll also check the entire system to make sure there's no other underlying issues for you.
Nathan, anotherSo what I'll do is I'll still do an evaluation just to make sure that there's nothing else going on for you.
What's missing both timesThe report, the options, the approval promise, the five-star seed, and the goal question.

The fix Every visit is a possible presentation, and the presentation is much harder to open if the customer was never told it was coming. The full sequence takes forty seconds: evaluation, anything else I find, report, options, nothing without your approval, we can start today, five-star service, and what are you hoping to get out of today? Say all of it, every time, even on a nine-minute thermostat call.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Close on their words
Drill: When a customer states the logic for buying, Nathan repeats it back and asks for the day in the same breath. Manager role-plays five customers who talk themselves into it and then hesitate.
Proof it worked: No tape where a customer's own buying logic is met with agreement instead of a proposed time.
Week 2
Frame before price
Drill: No number leaves his mouth without one sentence of context in front of it. Ten reps on delivering a five-figure repair figure.
Proof it worked: Every priced finding on tape preceded by a framing sentence.
Week 3
The full forty seconds
Drill: The complete expectation sequence on every call regardless of length, including the goal question. Nathan reads it off the card until it's automatic.
Proof it worked: All four beats present on every recording, including short ones.

What we owe Nathan Colquitt

  • Credit the flip, out loud. His confirmed lead came off the longest, least glamorous visit of his day. Nobody should have to argue for that to count.
  • A closing line that fits how he already talks. Nathan won't use anything that feels like pressure — it would break the thing that makes him good. He needs a soft, specific booking sentence that sounds like him.
  • Better recording coverage. Two of his four visits are unusable past the welcome and his longest visit captured two minutes. We are coaching him on a fraction of his actual work.
  • Recognition for the review ask. He was the only tech on the board who closed that loop and he did it in a way worth copying.
Bottom line

Nathan's problem is not the pitch — it's the pause at the end of it. He builds more genuine trust than anyone on this board, refuses to sell with fear, gets customers to reach conclusions themselves, and one of his calls turned into a real lead. Then a family told him they'd need the estimate sooner or later, and he answered no problem. Everything before that sentence was excellent. The whole job now is the sentence.
