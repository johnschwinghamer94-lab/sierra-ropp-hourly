The one-line verdict

Why it didn't flip

He asked for the flip, then talked the customer out of it.

What actually happened

Nathan made the Option C offer cleanly — would you like me to provide you with the information of what it would cost to update the system? — and even offered to hold a slot: I can put you on the schedule and if you guys decide that you don't want to get the estimate, you can always move it to another date. Then the customer said let me think about it, and Nathan answered Yeah, and it's always good to think about it just so you can absorb everything.

What moves it

The customer had already answered his own objection out loud — sooner or later, I mean when time comes I need to do anyway, so the question is do I want to do estimate now or when there's something broken. That is not a stall, it is a man reasoning himself toward yes. The FSG move is empathy, then a question — "what would make now the better one?" — not agreement. Agreeing closed the door he had just opened.

What Nathan did right — real strength, build on it

  • Trust, at a level you cannot teach (Empathy — Strong). Parking checked before he touched anything, names traded immediately, and when a widow told him about her husband's service he met her with his own family's — no pivot back to the sale. The other household said it plainly: we're very happy… that was very impressive.
  • He refuses the fear sale, out loud (Check Your Tone — Strong). I'm not like other companies that tell you scare tactic. It's gonna fail tomorrow. Be afraid. That sentence is worth protecting — it is exactly why this customer stayed relaxed while hearing an $8,500 number.
  • The energy analysis (Draw the Picture — Strong). Turning a high-amp compressor into you guys are overpaying by 10% on utilities every month is the single best value translation on this board.
  • He made the Option C offer, unprompted and well. Most no-closes never get there. Nathan asked the question, explained the specialist's value, and offered to hold a date. Everything he needs is already in the call — he just let go of it too early.

The three gaps

each one in Nathan's own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — "Let me think about it" got agreement instead of a question

Customer"let me think about it… I mean, sooner or later, when time comes I need to do anyway, so the question is do I want to do estimate now or when there's something broken."
Nathan"Yeah, and it's always good to think about it just so you can absorb everything."
Customer, again"the quote and estimate things, let's differ for now." — Nathan: "No problem."

The fix The customer stated the decision as a two-option question and then waited. That is an invitation, not an objection. Empathy, then question, then help: "Totally fair — most people do think about it. Can I ask, is it the timing or is it that you don't want someone else walking the house? Because the estimate costs nothing and expires never — the only thing that changes is whether you have the number in July or in a heat wave." Then stop talking. He was one question away.

Moderate

Gap 2 — The expectations stop halfway through LTOAT5

What he says every time"I'll do a couple checks for you. I'll also check the entire system to make sure there's no other underlying issues for you." (Look ✓ Thorough Evaluation ✓)
What is never said"I'll bring you back everything I find and show you your options." · "I won't do anything without your approval." · "My goal is to take care of everything today." · "If I earn a five-star today…" (Options ✗ Approval ✗ Today ✗ 5-Star ✗)

The fix This is the automatic fail, and it costs him downstream twice: the customer has no reason to expect options at the end, and there is no five-star promise to close back to. The full six take about twenty extra seconds. Nathan's empathy already buys him the customer's full attention in those twenty seconds — he just isn't spending it.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Finish LTOAT5
Drill: All six expectation beats out loud on every call, every day. Manager spot-checks two tapes a week for options, approval, today, and the five-star seed specifically.
Proof it worked: Six-for-six on both randomly pulled tapes.
Week 2
Question, don't agree
Drill: Banned phrase for the week: "it's always good to think about it." Replacement reflex: empathy, then one curious question, then silence. Ten role-plays with a manager on "let me think about it" and "let's defer for now."
Proof it worked: Every deferral on tape is followed by a question, not a concession.
Week 3
Ask for the five
Drill: Close every completed visit by tying back to the promise from the welcome. No exceptions, including calls where nothing sold.
Proof it worked: A review ask on every completed visit; reviews coming in with his name on them.
8

What we owe Nathan Colquitt

  • Protect the way he treats people. Nathan's welcome is the standard the rest of the board should be measured against. Nothing in this plan should push him toward pressure — the fix is one question, not a harder sell.
  • A specific objection script, not general advice. He does not need "handle objections better." He needs the exact words for "let me think about it" on a no-immediate-findings maintenance call, rehearsed with a manager until it is automatic.
  • A same-day specialist he can actually promise. He offered to hold a date and the customer still deferred. Give him a real, bookable window so the offer has a slot attached instead of a concept.
  • Say the energy analysis out loud in the huddle. "Overpaying by 10% every month" is a reusable line. It belongs to the whole team.
Bottom line

Nathan is not losing calls on trust, technique, or honesty — he is losing them in the four seconds after the customer says "let me think about it." Everything before that moment is at or near the top of this board. Drill one substitution: where he currently agrees, ask a question instead. That, plus finishing the expectation sequence he already starts, is the whole plan.
