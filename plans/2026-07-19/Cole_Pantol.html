1

The one-line verdict

Why it flipped

He didn't sell the repair. He sold the next conversation.

What actually happened

Cole found a seized fan motor, a compressor megging bad, over-amping on start and on run, and pressures at 434 on one side against a data-plate max of 278 — on a system that runs R22 he could not source for four to six weeks. Instead of forcing a repair the customer plainly did not want, he built the third option out loud — I did give you that third option that we were talking about, which will get one of my comfort advisors out here — then phoned the advisor, spelled out the address, had the ticket created and waited at the house to hand the job over in person.

Why that is the model

The customer's opening position was I don't want to double CL a ton of money into a new unit if I can get out of this thing. That is normally where a call dies. Cole reframed the visit as information the customer needs either way — a repair number and a replacement number so he can make the best decision — and then converted the agreement into a dispatched advisor before he left the driveway. Booking beats explaining. He did the booking.

5

What Cole did right — real strength, build on it

  • He finished the handoff. Most Option C conversations end with a promise that someone will call. Cole phoned the advisor from the customer's driveway, spelled the address out loud, had the ticket created and stayed on site to walk him through the house. That is the difference between an intention and a lead.
  • He sold the number, not the equipment. Facing a homeowner who had already decided not to spend, he made the replacement quote useful to the plan the customer already had — that way you have a repair number, you have a replacement number and you can make the best.
  • Complete, unhurried expectations. Safety, cleanliness, proper operation, report, options, approval, five-star, time constraints and the goal question — all of it, before he picked up a tool.
  • Honest on a hard number. It's R22. It's over 10 grand — stated once, plainly, with no hedging and no apology. Customers trust that.
  • Real technical depth. Winding test, locked-rotor and run-load amps against the data plate, high-side pressure against spec, and a clear explanation of why an A2L compressor cannot go into an R22 system.
6

The three gaps

each one in Cole's own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — an energy analysis with no number behind it

ColeI also took a few minutes to do energy analysis. Just based on the aged condition of that system going to be running up that power bill by about 15%.
What was never askedWhat the power bill actually is. No summer spike, no winter comparison, no thermostat setting, no filter history — none of the required discovery questions were on tape.

The fix Fifteen percent of an unknown number is not an argument. One question, asked in the first ten minutes, turns it into one — "What's your power bill running in the summer, and what was it in the winter?" Then the analysis lands as that's roughly ninety dollars a month you're paying this compressor for nothing, in the customer's own figures. Cole flipped this call anyway; with the bill in hand the advisor arrives with the value already built.

Moderate

Gap 2 — the customer's stated goal never got answered

Cole, in the welcomeWhat's your ultimate goal for today?
CustomerTo be able to sleep in my bed.
LaterAm I going to have cold air tonight or do I need to figure out something? — Cole: To be honest with you, probably not.

The fix He asked the best question in the FSG welcome and then let the answer drop. When the honest answer is no, the goal still deserves a plan: "You won't be cold upstairs tonight — so let's solve tonight separately. Here's what I'd do about sleeping arrangements, and here's how fast the advisor can have a real fix moving." Answering the stated goal, even with a workaround, is what makes the visit feel finished.

Moderate

Gap 3 — the option set took four questions to become clear

CustomerSo, I mean, something that can be resolved today or no?
Customer, againI guess I'm just confused. So the repair — you can't do the repairs?
Customer, againJust like the three parts that you identified. If I replace those three parts with this new…

The fix The R22 constraint was the single most important fact in the call and it emerged in pieces, under questioning. Lead with the constraint, then the options: "Before I show you prices — this system runs a refrigerant we can't get for four to six weeks. So nothing fixes this tonight. Given that, here are your three paths." Same information, one pass, no confusion.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
The three questions
Drill: Power bill, thermostat setting, service history — on every call, in the first ten minutes, before any tool comes out. Cole writes the customer's summer and winter bill on the report before he goes to the van.
Proof it worked: Every recording this week contains the power-bill question and the customer's own number.
Week 2
Constraint first
Drill: Any time a part, a refrigerant or a lead time makes today impossible, that fact is said before the options, not discovered during them. Manager role-plays three long-lead-time scenarios.
Proof it worked: No tape where a customer asks twice whether the work can happen today.
Week 3
Finish the visit
Drill: Answer the stated goal out loud at the end of every call — even when the answer is no — and ask for the five-star review before leaving. Cole's Option C handoff is already the standard; this closes the last two beats around it.
Proof it worked: Goal referenced in the wrap-up and a review asked for on every completed visit.
8

What we owe Cole Pantol

  • Show this handoff to the rest of the board. Calling the advisor from the driveway, creating the ticket and waiting on site is exactly the behaviour the flip metric is meant to reward. It should be the training clip, not a footnote.
  • A discovery card taped to the truck dash. Three questions, that's it. This is a habit gap, not a skill gap, and a physical prompt fixes habit gaps faster than coaching does.
  • Confirm the lead ticket typed out. The advisor was dispatched on tape; the lead had not appeared in ServiceTitan when this plan was written. Someone should verify it landed so Cole's number reflects the work he actually did.
  • A stopgap playbook for no-cool-tonight. When the honest answer is that nothing gets fixed today, techs need something to offer besides sympathy — portable units, priority scheduling, whatever we can actually do.
Bottom line

Cole ran the hardest kind of call — an obsolete system, a customer already heading for the exit, a repair number over ten grand — and still turned it into a dispatched Comfort Advisor before he left. That is the win, and it is not an accident: he sold the information rather than the machine. The gap is one step earlier. He is doing expert work on top of thin discovery, and three questions in the first ten minutes would put real customer numbers underneath an already-strong close.
