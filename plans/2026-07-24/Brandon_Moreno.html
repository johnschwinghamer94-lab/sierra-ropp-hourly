1

The one-line verdict

Why both flipped

He named the home-warranty trap before the customer walked into it.

What actually happened

In Spanish, to a customer who thought his home warranty covered the equipment: eso son como Santa Claus — followed by the practical consequence, that waiting on the warranty company means calling back again later and being in the same position. Then the offer: give me fifteen or twenty minutes, I will write everything up, get you the estimate, and call my manager to see what I can do faster and cheaper.

Why that works

The customer's stated goal was modest — something to make the house bearable now. Brandon answered the goal, named the warranty delay honestly, and put an estimate in motion in the same breath. Full technical depth in the customer's own language is not a nicety; it is why the argument landed.

What Brandon did right — real strength, build on it

  • Two for two, and one of them entirely in Spanish with no loss of technical depth — refrigerant regulation, licensing risk, warranty limits, all of it.
  • He asked what the customer was actually looking for and then answered that, rather than the equipment.
  • He handled the home-warranty assumption head-on. Three reps lost warm prospects to warranty companies this week; he was the one who named the delay problem and kept the customer.
  • He paced a visit around a customer's flight without cutting the evaluation short.
  • He promised to check with a manager for a faster, cheaper route. That is advocacy, and the customer heard it as such.

Three-week training plan

Proof it worked: Customer's own figure on every recording.
Week 2
Review back on the card
Drill: Review ask restored alongside the time-constraint question he already asks reliably.
Proof it worked: Review asked on every call.
Week 3
Deadline the promise
Drill: Any promise to check with a manager comes with a time by which the customer will hear back.
Proof it worked: A stated time on every escalation.
