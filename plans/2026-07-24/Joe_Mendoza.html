1

The one-line verdict

The day in one line

He handles refusals well and pricing pushback badly.

What actually happened

On the refused evaluation he protected everyone by writing it down — you did refuse the full evaluation today — priced the honest full picture anyway, then sold the single part the customer would accept while warning him plainly: I can put the starter on there and it could — if it works, great. If it doesn't, I can't refund that money back. On the float switch, the customer found the part online and Joe's answer was I don't make the prices.

What the second one needed

I don't make the prices concedes the argument. The answer is what the price actually buys: a diagnosis, a warranted repair, a licensed technician and a company that comes back. A customer comparing a part number to an installed repair needs that comparison made out loud, once, calmly.

What Joe did right — real strength, build on it

  • He documented a refused evaluation properly. You did refuse the full evaluation today on the invoice protects the customer and the company.
  • He warned before he sold. Telling a customer the starter might not revive the compressor, and that there is no refund, before taking the money.
  • He applied the military discount without being asked.
  • He photographed the heat-stress marks rather than asking the customer to take his word for it.
  • He stayed calm through a markup argument that most people would have taken personally.

Gap 1 — "I don't make the prices"

CustomerOr just a float? They have them online at supply places for like $30.
CustomerNobody told me I have a 6,000% markup on a float switch.
JoeI don't make the prices. I just kind of hold that up to you how they are.

The fix That answer agrees with the customer that the price is indefensible. It isn't — it is a diagnosis, a licensed installer, a warranted repair and a company that returns. "The part is thirty dollars. What you're buying is someone who found the fault, will stand behind the fix, and comes back if it fails. That's the difference — and it's yours either way, if you'd rather fit it yourself." Said once, calmly, then move on.

What we owe Joe Mendoza

  • A written answer to the markup question. Every technician meets it and Joe currently has nothing to say. That is a company failure, not his.
  • Credit the documented refusal. Recording that a customer declined the full evaluation protects everyone and almost nobody does it.
  • The review ask, fourth graded day.
  • Recording coverage. Three of six visits end before the findings.
Bottom line

Joe handled a refused evaluation exactly right — documented it, priced the honest picture anyway, sold the part the customer would accept and warned him it might not work. Then a customer found a float switch online for thirty dollars and the answer was I don't make the prices, which hands him the argument. Nobody has given Joe a way to explain what the price actually buys, and that is worth fixing for the whole team, not just for him.
