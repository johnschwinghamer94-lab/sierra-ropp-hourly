The one-line verdict

What he built

Duct loss proved with a temperature gun in front of the customer — the attic in this area was sitting at 90, and right when the temp gun hits it, it's instantly cooling because it's releasing your cold air… the good vents should be pushing out 46 to 52 degree air, against 70s coming out of the vents on the failing half. What is missing

Not one required question. No thermostat setting, no power bill, no filter frequency asked (he inferred it), and no rooms question on a call that was entirely about a room that will not cool. And the tape ends inside a supervisor conversation rather than with a customer's answer, so the outcome of ninety minutes of genuinely excellent work is unknown.

Draw the Picture
Strong
The best teaching on this board. The whole system in five boxes: thermostat sends a signal to that circuit board. It tells the cold box or the hot box. Your hot box is your furnace. Your cold box is just a metal coil that carries the refrigerant… when you turn your AC on, it absorbs the hot air from your home, pushes it across the ice cold coil, and that's how you generate cold air. Then the heat exchanger, built from first principles: the gas valve ignites and starts burners — they look like jet burners, they shoot into these tubes, because with flame comes carbon monoxide. The metal gets hot and the air blows across it just like this metal coil, only it's a hot piece of metal instead of a cold one.

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • He proved the duct loss instead of describing it. Temperature gun on the attic run, in the customer's sight, with the target range named: the good vents should be pushing out 46 to 52 degree air.
  • He taught a heat exchanger from scratch — burners, tubes, why flame produces carbon monoxide, what a breach releases — and checked understanding before every step.
  • He checked for carbon monoxide detectors before drawing conclusions and then made the honest point that detectors are a warning, not a protection.
  • He argued against his own labour. If I repair it here, it's going to fall apart here — a duct patch he could have sold and did not.
  • He corrected the filter interval with a reason, not a rule: about a month is enough to get built up enough to restrict the airflow.
  • He kept the four-year-old condenser out of the replacement scope rather than letting the specialist quote the whole system.

6

This week's drill — four questions before the ladder

Say it in this order, every call, before any diagnosis

"Before I get started — how often are you changing the filters? Which rooms run hotter or colder than the rest? What do you keep the thermostat at in the summer? And what does a standard power bill look like for you setting it there?"

Under a minute, and it front-loads the entire decision conversation with the customer's own numbers instead of his. The teaching is already the best on the board; this is the part that makes it convert.
