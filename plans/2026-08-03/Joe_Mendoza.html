1

The one-line verdict

What the day turned on

He explains the pain instead of asking about it.

What actually happened

The one that flipped went the other way. On the Folk call he made the future concrete — There are other components on the way out, like a rusted coil, fan motor, leaking oil… I would hate for her to be in a situation where we fix it now. And before summer ends, she has to call me back out because something else happened — and the son said it back to him: fixing something now and they keep fixing stuff will probably cost more than what a whole new unit is. Specialist there in thirty minutes.

What flips it

On the other five, the same information arrived as a lecture. Power bills doubling to $400 and heading for $700, a $3,000 repair that from a common sense perspective felt off, a nineteen-year-old system with several cancelled specialist appointments — all answered with facts. The Folk move is the model: make the future concrete, then stop talking and let the customer say the conclusion.

What Joe did right — real strength, build on it

  • He makes the future concrete, and that is what flipped his one call. There are other components on the way out, like a rusted coil, fan motor, leaking oil… before summer ends, she has to call me back out because something else happened. The son finished the thought himself and the specialist was there in thirty minutes.
  • He does not discount under pressure. Told $854 was three times what was expected, he answered with market position, not a lower number: Call any reputable company in town. We’re right in the margin of exactly what it costs. He lost that call without losing credibility.
  • He asks the question nobody asks. I also saw that you’ve had several appointments with equipment specialists as well, and they were cancelled. Any particular reason why? That is a genuinely brave question and the answer was the key to the whole call.
  • He brings the absent decision-maker into the room. On the Folk call he got the son on the phone mid-visit and re-explained the problem to him directly, rather than leaving the customer to relay it.
  • Six calls, 204 minutes, and the diagnostic quality never dropped. The gauge work on his fifth call was as careful as on his first.

Customer"$3,000 does seem, from a common sense perspective, a little off." — plus a $500 floor unit and a friend quoting $1,000.
JoeAnswered with BTU requirements and equipment costs.
ResultThe conversation turned adversarial and the customer left with three referrals and no commitment.

The fix When a customer says a number feels wrong, they are telling you about a feeling, not asking for a lesson. Ask before you answer: What’s your biggest concern here — is it the amount, or whether the repair is even worth it on a system this age? Those are two completely different objections with two different answers, and guessing wrong turns a consultation into an argument.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Pain gets a question, not a fact. Any sentence about bills, cost, or how long something has lasted is answered with a question and, where relevant, a no-cost specialist date — never with an explanation alone.
Proof it worked: Manager hears a question following every customer-stated pain point this week; two comfort-advisor dates booked off financial pain.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Ask before you answer on price. Every price objection gets ‘what’s your biggest concern here?’ before any figure, comparison or equipment-cost explanation.
Proof it worked: No call this week contains a cost justification that was not preceded by a question.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: No visit ends on signatures alone. Every completed call gets a findings recap, a named next step, and the five-star ask.
Proof it worked: Every completed visit this week has a recap in the notes and a review requested before leaving.

What we owe Joe Mendoza

  • Six calls in a day and a scoring pass that credited him with a flip he did not get. His real number is honest work and he deserves to be coached against the truth, not a generous count.
  • Specialist availability that matches what he promises — his one flip happened because somebody could genuinely be there in thirty minutes.
  • Credit for the two hardest things in this trade: he holds his price when it is challenged, and he tells customers inconvenient truths about warranties and mismatched equipment. Neither is coachable and he already has both.
Bottom line

Joe is the strongest diagnostician and the strongest teacher on this board, and he will not discount when he is pushed — that combination is rare and it is not what is costing him. What is costing him is a reflex: when a customer names their pain, he explains it. On the one call where he made the future concrete and then stopped talking, the customer’s own son closed it for him and a specialist was on site in half an hour. That is the same technician, one habit different. Ask the question, then be quiet, and this becomes the best day on the team.
