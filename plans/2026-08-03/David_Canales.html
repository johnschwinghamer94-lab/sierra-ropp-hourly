The one-line verdict

What the call turned on

The right question, asked immediately — and the answer left on the floor.

What actually happened

Ice on the coil, and within a minute: First, I just gotta ask, when’s the last time you replaced your filters? The answer was Never. David’s reply was Okay, so let me start with that, and the conversation moved to thaw logistics.

What flips it

‘Never’ is not a data point, it is the explanation the customer is standing in front of. That’s almost certainly what we’re looking at here — a starved coil freezes, and it’s been starving for years. Once it thaws I’ll confirm it, and then let’s talk about how we keep it from happening again. That sentence turns a repair visit into a maintenance conversation before the price is ever mentioned.

5

What David did right — real strength, build on it

  • He asked the right question first. Ice on the coil, and inside a minute: when’s the last time you replaced your filters? That is the correct opening move and most techs get there ten minutes later.
  • He refused to guess. I can’t do nothing if it’s iced up… a full evaluation, it’s going to have to thaw out completely. Diagnosing through ice would have been faster and wrong.
  • He gave her the whole differential in plain words. It can be air flow… a leak in the refrigerant… fan motor up in the attics not working. She asked a follow-up, which means it landed.
  • He managed the wait. Every step narrated while the coil thawed, so the slowest part of the visit never felt like nothing was happening.
  • Permission before entry, every time. Is it okay if I come in? and Is it okay if I just let myself in and out? Small, and it is why the visit stayed easy.

The gaps

each one in David’s own calls
Weak

“Never” was the whole conversation, and it lasted four seconds

David"First, I just gotta ask, when's the last time you replaced your filters?"
Customer"Never. I've lived in the house backwards... and I have no idea when they replaced them."
David"Okay, so let me start with that." — then straight to thaw logistics.

The fix She told him the system has never been maintained, in front of a coil that is frozen solid because it is starving. Connect the two out loud while the ice is still on it: That’s very likely what we’re looking at — a filter that restricted for years starves the coil and the coil freezes. Once it thaws I’ll confirm it. And whatever the repair turns out to be, let’s also talk about keeping this from happening again. That is the maintenance-plan conversation opening itself, before any repair number exists to argue about.

Moderate

She asked how it works and got a list instead of a question

Customer"Cool. What causes that?"
David"So it can be air flow. Flow issues. It could be a leak in the refrigerant. It could be fan motor up in the attics not working."
What was missing"...and based on what you just told me about the filters, which one of those would you bet on?"

The fix The list is accurate and it is his to give. But when a customer asks an open question, the cheapest way to make the answer theirs is to hand it back: Given what you just told me about the filters — which of those three sounds most likely to you? When she says ‘the airflow’, the diagnosis is hers and every recommendation afterwards is easier.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Connect the maintenance answer to the failure in front of you, out loud, within thirty seconds of hearing it — and use it to open the prevention conversation before any repair price exists.
Proof it worked: Manager hears the connection made on every call this week where service history is poor or absent.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Hand open questions back. When a customer asks what causes something, give the differential and then ask which one they would bet on given what they have just told you.
Proof it worked: At least one customer-stated diagnosis on tape per call this week.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Say the decision before you leave the room. State what you are checking and what each outcome means for their choices, so the options exist on the recording even if the ending does not.
Proof it worked: The sentence is audible on every call; every completed visit ends with two named paths.

What we owe David Canales

  • Recording equipment that lasts the visit. A 75-minute call with a few audible minutes gives him almost no credit for a day’s work and gives us nothing to coach from.
  • More than one call on tape. Judging a technician on a single truncated recording is not a fair read of anyone, and this plan should be treated as a partial picture.
  • Credit for the diagnostic discipline. He would not diagnose through ice even though it would have been quicker, and that is exactly the standard we ask for.
Bottom line

On the audible portion, David does the two hardest things right: he asks the highest-yield question first and he refuses to diagnose through ice when guessing would have been faster. What he leaves behind is the easiest value in the trade — a customer who has never changed a filter, standing next to a frozen coil, being told why the two are connected. Say that sentence and the maintenance conversation opens itself. The rest of the read on him is limited by the recording, not by him.
