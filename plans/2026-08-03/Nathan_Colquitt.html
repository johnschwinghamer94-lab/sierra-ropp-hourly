  • He protects the standard. I want to make sure that I do an actual service. I wouldn’t be able to get it done necessarily. Refusing to do it badly is the right instinct — it just needs a second half.
  • He takes the awkwardness off the customer. She apologised; he answered No, no, it’s no problem at all… Thank you for letting me know, by the way. That visit ended badly for us and well for her.
  • The teaching is real when he gets to it. Thermal overload explained exactly as the customer had experienced it, and a Pulte roofline insulation observation that got Well, that’s good news back.

What we owe Nathan Colquitt

  • Recording equipment that works. A 118-minute visit with a few audible minutes means this plan judges him mostly on a seven-minute doorstep conversation, which is not a fair read of a technician’s day.
  • A booking process that does not set him up to fail. The office promised forty-five minutes for a three-system service. He absorbed that in the customer’s doorway, alone, and it cost the visit.
  • A standing rule that he may service one system and rebook the rest. Right now he is choosing between doing it badly and doing nothing, and neither is a good option.
Bottom line

The read on Nathan here is thin and that is mostly our fault — one of his two recordings barely exists. What is on tape shows a technician with the right instincts and no second move: he was honest about the time, he protected the quality of the work, and then he left with nothing done because nobody had told him he could do part of it. The fix is one question — which system is actually the problem — and one habit: book the return visit in the room, not through the office. Both are small, and the dispatch promise that created this situation is ours to fix, not his.
