The one-line verdict

What the day turned on

He earns the decision and then never asks for it.

What actually happened

On the Melchor call he did everything right: discovery down to the customer’s life plans (forever home), photographs of the coil freezing, the refrigerant phase-out explained, both paths priced in principle — a patch with a six-to-eight week parts wait, or replacement at around $150 a month. Then the call ended on a promise to ring back with numbers.

What flips it

One sentence was missing at the end of eighty-one minutes: So let me get you the exact numbers on both paths — and while I do, let’s put our specialist on the calendar so you’ve got the replacement figure in front of you at the same time. Is later this week better, or the weekend? He had earned that ask three times over.

5

What Mario did right — real strength, build on it

  • The teaching is genuinely elite. Compressor as the heart, blower motor as the lungs, capacitor as the battery — plus photographs of the coil freeze, the oil leaks and the heat-exchanger stress. Customers asked follow-up questions, which is the proof it landed.
  • He asks the forever-home question. On Melchor he established that this is the house they intend to keep. That single answer changes every recommendation that follows — and hardly anyone else on this board asks it.
  • He tells customers the inconvenient truth. A six-to-eight week wait for parts on the repair path was stated plainly, when hiding it would have been easier and would have made the repair look better.
  • He asks permission for the information itself. Is that information you’re going to want to know? — a small, unusual courtesy that gets the customer to opt into the findings conversation.
  • Three long visits and the thoroughness never dropped. The 108-minute maintenance call was as careful at the end as at the start.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Every visit ends with a date. No call finishes on ‘I’ll call you with numbers’ — the specialist visit or the follow-up gets a day and a time before he leaves the driveway.
Proof it worked: Manager hears a booked date or a declined offer at the end of every recording this week.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Utility bills are a buying signal. Any customer-stated power bill gets a question and a no-cost comfort-advisor offer within thirty seconds.
Proof it worked: Every call this week where a bill is mentioned has the follow-up question audible on tape.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Order of operations: greet, expectations, four questions, then work. Personal conversation happens during the work, not before it. Add the five-star ask to the closing.
Proof it worked: Expectations audible in the first five minutes of every recording; review requested on every completed visit.

Bottom line

Mario spent nearly four hours in customers’ homes and taught better than almost anyone on this day. Three households ended their visit understanding exactly what was wrong, with photographs to prove it. What none of them got was a question at the end. He builds the whole case and then leaves without asking for the verdict — and on the maintenance call, the customer handed him the opening with their own power bill. He does not need more skill; he needs one sentence at the end of every visit that has a day and a time in it.
