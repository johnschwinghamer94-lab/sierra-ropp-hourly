The one-line verdict

What the day turned on

The policy was the ending. It should have been the opening.

What actually happened

We can’t use these parts for your repair just because that’s not how our policies work. Accurate, and delivered as a full stop. The customer drew the obvious conclusion — Okay, then we don’t — we can’t do any business then, right? — and Francisco agreed with him.

What flips it

Everything the customer said before that was a buying signal. His cousin had diagnosed it and doesn’t do any work. He had already spent money on Honeywell dampers. He wanted somebody competent to fit them. The move is to keep the visit alive: I can’t warranty parts I didn’t supply — but let me look at what you bought and price the same job with our parts, so you can see whether it’s even a big difference. Costs you nothing today.

5

What Francisco did right — real strength, build on it

  • He is easy to have in the house. The garden and duck conversation on the Holden call built genuine warmth in under two minutes, and the customer relaxed into the visit.
  • He explains the process before the inspection, not after. inspect inside/outside, check electrical/safety/performance, give you a report, then you let me know what makes sense — that sentence removes the fear of being sold something unknown.
  • He introduces his partner as part of the team. this is my partner Jose, we’re here to help you out today — a small thing that stops a second person in the house feeling like a stranger.
  • He held the line on the parts policy. The answer he gave was the right answer. The failure was what came after it, not the answer itself.
  • He keeps his composure. A bee on the doorstep and a customer refusing the visit both got the same even, unbothered tone.

The fix Every fact in that exchange says this customer wants to spend money with somebody competent. A constraint should always arrive attached to an alternative.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Never end on a policy. Every ‘we can’t do that’ is followed immediately by ‘here’s what we can do’ — a priced alternative, an inspection, or a specialist visit at no cost.
Proof it worked: Manager hears an alternative attached to every constraint stated on tape this week; no visit ends within ten minutes without one.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Four questions before any conclusion, including when the customer has already diagnosed it. System behaviour, how long, what else is wrong, and what a good outcome looks like today.
Proof it worked: Every recording this week has all four audible before any recommendation or refusal.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Promise the findings out loud before starting work, and deliver them before leaving — plus the five-star ask on every completed visit.
Proof it worked: Every call this week has a findings conversation on tape; review requested on all completed visits.
8

What we owe Francisco Valencia

  • A clear, written answer for the customer-supplied-parts conversation. He was right on the policy and had nothing to offer alongside it — that is a script we have not given him, and it costs us jobs.
  • Recording coverage. Fifteen minutes on tape across a day gives him almost no chance to show what he does well.
  • Backing on the boundary itself. He did not bend on a policy that protects the customer as much as us, and that should be said to him plainly before any of the coaching above.
Bottom line

Francisco was right about the parts and wrong about what to do next. That is a fixable, one-sentence problem — a constraint should never leave his mouth without an alternative attached to it. The deeper habit underneath it is discovery: on the call that ended in six minutes, he had no second card to play because he had not asked a single question about the system. Four questions before any conclusion, and one ‘here’s what we can do’ after every ‘here’s what we can’t’, and this day looks completely different. His warmth is real and it is worth building on.
