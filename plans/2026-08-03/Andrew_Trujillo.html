The one-line verdict

What the call turned on

They named the fear. He soothed it instead of using it.

What actually happened

Andrew asked the right question — Do you guys have an ultimate goal, expectations for me in terms of my visit here today? — and the household answered honestly: Our only concern is we don’t really we’re hoping we don’t have to replace anything like in terms of a unit. His reply was Safe to say just reliability peace of mind. Absolutely. Yeah. Thousand percent. Like I said we’ll cover things as they come up.

What flips it

That answer is the whole shape of the sale. The FSG move is to take it and turn it into a question they answer: Reliability and peace of mind — got it. If the motor is seized, do you want me to price the repair first, or would you rather see both side by side before you decide? Their fear becomes the frame for the options instead of a reason not to build them.

What Andrew did right — real strength, build on it

  • He kills the billing anxiety before it starts. Because last thing I want to do is give you guys a surprise bill at the end of my work. Most techs on this board never say the money part out loud in the welcome. He does.
  • Genuine, unforced rapport. The dog apology, the pet logistics, the interest in the collection — the household was relaxed and cooperative for the whole visit.
  • Complete discovery. Timeline, trip length, sitter feedback, maintenance history, filter cadence and thermostat setting all asked. Nothing on the required-questions list was skipped.
  • He asked the goal question. Do you guys have an ultimate goal, expectations for me in terms of my visit here today? is the single highest-value question in the FSG model, and he asked it unprompted.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Answer the goal question with a question. Whenever a customer names what they hope to avoid, respond within ten seconds with a choice — repair priced alone, or repair priced beside a replacement.
Proof it worked: Manager hears the follow-up question on every call this week where a cost or replacement worry is voiced.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Rapport goes at the edges. Personal conversation before the inspection starts or after the report is written — never in the middle of the diagnostic — and every time it does happen, land it back on the job in one sentence.
Proof it worked: No call this week has more than five continuous minutes of non-service conversation inside the assessment.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Narrate the pivot. Before leaving the room to inspect or write, say what happens next and what the customer will be asked to decide.
Proof it worked: The sentence is audible on every recording, and every completed call ends with at least two named options.

What we owe Andrew Trujillo

  • A working recorder. One call on tape for a full shift, and that one cuts off at the attic — we cannot coach what we cannot hear, and the outcome of his day is unknown to us for reasons that are not his.
  • Credit where it is due: the expectations he sets are better than most of the board’s, including the billing sentence almost nobody else says.
  • A scripted answer for the ‘we don’t want to replace it’ moment. It comes up on nearly every call in this market and he should not have to invent it in the room.
Bottom line

Andrew opens a call better than most of this board — scope, safety, approval and the bill, all named before he starts, with warmth that is obviously genuine. The whole gap is one beat: he asked the goal question, got a real answer, and then agreed with it instead of building on it. Turn that answer into a choice and he has the front half and the back half of the call. Right now he only has the front half on tape — and getting more of his day recorded is on us, not him.
