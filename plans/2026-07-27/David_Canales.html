1

The one-line verdict

Why it didn't flip

He deflected the lead instead of booking it.

What actually happened

On the maintenance call the customer raised a mini-split that was not cooling well. David correctly declined to quote outside his scope — yo nomás soy de reparaciones — and asked the customer to call sales.

What it means

Declining to quote is right; handing the customer a phone number is not. The tech who hears the opportunity is the one who books it. "Let me get our specialist out to look at that with you" keeps the customer in the room and creates the lead.

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • Deep, genuine rapport. He engaged as a person, not a technician reading a rapport step — and the customer gave him complete access and complete trust in return.
  • The best analogy set on the board after Ben's. Car battery, engine oil, filter restriction — every one of them landed.
  • He stayed in his lane under pressure. Declining to quote work outside SILO scope is the correct process, and he did it without hedging or apologising.
  • Thorough beyond what was asked. On the evaluation he inspected both units end to end — coil rust, heat exchanger spots, water staining — and showed the customer each one.

6

This week’s drill

Out of scope is not out of reach

Trigger: the customer mentions any system or problem outside your lane.

Response, instead of a phone number:

"That's outside what I handle — but I'm not going to make you chase it. Let me get our specialist out to look at that with you. Does later this week work, or is next week better?"

Same boundary, opposite outcome. You keep the scope; the customer keeps the momentum.

8

What we owe David

  • Show him what the handoff sentence sounds like. He is not resisting it — he has been taught the boundary and never taught the bridge.
  • Use his analogies in training. Car battery, engine oil, filter restriction — that set should be standard issue.
  • Help him with pacing, not with rapport. Do not coach the warmth out of him. Coach the clock.
Bottom line

David gets further into a customer's trust than almost anyone here, and he explains failing equipment with analogies that actually land. He also knows exactly where his scope ends — which is right. What he has never been given is the bridge: the sentence that keeps a customer he cannot quote from becoming a customer who has to chase someone. "Let me get our specialist out to look at that with you" is the whole thing. It costs him nothing and it is the difference between a good visit and a lead.
