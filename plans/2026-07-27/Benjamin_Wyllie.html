1

The one-line verdict

Why it flipped

He answers "what are the options" with a time, not a speech.

What actually happened

On MCCRONE the customer asked What are the options to get in here? Ben: I can get someone out here for that. It's a different department, but I can get a guy out here for that to talk about the pricing. Customer: And what's the approximate timeline of that? Ben: I can get them out here today. Customer: Oh, perfect.

What it means

Four exchanges, no persuasion, appointment booked. That is the whole Option C play and he ran it clean twice in one day. The rest of the coaching is about the tail of the call, not the close.

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • He turned a hostile prior quote into trust. RAGHEB had already been told thirty thousand dollars by another company. Ben stayed in his lane — I can give repair pricing — priced the repairs honestly, and let the arithmetic make the case.
  • Two Option C handoffs, both booked same-day. No speech, no convincing. Question, answer, time, done.
  • He took care of a tenant who could not buy anything. WOLF: the tenant was diabetic, in a 90-degree house, crying that morning. I want you taken care of. I want you to become comfortable in this home. Nothing in it for him. He did it anyway.
  • Analogies built for the specific person in front of him. Blood pressure for the med student, a clogged artery for the surgical patient. That is not a script — that is listening.

The three gaps

Weak

Gap 1 — Five calls, zero five-star asks

PatternNot one review request across five customers, including two who left delighted and booked.
CostTwo flips and no reviews out of a day where the rapport was arguably the best on the board.

The fix Attach it to the handoff, not the goodbye: "I've got Derek coming out this afternoon. While I'm still here — if today was a five-star visit, would you mind saying so in the review? It's the only thing I get graded on."

Moderate

Gap 2 — The rapport runs long past its usefulness

RAGHEBA multi-minute conversation about cologne houses, discounters and sample bottles in the middle of an attic inspection.
CostIt was a hundred-degree evening with customers waiting. The rapport was already won by then.

The fix Rapport buys the sale in the first ten minutes; after that it costs the next customer. Set a mental checkpoint at the attic — once tools are out, conversation follows the work.

Solid

Gap 3 — WOLF ended without the homeowner ever hearing the options

SituationDecision-maker in Virginia, tenant on site, system confirmed dead.
What happenedHe diagnosed it thoroughly and the visit ended with the tenant, not the owner.

The fix When the buyer is remote, get them on the phone before you pack up — the same way you got the deed-holder's authorisation on other calls. A dead compressor explained live to the person with the chequebook is a different call than a report emailed to Virginia.

6

This week’s drill

Book the advisor, then ask for the review — in that order, same breath

Trigger: the customer has just agreed to the specialist visit.

Response, before you move:

"Perfect — he'll be out this afternoon. And while I've still got you: if today felt like a five-star visit, a quick review means everything on my end."

You already win the room. This is the only thing you are leaving behind.

7

3-week training plan

Week 1
Five-star on every call
Attach the ask to the handoff. Target five for five; report the count daily.
Week 2
Ten-minute rapport rule
Rapport before tools come out, work conversation after. Same warmth, half the runtime.
Week 3
Remote decision-makers
On any call where the buyer is not on site, get them on the phone before you leave. Practise the opening line with a manager.
8

What we owe Benjamin

  • Put the RAGHEB call in training. Explaining a compressor as blood pressure to a med student is the clearest Be A Nerd example this board has produced.
  • Tell him the WOLF visit was the right thing. He spent thirty-five minutes on a house that could never buy from him and treated the tenant with real dignity. That should be named out loud, not quietly absorbed.
  • Give him a review target, not a review lecture. He is not resisting the ask — he is forgetting it. A number on a whiteboard fixes that faster than a conversation.
Bottom line

Ben has the front end of this job solved. The expectations block is word-perfect, the analogies are tailored to the individual customer, and when someone asks what happens next he books a time instead of making a speech — twice in one day. Two things are left: he never asks for the review, and the rapport keeps running after it has done its work. Neither is a skill problem. Both are habits, and habits are the easiest thing on this list to fix.
