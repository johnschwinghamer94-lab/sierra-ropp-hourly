The one-line verdict

Why it didn't flip

The customer asked for options and got homework.

What actually happened

On the Wilson job the tenant relayed the owner's instruction almost word for word: whatever they decide, just like. Tell them to call me and I'll talk to, like, talk to you about, like, different options and stuff. David made the call, and reported back: said he's gonna look up thermostats and probably just have us install it. Then he collected signatures and left.

What the FSG model calls for

The owner asked to be walked through options. That is an invitation to build the option set, not to hand him a shopping task. Two or three tiers on the phone — like-for-like replacement, a smarter zoning-compatible stat, and what a matched pair looks like — would have closed it on the call. Nobody buys faster after being asked to do their own research.

What he did right — real strength, build on it

  • He asks for a name he missed last time. I'm sorry, I didn't catch your name last time — a small thing that tells a long-standing member she is a person rather than an address.
  • He owned a dispatch failure without hiding behind it. It shouldn't have happened in the first place... Dispatch is a whole different department. So either way, I'm here now. I have the filters. Explained, not excused.
  • He stepped into a departed technician's shoes gracefully. A member who had loved Tyrone for ten years got I hope I can give you the same service that he provided. You know, I'm here for you guys. That is exactly how you inherit a relationship.
  • The zone-board explanation is genuinely good teaching. He translated a confusing setup into something the tenants could follow, and he checked the whole system before accepting their thermostat theory.
  • He is easy to have in the house. Permission asked before every entry and re-entry, on every call, without exception.

6

This week’s drill — word for word

Options on the phone — for absent decision-makers

Trigger: you have the owner on the line and something needs deciding.

Present, don't delegate:

“I've got three ways to go here. Option one is the straight swap — cheapest and quickest. Option two fixes the underlying fault so it doesn't come back. Option three gets both floors matched and on one system. My recommendation is two, and here's why. Which one do you want me to write up?”

Then stop talking. The customer already asked to be walked through options — all that's left is doing it.

What we owe David

  • A one-page tiering template. He is not avoiding options, he is missing a structure for them. Three tiers and a recommendation, on a card in the van.
  • Continuity credit on inherited accounts. He picked up a relationship a departed technician had built over ten years and handled it with real grace. The office should make sure that member knows he is her tech now.
  • Backup when dispatch drops a ball. He absorbed a scheduling failure that wasn't his and still delivered the visit. He shouldn't have to.
Bottom line

David is genuinely easy to have in your home — he asks before he enters, he owns other people's mistakes, and he stepped into a ten-year relationship with a departed tech without a wobble. What is missing is the middle of the visit. The three critical questions are absent from all three calls, and without them there is nothing to build an option set on — which is exactly what happened when an owner asked, in plain English, to be walked through his options. Ninety seconds of questions, then three tiers. That is the whole fix.
