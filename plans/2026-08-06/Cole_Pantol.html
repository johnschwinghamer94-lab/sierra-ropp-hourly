The one-line verdict

Why the Kalai call didn’t flip

She talked herself into it and he offered to do it sometime.

What actually happened

After the numbers landed, the customer did his job for him: if it’s gonna be close to that, I might as well just get a new one. His reply: Yeah, so with that, I can always set that up for you. That way you can get that cost, that way you have a repair price, you have a replacement price. She then reached for the exit — I will call to schedule… I want to figure out my warranty first — and after one good push she closed it: We’ll wait, we’ll wait.

What flips it

I might as well just get a new one is not a thought to be supported, it is a yes waiting for a time. The advisor should be booked in the next sentence, before the warranty ever comes up: That’s exactly why the advisor visit exists, and it costs you nothing. He’s out this way Thursday — morning or afternoon? Whatever your warranty ends up covering, you’ll want that number in your hand when you call them. The warranty then becomes a reason to take the appointment rather than a reason to delay it.

The gaps

each one in Cole’s own calls
Weak

Gap 1 — The word “always” is costing him appointments

Customer, the buying signal"Yeah, because if it's gonna be close to that, I might as well just get a new one."
Cole"Yeah, so with that, I can always set that up for you." … "I can always get one of my equipment specialists out here." … "I can always set it up so you can get that information."

The fix I can always tells the customer this can happen at any time, which means it does not have to happen now — and nothing that does not happen now happens at all. Replace it with a day, every time: Let me get him out here Thursday. Morning or afternoon? Same offer, same zero cost, one difference: it has a date, so it exists.

Moderate

Gap 2 — He argued with the home warranty instead of getting curious about it

Customer"I will call to schedule, um, to see. Yeah, what are— I want to figure out my warranty first."
Cole, in one block"We don't work with a home warranty. Um, they also— they don't replace systems. They just kind of patch them up, kind of get them by. Like Band-Aids is what we usually say… you'll still be overpaying on utilities and things still can happen down the line."
Customer"Um, We'll wait, we'll wait."

The fix Every fact in that paragraph is true and it still lost the appointment, because it told her that her plan was wrong. The model is empathy, then a question, then help: Good — absolutely check the warranty first, that’s free money if it covers it. What did they tell you it covers? Then use her own answer: a warranty claim needs a documented diagnosis and a replacement figure, and the advisor visit hands her both. Her plan becomes the reason to book, not the reason to wait.

Weak

At the door"Above all, we're here to give you a 5-star experience. If I'm not doing that, let me know so I can change it."
At the end"Signature here just states I did the tune-up for you today. And then this one is I wasn't screaming at you, yelling at you, a bunch of holes in the walls, nothing like that."

Right after that last signature: And if today really was five stars, a Google review with my name in it is the biggest thing you could do for me. No pressure at all. He seeds this on every call and collects it on none, on a day where he changed a stranger’s filters for free and got thanked for it.

Week 2
