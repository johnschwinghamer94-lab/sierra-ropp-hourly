What the day turned on

When he knows the answer he is one of the best teachers here. When he doesn’t, he apologises and leaves.

Full expectations sequence including the review ask, an hour of genuine teaching, honest tiers — 2,730 immediate, over $8,600 future — and then the hand-off framed against his own interest: this is where I make my money, is by the repairs, because I’m the repair guy. 5

What David did right — real strength, build on it

  • He asks permission to teach, then teaches properly. has anyone ever explained to you how an AC system works? followed by the complete system, drawn out, in car analogies a 25-year homeowner could follow. Among the best education on this board.
  • He named his own incentive and gave the sale away anyway. this is where I make my money, is by the repairs… I will take your money if you would like, but at least we can give you that option. That is why the free visit was believed.
  • He converted a time constraint into a same-day appointment. Most techs hear “I have to leave by two” and book for next week. He asked whether the specialist could be there within the hour, and made it happen.
  • He raised a safety issue nobody asked about. The heat-exchanger hot spot and carbon monoxide risk, photographed in August, on a furnace nobody was thinking about.
  • He asks for the review. Very few people on this board say the words at all, and he builds them into his opening so they cannot be forgotten.

Same truth, and he keeps the room.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Never leave a stated want on the table. If a customer mentions a membership, a room, a noise or a plan, it gets sold, written up or booked before he leaves — regardless of what else went wrong on the call.
Proof it worked: Every customer request on tape has a matching outcome on the ticket.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Own it, then route it. Any time we cannot do something, the sentence structure is “that’s on us / here is what I will do / here is when I will get back to you” — never blame on dispatch, never apologising for his own competence.
Proof it worked: No self-deprecating statements on any recording; every dead end ends with a committed follow-up.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Hand over the conclusion. Replace do you have any questions on that? with a question the customer has to answer, and wait for it.
Proof it worked: A manager can point to the customer saying the problem back in their own words on every call.

What we owe David Canales

  • Booking notes that reach the technician. The customer raised the diagnostic laptop when he scheduled, and it did not follow the job.
  • His teaching sequence recorded for the team. Asking permission to explain, then building the system from the outdoor unit up with car analogies, is genuinely excellent and completely repeatable.
  • Credit for holding the price line. Asked to compare parts against a new unit, he routed it to the equipment specialist rather than guessing — that is the SILO process and should never read as a gap.
  • Same-day specialist coverage kept available. He asked for one within the hour and got it, and that is exactly why this day converted.
Bottom line

David’s long call is close to a model of the whole process — expectations set, four honest check-ins, real teaching, tiered options, his own incentive named out loud, and a specialist standing in the house before he left. The short call is the same technician with the confidence removed. One tool he did not have turned into an apology, a referral to a competitor the customer had already fired, and two purchases walking out the door. The technical skill is not the gap. The gap is what he does when he does not have the answer.
