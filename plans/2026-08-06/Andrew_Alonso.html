1

The one-line verdict

Why it flipped

He made the hand-off free, easy, and immediate.

What actually happened

He walked the customer through both systems with photographs — you see all this rust building up on the shaft here?, you see how it’s bulged out? — showed that the repair money was going into a 36-year-old machine, and then pivoted cleanly: It might be time to speak with somebody from our install team about updating it. He removed both barriers in one breath: you’re not signing for the repairs… I just need signatures to allow him to come and so it’s zero dollars to speak to that guy.

What he still left on the table

He never asked for the five-star review, and he never handed the customer the conclusion — every explanation ended with Andrew stating the answer rather than the customer saying it back. On a call this warm, both were free.

5

What Andrew did right — real strength, build on it

  • He made the hand-off cost nothing and mean nothing. you’re not signing for the repairs… It’s to have him come out and talk to you guys about an updated system and so it’s zero dollars to speak to that guy. Two sentences, both barriers gone. This is the SILO play and he ran it clean.
  • Elite, generous teaching. Refrigerant history, the three equipment tiers with the years they appeared, the full refrigeration cycle drawn by hand, and how a heat pump reverses it: it can be 0°F outside, and there is still heat to pull from outside.
  • Transparency used as a closing tool, not a confession. can I be really transparent with you?… the life expectancy of these two is about the same. The only difference is efficiency. Telling a customer the expensive one will not last longer is exactly what buys the trust that follows.
  • He put himself in the answer. I’m gonna put a two stage Lennox on my house. Nothing else in a sales call carries that weight.
  • He refused the customer’s own exit. Sorry I couldn’t help you → No man, I’m here to help you man. The flip happens after that line, not before it.

Gap 1 — The best five-star position of the day, and no ask

What the customer said, unprompted"Good stuff, Andrew. Good." — and later, after the whole education: "Heat pumps are so cool, man."
How the visit ended instead"Do you mind if I take a photo of the Bronco so I can show it to my wife?… I’ll pass by tomorrow when they’re installing it. Good luck, my man."

The fix A customer who says good stuff out loud is a review already written. It belongs at the door as its own clean sentence, before the small talk starts: Mike, before I take off — if today was a five-star experience for you, a review with my name in it is genuinely how I get judged around here. Would you mind? Say it, then stop. He earns this one on almost every call and asks for it on none.

The one time he did it right"Are you guys aware of why, about the old refrigerants, this R22 stuff?" — and the customer came back with "Yeah. I’m trying to tell her… They didn’t even make that stuff anymore, do they?"
The pattern everywhere elseRust on the shaft, the bulged capacitor, the compressor testing at zero, the three tiers — each one explained to a customer whose replies are "Okay," "Oh," and "Yeah."

The fix He already owns the setup; he just needs to stop before the punchline. After the capacitor photo: It should test at 40 and it’s reading zero — what do you figure that means for this unit on the next 115-degree day? When the customer says it’s not going to run, the urgency is his, and the hand-off stops being a recommendation and becomes his idea.

Solid

Gap 3 — He kept selling after he had already won

After the signatures were taken"So yeah, they pay me per diagnostic. Do you know what piece rate is?… I get them done on average about 3 hours and 45 minutes. That’s how I make my money."

The fix Nothing here hurt him — the customer was warm and the specialist was already on the way. But the habit is the risk: on a colder call, minutes of extra talk after the yes is where a customer finds a reason to reconsider. Once the Option C signature is on the tablet, the next words are the five-star ask and the door. Bank it and go.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Every call ends with the five-star ask, spoken as its own sentence at the door, on flips and no-closes alike.
Proof it worked: The review request is audible on every recording that week without exception.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Hand over the conclusion. Every photograph and every meter reading ends in a question to the customer, not a statement — and then silence until they answer.
Proof it worked: On each recording a manager can point to the customer saying the problem back in their own words.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Close the loop and stop. Signature, five-star ask, door — no new material after the yes.
Proof it worked: Time between the Option C signature and leaving the home is under five minutes on every flipped call.

Andrew ran the hand-off the way the playbook draws it up: show the evidence, teach honestly, make the specialist free, and get the signature in the room. Nothing about his selling needs fixing. What is missing is smaller and entirely free — he never asks for the review he has plainly earned, and he tells the customer the answer instead of letting the customer say it.
