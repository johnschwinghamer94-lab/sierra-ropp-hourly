1

The one-line verdict

Why it didn’t flip

He offered the visit three times and never offered a day.

What actually happened

He laid out the specialist option properly — I have listed here the option where you can speak to one of our specialists from our install team, then you don’t necessarily have to say yes, then it’s totally free. Doesn’t obligate you in anything. Each time the customer moved a step further away: I don’t want to make a commitment of when for them to come out just today, then I just don’t want to do it this week. He accepted, and offered to follow up.

What flips it

She never objected to the visit — she objected to this week, and she then told him her own calendar: I have something Monday and Tuesday. That is a scheduling conversation, not a no. Totally fair — this week is full. Monday and Tuesday are out for you, so let me put our specialist down for Thursday morning. It’s free, he’s in and out, and if you want to move it, one text and it’s moved. Then stop talking.

5

What Andrew did right — real strength, build on it

  • He walked into a stated objection and disarmed it in ninety seconds. I’m not buying new Lennox. I did that already → two curious questions → Oh no, it’s just price, the cost. Most techs hear that opener and spend the visit avoiding the topic. He found out what it actually meant.
  • He refused to guess at a number, and said why. It’s hard for me to give you an exact number, and I wouldn’t want to give you an exact number and do you that disservice because… if we explore that option down the road and it’s a completely different number, then I’m the bad guy. That is exactly the SILO process — route the pricing to the specialist — and he made the customer feel protected by it rather than stonewalled.
  • Best refrigerant explanation of the day. Two law changes, two dates, and the supply-and-demand consequence, ending on a line she could repeat: you are essentially a generation behind.
  • He reconnected before presenting. You mentioned to me earlier… you want to see what we can do to keep these running as long as possible. So that’s our goal right here. Almost nobody on this board does this step at all.
  • Real human presence on a hard visit. A widow, a week into a new house, still carrying the Paradise fire. He listened to all of it without letting the call fall apart, and she asked for him by name at the end.

The gaps

each one in Andrew’s own call
Moderate

Gap 1 — “Not this week” was treated as “no”

Customer"Yeah, and I don't want to make a commitment of when for them to come out just today, or, you know." … "Yeah, I just don't want to do it this week."
Andrew"I know we've had a lot of us coming and going today. Yeah, so I completely understand." … "Would you like me to? I can follow up with you if that's all right, just to see. You know what your schedule's looking like?"
What she said next, unprompted"I think I have something. I have something Monday and Tuesday. Going on, so we'll see."

The fix She handed him her calendar. A customer who names the days she is busy is telling you which days she is free — that is not a refusal, it is a scheduling problem, and it dissolves the moment somebody proposes a date. Totally fair, this week is a lot. Monday and Tuesday are spoken for — let me put our specialist down for Thursday morning. Free, no obligation, and one text moves it. Then silence. An offer to follow up puts the work on the person who is already overwhelmed; a proposed date puts it on us.

Customer"I don't know how to do that."

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Never offer the specialist without offering a day. Every hand-off ends with two concrete time choices, and a soft “not now” gets one date proposed before it is accepted.
Proof it worked: On every recording where the specialist option is presented, a specific day is spoken out loud.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Ask, then hold the silence. After any question to the customer, no new information until they have finished answering — especially when the answer is short or uncertain.
Proof it worked: A manager can point to at least three moments per call where the customer, not the tech, filled the gap.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Seed the five-star at the door and ask for it before leaving, every call, closed or not.
Proof it worked: Both the seed and the ask are audible on every recording that week.
8

What we owe Andrew Trujillo

  • His refrigerant explanation captured and taught to the team. Two law changes, the dates, the supply consequence, and a one-line takeaway — delivered without a single number that belonged to the specialist.
  • A specialist who can be booked into next week from the customer’s doorstep. This call did not need more persuasion, it needed a calendar he could offer from.
  • Recognition that declining to quote replacement pricing was correct. He held the SILO process under direct pressure to name a figure and explained why in a way that built trust — that should never be read as a gap.
  • Support on the length of this visit. Four and a half hours in one home on a day with a grieving, newly moved customer is a real load, and the quality never dropped.
Bottom line

Andrew ran almost the entire model correctly on the hardest kind of visit there is, and lost it on one word: when. The customer never said no to the specialist — she said not this week, then told him exactly which days were already taken. Everything else was in place: her trust, her attention, honest prices, and a real reason to act. One habit closes this gap for good — the free visit gets a day attached to it before it is offered, every single time.
