1

The one-line verdict

What the day turned on

A customer described her own replacement plan out loud, and the call stayed on repair.

What actually happened

On the 67-minute maintenance visit the customer volunteered the whole decision: I realize that it’s 26 years old… if there was anything that was right this minute I would like to get through this summer if I can and I’ve got to put it on a new roof at the same time… I want to do it all at once if I can. Andrew’s answer: let’s at least explore what we can do to at least keep it going as long as we can with what we got.

What flips it

She was not objecting. She was describing a replacement she has already decided on and is sequencing against a roof. The response that flips that call is a booking, not a band-aid: Then let’s get our comfort advisor out while the weather’s still cooling off, so when you and the roofer pick a week, the AC number is already in your hand. Same visit, same customer, completely different ending.

5

What Andrew did right — real strength, build on it

  • He asks the concern question, not just the symptom question. What concerns do you have, though, in terms of performance wise? Mechanically wise. We’d hate for the AC to go out or anything like that. That is the FSG question, asked in his own words, and it is what produced his flip.
  • The $39 is on the table before he walks in. Did the office let you know about the $39 charge coming out? Small thing, and it is why nothing later in his calls feels like a sales move.
  • He is honest when the job is not his. The water-softener call could have been salvaged with a vague referral; instead he explained exactly what the plumbing team would and would not do.
  • He tells customers what protects them. The unprompted warning about third-party installs voiding a warranty is the kind of detail that buys trust for the whole visit.

The fix This is not an objection to overcome, it is a purchase being scheduled out loud. The only thing missing was someone to put a number and a date against it: That’s exactly the right way to do it. Let me get our comfort advisor out in the next couple of weeks — no charge — so when you and the roofer settle on a week, you already know what the system costs and how long it takes. In the meantime I’ll do everything I can to hold this one together for the summer. Both halves. He only ever said the second one.

Weak

Four calls, no second path named on any of them

Pattern128 minutes on tape across four customers — a 26-year-old system, a house holding 81° in 116° heat, an evening customer with an open list of repair estimates — and no comfort-advisor or specialist visit offered on any of them.
What did workThe Harris call produced a lead the same evening, on the strength of the discovery questions alone. That is what happens when the questions are good; imagine it with an option attached.

The fix The habit to build is mechanical, not emotional: nothing goes to the van until two paths have been said out loud. Here’s the repair I can do today, and here’s the no-cost specialist visit for the bigger picture. Which of those do you want to start with? He already earns the right to ask it on every call.

Weak

The customer with a list of estimates was heard and then left alone

Evening call, no job numberThe customer raised a previous list of repair estimates. Andrew acknowledged it and moved on without asking whether any of those items had come back.
Why it mattersAn old estimate list is a customer telling you they have already been shopping this problem. It is the cheapest check-in question on the board.

The fix Reopen the list every time it appears: You mentioned you’d had estimates before — which of those did you end up doing, and which ones are still sitting there? The answer tells you the budget, the history and the objection in one go, before you ever get to the van.

7

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: Two paths before the van. On every call, name the repair and the no-cost specialist visit out loud before leaving the customer. Nothing gets written up until both have been said.
Proof it worked: Manager hears two named paths at the end of every diagnosis this week; no call ends on ‘let’s keep it going as long as we can.’
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Timing statements are buying signals. Whenever a customer names a season, a roof, a move or a ‘when I’m ready’, book the advisor against that date instead of agreeing with it.
Proof it worked: Every call this week containing a stated timeline ends with a specific advisor slot offered, and the answer logged either way.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Seed the five-star ask into the expectations line he already gives, and collect it before leaving.
Proof it worked: Review seeded on every call; asked for on every completed visit.
8

What we owe Andrew Trujillo

  • Comfort-advisor availability that can be offered in the room — his flip happened because a lead could be typed the same evening, and the Nelson call needed the same thing.
  • Credit for the discovery work. His concern question is already at standard and it should be played back to newer technicians as the model.
  • A cleaner dispatch note for misrouted calls. He lost most of an hour to a water-softener ticket booked as an AC complaint, and he handled it better than the booking deserved.
Bottom line

Andrew is not short on warmth, honesty or discovery — those are all at standard and the Harris lead proves it. What he is short on is one sentence. On the Nelson call a customer described her own replacement, her own budget and her own timeline, and the call ended on keeping the old system alive. He does not need to learn how to sell; he needs to stop leaving the second path unsaid.
