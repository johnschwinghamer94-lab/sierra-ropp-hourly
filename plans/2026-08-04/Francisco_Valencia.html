The one-line verdict

What actually happened

5

What Francisco did right — real strength, build on it

  • He explains the failure chain, not the part. The fan motor is in charge of helping cool off that compressor. When it’s not spinning, then that compressor overheats itself, and it starts hurting the internal wiring because it becomes like an oven in there. The customer can retell that to their spouse.
  • He gets the customer to say the conclusion. On Padilla it was the customer who said chasing good money with bad money — the strongest close on his day, and it came out of her mouth, not his.
  • He follows the SILO process on pricing correctly. Replacement numbers get routed to the comfort advisor rather than guessed at in the driveway. That is the process working, and it is why Rister flipped.
  • His expectations line is complete. Then I’ll give you a report, and then you just let me know what you guys want to do with that information. Scope, sequence, and the decision left with the customer.
  • He builds real options when he has the time. Three tiers on Padilla, each with a cost and a timeline — that is a complete Decision step and he owns it.

The situationArbuckle · Job 670792735 — two systems, the older upstairs one had failed a week earlier and the customer said it was struggling and asked about replacing it.
What happenedDeflected to the repair technician first. No comfort-advisor appointment offered. Seven minutes on site.

The fix The customer had already made the enquiry — all that was needed was to accept it: Let me get our comfort advisor out to price the upstairs replacement, no charge, while the repair side handles the immediate problem. You’ll have both numbers in front of you. That sentence takes fifteen seconds and it is the difference between his best calls and his shortest ones.

Moderate

Short calls lose the whole back half of the model

The patternPadilla 57 min and Rister 106 min — both flipped, both with options built. Arbuckle 7 min, Casillas 8 min, Lippe 14 min — none flipped, no options built on any.
Lippe specificallyThe evaluation was approved and the customer was left uncertain about next steps — a call that had already earned the right to name one.

The fix The Decision step is not a function of call length; it is a function of habit. Even a fourteen-minute visit ends with two sentences: what I found, and the two things you can do about it. Set a rule — no call, however short, ends without a named next step and a date.

What we owe Francisco Valencia

Bottom line

Francisco’s long calls are among the best work on this team: real options, a failure chain the customer can repeat, and a cost conversation the customer closes herself. His short calls are a different technician — symptom, diagnosis, goodbye. On a seven-minute visit a customer asked about replacing a unit and was sent back to repair. The skill is not in question; the habit is. Every call, however short, needs the same last two sentences.
