1

The one-line verdict

Why three of five flipped

He names Option C out loud and then asks for the time.

What actually happened

On the HEWITT call the customer wanted the cheap fix and his wife wanted a second opinion. Francisco did both — installed the capacitor to get them cooling that night, then asked directly: did you guys want to schedule out the person for a later time in the week so that way at least you guys can get that info? It doesn't hurt just to get that info. Then when would you guys have time? then morning, midday, afternoon? — and booked Wednesday, 8 to 10.

What the FSG model calls for

That is the flip done correctly, twice over: the customer got cooling <i>and</i> an appointment, and nobody had to lose. On two other calls he skipped the booking step entirely and simply had the advisor dispatched to the house while he wrote the report. The Decision step is his strength — the Assessment step underneath it is where the coaching lives.

The fix Francisco is winning on teaching and option-building, and he is leaving the customer's own evidence on the table. On a thirty-year-old unit running non-stop, the power-bill number is the single most persuasive fact in the house — and it belongs in the customer's mouth, not his. Ask all three at the thermostat before the tools come out. His flip rate is already the second-best on the board; this is what takes it higher.

Moderate

Gap 2 — The numbers blur when the customer presses

Francisco"$4,474, $6,000, and then the coils is about $5,000." … "you're probably gonna be anywhere in the midst of $13,000 to $15,000."
Customer, lost"So the capacitor is $6,500? That's not including the fridge."
Francisco, walking it back"So the capacitor is $474. The compressor itself is $6,500 to replace with the refrigerant inside of it."

The fix That exchange went around three times before it landed. When a customer starts stacking figures, stop adding and reset to the page: point at one line, name one number, and stop until they nod. Capacitor, four seventy-four, tonight. Compressor, sixty-five hundred, later. Whole system, that's Sean's number, not mine. Three lines, three numbers, then silence. His teaching is far too good to be undone by arithmetic.

The fix He is closing his calls with a genuinely good question and stopping one sentence early. Seed it in the expectations block — my goal today is a five-star experience, so if I'm falling short at any point, stop me — and then collect it while you are packing up. On a night where he got a family cooling, booked an advisor and emailed the photos, the review was already earned.

6

This week's drill — three questions, then three numbers

Two habits, front and back

<b>At the thermostat, before the tools:</b>

"How often are the filters getting changed? Which rooms run hotter or colder? And what do you keep it set at in the summer — and what's a standard power bill for you at that?"

<b>At the presentation, when they start stacking figures:</b> point at one line, say one number, stop. Let them catch up before you say the next one.

What we owe Francisco

  • Advisor outcomes fed back to him on all three flips, so he learns which handoffs convert.
  • Recognition for the drawn diagram and the did I confuse you anywhere? habit — both should be taught to the rest of the board.
  • A pricing-delivery rep with a manager, since it is the only thing standing between his teaching and a cleaner close.
Bottom line

Francisco flipped three of five calls by doing the two hardest things well: he makes customers genuinely understand their system, and he asks for the appointment with a day and a time attached. What he never does is ask the four questions that would put the customer's own evidence — their bill, their hot room, their filter habit — into the customer's mouth before he ever quotes a price. He is closing on his teaching alone. Add the questions and this stops being a good day and starts being a repeatable one.
