1

The one-line verdict

The day in two calls

He converts by refusing to waste the customer's money — and loses by offering them the exit.

GEBHARDT — textbook

Compressor mechanically failed, R22, and he said the number without flinching: that compressor is 9,300. Then he protected the customer from it — it's a 26 year old system. Do you really want to put almost $10,000 into it to try to maybe potentially get another year? — and made the handoff concrete on the spot: Let me get Jordan out here while I write everything up. That way he gives you the information of what a new system costs, and then you'll have the information on the repairs, and then you can compare and contrast. The customer agreed and Joe called from the table.

GUILLERMO — talked out of his own visit

A promotional tune-up, third visit to the house, known concerns on file. The customer said I don't really have the funds for a full replacement. Joe answered I'm not here to have that conversation by any means — and then asked, four separate times, whether the customer wanted him to look at anything at all: Do you want to leave it alone? You want to proceed? That's up to you. The customer took the exit: I think we'll probably leave it alone for now. No evaluation was performed.

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • He got a specialist to the house from the kitchen table. Let me get Jordan out here while I write everything up… then you can compare and contrast what makes the most sense. Offered, agreed and called, all inside one visit.
  • He sold a membership by showing the arithmetic, not by describing the product — the part, the labour, the fifteen percent and the number before and after.
  • He refused an illegal shortcut and explained why. A customer wanted R22 poured in; Joe told him plainly: I risk my license by just charging it up without fixing the actual system. Then he told him what the honest route looked like.
  • He turned down a driveway to protect it. I work with oils all day. Last thing I want is to stain your driveway.
  • He asked the outcome question on a promotional call. What is it that we're looking to accomplish here today? — exactly the right question, on the one call where it mattered most.
  • He was genuinely present for a customer in the middle of cancer treatment, and offered something of his own rather than a platitude.

6

This week's drill — separate the tune-up from the sale, out loud

Trigger and response

Trigger: any version of "I can't afford a new system" said before the evaluation has happened.

"Good — then let's take that off the table completely. I'm not here to sell you a system today. I'm here to do the tune-up you booked, tell you exactly what's going on, and give you the number to keep this one running. You can do nothing with that information and it's still yours. Sound fair?"

Then start the evaluation. The customer's budget worry is a reason to look more carefully, not a reason to leave.

Bottom line

Joe ran more calls than anyone and won on the two that were hardest, both times by putting the customer's money ahead of the sale — a $9,300 compressor he talked a customer out of, and a membership he justified with arithmetic instead of adjectives. The same instinct is what cost him the third call: when a customer said money was tight, he offered to leave, and then offered again until the customer agreed. Do the work you were booked for, ask for the review you have already earned, and this is the strongest board on the team.
