The one-line verdict

Why one flipped

He asks the bill question every time — even when the answer is nothing.

What actually happened

Whenever everything's running, how much is the power bill spiking up in the summer? — asked on a house running solar, where the answer was we have like a $30 power bill. The customer's real concern was different and she said it plainly: I just want to see if it's something I can afford to fix or not, on a system only eight years old next to one they had just replaced after forty-two years.

What worked

With the cost argument unavailable, the lever was the comparison the customer had already made herself — the old system lasted forty-two years and this one is failing at eight. Noah's discovery surfaced that within minutes, and the lead followed. The bill question is worth asking even when the answer is zero, because the answer tells you which argument to use instead.

What Noah did right — real strength, build on it

  • He asks the bill question every single time. Third graded day running, including on a solar home where the answer was almost nothing — and the answer still told him which argument to use.
  • His discovery surfaced the customer's real concern in minutes. I just want to see if it's something I can afford to fix or not is the whole call, said early because he asked properly.
  • He let the customer make the comparison. Forty-two years versus eight — her framing, and the strongest possible case for replacement.
  • He arranges for the decision-maker to be present. Timing the report around the husband's availability is why the conversation had a chance.
  • He explains his own carefulness. The pigeons line is small and it tells the customer exactly what kind of technician is in their house.

What we owe Noah Weng

  • Credit the solar-home flip. He converted a call where his usual strongest argument did not exist, by using what the customer gave him instead.
  • Recording coverage. Four graded days and his presentations remain almost entirely unrecorded.
  • A four-system playbook. A house with four systems and a humidifier needs a different structure than a single-system call, and he was improvising it.
  • The review ask, made mandatory. Four graded days of promising and never collecting.
Bottom line

Noah asked what the power bill was on a house with solar panels, got told thirty dollars, and converted the call anyway — because the discovery had already surfaced the argument that mattered to that customer. That is the habit working. What is not working is the back half: two long visits, one of them four systems, produced no findings on tape and no leads. Write the report on every call, keep the recorder running, and ask for the review you promise at the start.
