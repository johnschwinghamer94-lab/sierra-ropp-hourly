1

The one-line verdict

Why four didn't flip

He built the whole case and then handed back the pen.

What actually happened

On the second-opinion call he did everything right up to the last exchange: named the immediate coil repair, warned the number was liquid, explained that the coil could take four to eight weeks to arrive, told the truth about the long term — could take care of the coil, but is that going to be a long-term solution? No, that's why I gave you the option to update — and offered the install specialist. Then: if you want to go with the repair cost, be my guest, and to we'll think about it, he asked the right question, got nothing right now, and took the signature.

What it was

A customer facing four thousand dollars now and eight thousand later, with weeks without cooling either way, is not stalling — they are stuck. Stuck customers need a next step, not more information. The specialist visit costs nothing and answers the exact question they cannot answer alone, so it should have been booked to a time before the pen came out, not offered as a philosophical option.

5

What Benjamin did right — real strength, build on it

  • He tells customers the repair won't save them. Could take care of the coil, but is that going to be a long-term solution? No. That costs him a four-thousand-dollar ticket and buys the credibility a replacement conversation needs.
  • He asked the right closing question. What things you have to think about that I can answer for you? is the correct move — he simply stopped when the answer was vague.
  • The nerve-damage analogy for a failing control board is the clearest explanation on the day and the customer immediately understood the consequence.
  • He researched a warranty position live in front of the customer rather than guessing, including what the manufacturer does and doesn't cover.
  • He flagged that a quote could move before the customer discovered it later. Small, honest, and rare.

8

What we owe Benjamin Wyllie

  • Credit the honesty and then fix the ending. He told a customer the repair would not solve their problem. That is exactly right, and it is wasted if it does not end in an appointment.
  • A stuck-customer script. Not an objection handler — a way to give someone facing two bad numbers a free third step with a date on it.
  • The home-warranty play, written down. Two graded days, two long warranty conversations, zero outcomes.
  • Clarity on ride-alongs. He is regularly accompanied and nobody has told him where the coaching happens.
Bottom line

Benjamin's presentation on the second-opinion call was the most complete and the most honest anyone gave this day — he priced the repair, priced the failure behind it, and told the customer plainly that the repair fixes nothing. Then he handed them the pen. Four of five calls ended with a well-informed customer and no next step. He is not missing information or trust; he is missing a date on a calendar.
