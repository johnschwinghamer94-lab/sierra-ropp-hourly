The one-line verdict

What the day turned on

He asks people what their plan is — and then puts a date on it.

What actually happened

The question that produced both flips: I think you need to come up with a game plan, because what if this thing fails tomorrow? Like, do you have a plan what you're going to do if it completely stops working? The customer answered with his own plan, and David closed it: so Friday 1 to 3, I just need a couple signatures. So this is just for them to come talk to you. So it's just $0 there. On the second call he phoned the manager from the living room after hours to get Monday booked.

What to fix

Two habits are costing him. He tells customers a problem cannot be solved — there's no solutions, there's nothing we can really do — on issues that are exactly what a specialist exists for. And on the third call, an 80-year-old customer said October, November, I want to replace it. I ain't waiting for it to drop dead next summer at 120 degrees and left the visit with no appointment.

5

What David did right — real strength, build on it

  • The game-plan question. Do you have a plan what you're going to do if it completely stops working? It produced both of his flips. It is not a pitch, it is not pressure, and it makes the customer state their own urgency out loud. This should be taught to the whole board.
  • He books it before he leaves. so Friday 1 to 3, I just need a couple signatures… it's just $0 there. On the other call he rang his manager after hours from the customer's living room to get Monday confirmed. Two appointments, two signatures, same day.
  • He ruled out a part he could have sold. The start capacitor measured 54 against 55 and he said so plainly. That single act of restraint is why the rest of his diagnosis was believed.
  • He explained the mystery, not just the fault. The internal-overload switch answered exactly what the customer had been confused about all afternoon — cooling that stopped and then came back.
  • He turned an objection into a date without arguing. I'm just kind of don't want any more this week → are you guys okay with Monday? No pressure, no re-pitch, just a smaller ask.
  • He took ownership of somebody else’s mistake. Sent to measure a filter the customer had already been promised, he apologised, photographed the equipment from multiple angles so it could not be disputed again, and escalated it himself.
  • He protects customers from being sold twice. On a home-inspection failure he gave the customers the evidence to take back to their realtor while refusing to badmouth anyone: I don't want to say nothing bad about your realtor.

Gap 1 — He tells customers a problem has no solution

Customer, asking directly"so what, what's the solution?" — David: "I mean there's, there's no solution… There's no solutions."
On the other call, about a hot room over the garage"There's really no way to do that unless you condition the garage space… people tell you, like, what can we do? There's nothing we can really do."
Later, contradicting himself"I mean, we can. We can try."

The fix An improperly installed plenum coil and an unconditioned space over a bedroom are both solvable — that is precisely what the comfort specialist is for. Saying “there's no solution” ends the conversation and then he has to walk it back thirty seconds later. Replace it with: It's not something I can fix from a repair ticket — but it is fixable, and it's exactly what our specialist looks at. Let me have him price both the right coil setup and the replacement so you can see the difference. Never hand a customer a dead end.

Three-week training plan

Week 1
Week 1
Drill: The full expectations sequence at the door, every call, audible on tape — look, thorough evaluation, options, approval, today, five star.
Proof it worked: All six beats in the first three minutes of every recording, and the five-star ask collected at the end.
Week 2
Week 2
Drill: Ban “there's nothing we can do.” Every unsolvable-sounding problem gets routed to the specialist as a named path instead.
Proof it worked: Zero dead ends on any recording; each one has a path and, where the customer agrees, a date.
Week 3
Week 3
Drill: Any stated replacement timeline — a month, a season, “before next summer” — becomes a specialist appointment in the same conversation, regardless of what the ticket was for. Plus: keep the visit on the customer's home.
Proof it worked: Every recording containing a replacement timeline also contains a booked or explicitly declined specialist visit.
