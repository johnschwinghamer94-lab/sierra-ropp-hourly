1

The one-line verdict

The call that defines the day

He taught a father of two exactly why his AC dies at nap time — and the tape runs out before he sells anything.

What he did

The complaint was vague — it gets like through that cycle delay… and then it gets super hot and then it finally kicks in. He turned it into a diagnosis the customer could repeat back. He drew it: I'll draw it out for you guys. That way it makes more sense. He named the limit and the reading against it — when it reaches 450, that's as high as it should be… right now when we check them, we're at 450 — and then the amperage: the amount required for those compressors, this one's 79… we're at 95. He explained the compressor as a heart: the higher the blood pressure, the strokes, heart attacks… when it exceeds 79, we're at 95. So it's just a matter of time. He found a live secondary drain leak, videoed it, and warned what it costs to ignore: if it fills up, it can spill over and cause ceiling damage.

What he did not do

Get to options. When the customer asked the buying question directly — what are we looking at? Price-wise, I guess — the answer was I'm not 100% sure. I'd have to look at the price book. The recording ends with him outside writing the report. A customer who has just been told both compressors are dying, on units he was told are original to the home with parts that take about six to eight weeks, never hears a number or a plan on tape.

4

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • He asked before delivering bad news — do you guys want to know what's wrong with the winter components? — which is why the carbon-monoxide conversation landed as information and not as a scare.
  • He confirmed the service fee and got a yes before touching anything, on a two-system charge.
  • He drew it out. Paper clips for a heat exchanger, blood pressure for a compressor, a ceiling number for thermal overload.
  • He found a live leak into the secondary pan, videoed it, and told the customer what ignoring it costs.
  • He gave time estimates every single time he stepped away and asked how the customer wanted to be interrupted.
  • He sequenced the bad news — one fire at a time — instead of quoting two system replacements at a family with two toddlers.
  • On the maintenance he arrived with the right filters, changed all three, priced them openly and carried the old ones out.

6

This week's drill — never park a price question

When a customer asks "what are we looking at?" before the report is written

"Great question — exact numbers on paper in twenty minutes. Here's the shape of it: you'll see a repair option and a replace option side by side, with what each one buys you. While I'm writing, is there anything else you want me to price out?"

He already earns the right to be asked. The only thing to add is refusing to let the moment cool while he walks to the truck.
