1

The one-line verdict

The call that defines the day

He turned a self-declared anti-upsell customer into someone asking him for prices — and then stopped.

What he did

She told him where she stood in the first ten minutes: I'm real anti-upselling… do your job. He did not argue and he did not retreat. He taught. He walked the whole system in order — thermostat to switch to mixing box to trunks to coil to compressor — then named what he could see and what he could not: this cooling coil, I call it like the Statue of Liberty effect… once we get that warm air pulled from the home across this cold coil, we start to see condensation, which eventually starts to rust and oxidise. He explained why summer is the busy season — pressure and temperature go together really well — and he repeatedly told her what was not wrong: you don't have any leaks there at the moment. I wanted to make sure before conveying it to you; I'm not seeing cracks, because that's very good. By the back half of the call, she was asking him: did you get a price on the compressor while I'm writing my report? and so you'll get me each of the tier prices?

What he did not do

Ask for anything. He priced the compressor — the price for that compressor is 6,530 — named the future coil and heat exchanger at around 7,500, checked with management, discovered she already had the filter cabinet and honourably priced only the filter rather than the install, and arranged for another technician to run a filter out. Then the call ends. No option sheet, no which of these would you like me to take care of today, no membership question, no review asked, and no follow-up date on a compressor he had just described as the pressing issue.

What went right — real strength, build on it

  • He beat I'm real anti-upselling with teaching instead of pressure, and the same customer later asked him for three tiers of pricing.
  • He told her what was not wrong, repeatedly and specifically — no leaks, no cracks, dry secondary pan — which is why the things that were wrong were believed.
  • He found she already had the filter cabinet and cut his own quote, pricing the filter alone rather than the install.
  • He kept a promise about her time — done before 10:30, as committed at the door.
  • He checked a heat exchanger out of season and explained why it was part of what she had paid for.
  • He pulled the filter down to show her rather than asking her to take his word for its condition.
  • He answered what if I wait until it fails? honestly — honestly, no — with no manufactured urgency.

This week's drill — the sheet, then the ask

Any time a customer asks for more than one price

"Let me put these side by side so you can see them against each other. Here's what I'd do in your house and why. Which of these would you like me to take care of today?"

He has the hardest half of this already — the trust and the technical case. What is missing is one sentence at the end of a hundred-minute call.
