Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Cole hands off Option C on 56% of his scoreable calls (9 of 16) and wins repairs on 13% (2 of 16), and when he does hand off he executes it better than anyone on this roster: the Comfort Advisor arrives while he is still in the house. Call 15, it takes them about 30-40 minutes to get out here. I stay here until they get here. They measure up the system, see what it takes to get you to the new updated system, and they give you a concrete price. He is also the most disciplined technician in the corpus on the one thing the SILO process is strict about — asked four separate times what a new system costs, he declines four times in the same words: I’m not entirely sure what new units cost. I just specialize in the repair side. His single biggest gap is that on two calls the customer asked the buying question outright and no specialist followed. Call 3, What’s it cost to get a whole new air conditioner?, and Call 7, what is it the cost to replace a compressor? — both answered with refrigerant scarcity and a repair estimate, neither producing a turnover lead. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: the routing sentence he already says perfectly needs a second half. I just work on the repair side should always be followed by — and I can have the person who does know here in forty minutes.

Strengths

1. The warm handoff — the Comfort Advisor arrives before he leaves (Strong)

This is the best-executed handoff mechanic in the week’s corpus, and it is why his flips are unusually solid. Call 2: those guys can be here within 30 to 45 minutes. I usually hang out until they get here and then I show them around. Call 14, with the house hot and the customer waiting: I can usually get someone out here within 30 or 40 minutes. I hang out here, I show them everything that’s going on, and then they sit down with you and they give you a concrete price after they measured everything up. Call 8, when same-day is not possible, he books instead of hoping: let me give Ryan a call and see what times I have available tomorrow… they’ll be seeing you tomorrow between 1 and 3. Coaching value: the customer never has to re-explain the problem, and the specialist walks into a diagnosis that has already been accepted. Other technicians on this roster create the lead; Cole hands over the relationship with it.

2. Refusing to price a replacement, consistently and without awkwardness (Strong)

Four customers on four separate calls ask him what a new system costs, and he gives the same answer every time. Call 1: I just work on the repair side. I’m not entirely sure what a new unit cost. Call 3: I’m not entirely sure what new units cost. I mean, I just specialize in the repair side. Call 11 and Call 12 repeat it almost verbatim, and on Call 4 the customer asks outright — and you can’t quote me a price on replacing the whole system? — and he holds the line. Coaching value: this is exactly what the SILO process asks of a technician, and it is the discipline that makes the Comfort Advisor’s number credible when it arrives. Nothing here should change. What it needs is a second sentence, which is the subject of the first gap below.

3. Correcting another company’s misdiagnosis without attacking them (Strong)

Call 3 is the most impressive twenty minutes in his week. The customer has been told by a previous visitor that the system needs refrigerant. Cole explains why that cannot have been established: I don’t know how they tested whether the fact that it needs refrigerant or not, because you can’t see how much is inside of there unless that compressor turns on. He then explains why the recommendation would have been wrong even if true — we can’t just recommend to put refrigerant into it because it’s like filling your tire with air that has a hole. It’s gonna leak back out and cost you more money — and refuses to conclude until the equipment cools enough for a real reading. His framing is about standards, not about the competitor: puts a bad taste in everyone’s mouth about HVAC people when people like that come out. So that’s not the way we operate, and it’s not the way we’ll ever operate. Honesty is the best policy. The customer’s closing words are Thank you, Cole. Thank you for being honest. Coaching value: this is the highest-trust moment on the roster this week. It is also, as the gaps below record, the call with no turnover lead.

4. Teaching the refrigerant generations so the customer can reason for themselves (Strong)

He places the customer’s equipment on a timeline rather than just labelling it obsolete. Call 1: you’re on the first refrigerant nowadays. We’re on the third one. So that refrigerant was banned in 2010 to be manufactured or imported. Call 12 explains why the change happened and what replaced it: they moved down to A2L… they got rid of the 410, EPA banned it because it’s bad for the environment. If this ever gets let into the atmosphere, it destroys ozone. The incompatibility is always the same image — it’s like putting diesel in your gas car (Calls 2, 11, 15) — and Call 12 adds the memorable one for R-22: they call it liquid gold. Coaching value: a customer who understands there are three generations understands why the coil and the condenser have to move together, which is the technical core of the Option C argument.

5. Naming a safety issue accurately and without theatre (Solid)

Call 8, on a heat exchanger: where we have these black areas is where that metal is the thinnest. This is the last step before we see it crack. When it does crack, those bad gases that are supposed to leave the home follow the air stream and come into the home… It’s not cracked at the moment. But when we start to see these large black areas, that’s the next step. Call 11 explains why the rusted coil cannot simply be treated: we also can’t put chemicals on there to stop the rust because when we put chemicals on there, that’s the direct air you’re going to be breathing in. Coaching value: both are stated with the current status made explicit, which is what separates a legitimate warning from a scare.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The routing sentence stops halfway (Weak)

Cole’s refusal to quote a replacement is correct and consistent. What is missing is what should follow it. Call 3, the customer asks What’s it cost to get a whole new air conditioner? after being told the compressor is over $6,500, the coil is over $5,400 when it fails, and the system is overpaying on utilities by 23%. The answer stays inside repairs and no specialist is named; no turnover lead exists for that job. Call 7 is the same shape on a three-system property with two compressors over their plate values (125 against a 112 maximum, 91 against 79): what is it the cost to replace a compressor? is answered with R-22 scarcity pricing and nothing else. What would have flipped it: both customers had asked to be handed over. I just work on the repair side is only half the sentence — Call 15 has the other half, and it takes forty minutes to deliver.

Gap 2 — Converting a booked visit into a callback when the customer pushes on price (Moderate)

Call 2 he does everything right up to the last exchange. The offer is made properly, with duration and cost stated. The customer objects on price and names an alternative: it might be out of my price range because different companies charge different pricing… I can get my California guy to come here and replace it. Instead of the forty-minute arrival he offers on other calls, Cole closes with I’ll have them call you, okay? And they’ll give you their name because we got a lot of guys. No lead was created. What would strengthen it: a price objection is the argument for a free concrete quote, not against it. The response that fits is the one he uses on Call 14 — the specialist can be here before the objection has time to harden.

Gap 3 — Long option presentations that arrive after the customer has stopped listening (Moderate)

On Calls 3 and 12 the report runs through immediate repairs, future repairs, refrigerant pricing per pound, energy percentages and rebate mechanics in a single unbroken presentation. Call 3’s customer interrupts twice with the same question in different words — what’s it going to take to fix it and how much is it going to cost?, then That’s $6,500. How much else is it going to cost to get this to work? — which is the sound of someone who has lost the thread. What would strengthen it: the third option should be introduced before the arithmetic rather than after it. On Call 15, where it comes early, the customer signs; on Call 3, where it never arrives, the customer thanks him and keeps their broken air conditioner.

Gap 4 — No review ask anywhere in twenty-two recordings (Weak)

Call 3 ends with Thank you, Cole. Thank you for being honest from a customer who had just been misled by another company. Call 4’s customer volunteers you guys have been so professional and easy to work with. I really appreciate your time. Call 8’s ends with Thank you, Cole. I really appreciate it. None of the twenty-two recordings contains a request for a review. What would strengthen it: the signature ritual on Call 8 is already a natural pause with the customer’s pen in hand.

Gap 5 — A derogatory remark about a customer on the recording (Weak)

Call 14 ends, after the customer conversation, with an exchange in which the customer’s frustration is attributed to their ethnicity. Call 4 and Call 15 also contain extended crude banter with a colleague while on the property. What would strengthen it: this changes no band in this report and no customer is heard reacting to it, but it is on a company recording made during a paid visit, and the Call 14 remark is of a different character from ordinary off-topic talk. It is recorded here because a manager should see it, not because it is a coaching point about technique.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Sixteen scoreable calls, and the diagnosis is essentially invariant across all of them — the same two compressor measurements, the same diagram, the same energy percentage. The split is entirely about whether the specialist is summoned.

  • Ready: Neutral. Prior-work inventory appears on flips and no-moves alike.
  • Welcome: Neutral. The introduction is consistent; the expectations block is thin on both outcomes.
  • Assessment: Neutral, and unusually so. Call 3, a no-move, contains the most careful diagnostic reasoning in his week, and Call 7, a no-move, measures three systems properly.
  • Decision: The entire difference, and it is binary. Where the Comfort Advisor is summoned — usually within forty minutes, occasionally booked for the next day — the call flips. On Calls 3 and 7 no third option is built at all, and on Call 2 a booked visit is downgraded to a callback that never happened.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The paperwork, the photographs and the absent review ask look the same whichever way the call ended.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Cole’s two clearest losses are the two calls where the customer asked the buying question in plain words. On Call 3 the customer had just been misled by a competitor, trusted him visibly, and asked what a new system costs; on Call 7 a homeowner with three systems and two failing compressors asked the same thing. Both received an accurate refusal to quote and nothing after it. Everything needed for the flip was present — the diagnosis, the trust, the customer’s own question — except the second half of a sentence he says correctly on every call. His wins are not better calls than his losses; they are the same calls with a phone call to a Comfort Advisor in them.

Beyond the Guide

The Comfort Advisor’s visit is captured on the same recording

Call 12’s recording continues through the specialist’s arrival and into a full replacement conversation — Costco executive-membership rebate mechanics, a 2% bonus, a 10% shop card and system totals. Cole’s own position minutes earlier on the same recording is explicit: I’m not entirely sure. I just work on the repair side, so I don’t know exactly how much new systems cost. Observation: the material is recorded here as evidence that the warm handoff works end to end, not as technician behaviour. It is also a reminder when reading these transcripts that a single recording can contain two roles.

Energy analysis as a standing part of the options sheet

A quantified utility penalty appears on Calls 2, 3, 4, 11 and 15 — 12%, 23%, 24%, 8% and 10% respectively — and on Call 15 he volunteers the reason the customer may not see it: I know you mentioned you have solar, so it might be a reason as to why you’re not seeing that on your bill. Coaching implication: consistent, quantified and honestly caveated. Worth standardising across the team, provided the derivation is the same every time.

Explaining what a home warranty will and will not do

Call 5: they don’t come out until anything’s actually broken… they won’t come out kind of like what we are today, just taking a look at the system, or replacing any preventative parts. It’ll be to the point where when this motor breaks, it’s when they come out. Call 13’s customer defers to their home warranty and he accepts it without pressure. Coaching implication: accurate and non-adversarial, and it positions preventative work without disparaging the customer’s existing cover. It is also, on Call 13, the point at which a no-cost comparison would have cost nothing to offer.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Finish the routing sentence. I just work on the repair side is correct and should never change; it needs — and I can have the person who does know here in forty minutes attached to it. Calls 3 and 7 are the two calls where the customer asked and the second half was missing.
  • Answer a price objection with the free quote, not with a callback. Call 2’s customer said the price might be out of range and was offered a phone call. Call 14’s customer was in a hot house and had a specialist there inside forty minutes.
  • Introduce Option C before the arithmetic, not after it. On Call 3 the customer asked the same question twice while the numbers were still being read out.
  • Add the review ask to the signature ritual. Twenty-two recordings, no ask, on a week that includes a customer thanking him specifically for being honest.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for three of the five no-moves; Priorities 3 and 4 improve calls that are already going well.
