Bottom Line

Benjamin hands off Option C on 59% of his scoreable calls (10 of 17) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 17), and he makes the most complete case for the handoff of anyone on this roster: he prices the repair, sets it against the age of the equipment, and names the risk of spending the money twice — Call 2, I don’t want you putting $10,000 into a system and those coils leak, and those coils are something we can’t even get anymore because the refrigerant that you have flowing through your system has been banned since last year. Do you want to schedule at least for the time when you and your wife are available?

Strengths

1. Turning the repair estimate into the argument for the handoff (Strong)

Nobody on the roster does this as completely. He prices the repair honestly, then sets it against the equipment’s age and the refrigerant position, and lets the customer draw the conclusion. Call 9: let’s say the compressor goes out first and you want to for some reason spend $6,400 on your older system, which I don’t recommend because right here you got around $8,000 in just repairs. It’s a lot of money put towards an older system. That’s why at least getting this option. Call 2: I wouldn’t recommend putting a lot of money towards an older system… the system itself is still 11 years old. So those coils at any point could pop a leak. Call 14: you try to fix the compressor, so you get a leak or another component fails and you’re like, holy crap, I just spent $5,500. Coaching value: the customer is never argued with; they are shown two numbers and the reason one of them may be wasted. It is the most reusable Option C argument in this week’s corpus.

2. Holding the line on replacement pricing while pricing repairs precisely (Strong)

He is exact about what he can quote and explicit about what he cannot. Call 14: I don’t have pricing on what new systems look like. Call 15, asked directly — So you don’t have any idea how much is a new system? — he answers I don’t know how much new systems cost and stays on what he does know, which on that call is that the refrigerant alone is unobtainable at any price. Call 15 again: unfortunately I just do repairs, but I did talk with your parents and they were okay with having someone come out here to talk about what pricing looks like. Coaching value: this is exactly right under the SILO process. The technician prices repairs and routes replacement, and his precision on the first half is what makes the routing on the second half credible.

3. Making the customer’s reason the reason (Strong)

He reliably restates the customer’s own stated goal back into the report’s opening line — you said you want your system to be reliable during the summer, which is why I conducted a thorough evaluation (Call 5), I came here for a no-cool service call and you want your system up and running as fast as possible (Call 13). On Call 12 the goal becomes the argument for urgency: I don’t want you to wait 6 to 8 weeks to get something taken care of because I know you don’t want to go all of July and August with no air conditioning… I want to get you the fastest plan possible. On Call 6 he identifies the customer’s real complaint — filters clunking — and shows how a system change removes it entirely. Coaching value: the handoff is framed as service to the goal the customer arrived with, not as a new proposition.

4. Regulatory and safety honesty, even when it costs him the easy fix (Strong)

Call 3 and Call 15 both involve a leaking system and a customer who would happily have paid for a top-up: due to the EPA, we can’t just fill it right back up because it goes straight out into the environment and we’ll get fined for that… I’ll lose my license. Call 7 he declines part of the job for safety and says so up front: I won’t be able to clean the bottom of your condenser out there because of all the bird poop inside of it, because it’s a hazard. Call 4 he refuses to fit a customer-supplied part — wrong size, and outside the company’s warranty. Coaching value: three separate opportunities to take the shortcut, three refusals, each explained. This is the foundation the rest of his credibility rests on.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Leaving the offer conditional after the customer states replacement intent (Weak)

Call 14 is the clearest lost flip of the week across the whole roster. Two systems, both compressors over their plate values (highest it should be is 79. You’re at 96), a failed motor start device, roughly $7,000 in repairs, and builder-grade equipment on banned refrigerant. The customer reaches the conclusion unprompted: at these prices, I think we would just like replace the whole thing. What follows is not a booking but a hypothetical — if that’s something that you guys want to at least learn to get to know and see what that looks like, we can always do that — and then a general remark that prices rose 30% last year. No specialist is scheduled and no turnover lead exists for that job. What would have flipped it: the customer had already said yes to the idea. Call 7 shows the sentence that converts it: name the specialist’s working hours and ask which one suits them.

Gap 2 — Listing replacements as repairs when the system is past saving (Moderate)

Call 3 contains no third option at all. The compressor is drawing high power, the blower motor and fan motor are both leaking oil, and a coil leak is identified as unrepairable — let’s say that it’s a leak somewhere in these fins. That’s not fixable. That’s not repairable, unfortunately. His summary is a list of three component replacements and a shrug: it’s up to you what you want to do… so we’re looking at replacing the compressor, replacing the blower motor, replacing that fan motor. What are your questions about that? The customer was, on the same recording, collecting estimates from other companies. No lead was created. What would have flipped it: when three of the four major components need replacing and the coil cannot be sourced, the options sheet has a Section C by definition. It was not built.

Gap 3 — Deferring the lead to a phone call instead of creating it on the visit (Moderate)

Call 18 reads on the transcript as a completed win: the customer wants replacement, wants it from Sierra, and understands the process. Benjamin’s plan is to act later — I’m calling him at noon on Tuesday to get a CA out immediately. ServiceTitan shows no turnover lead was ever created off that job on any date. Call 5 has the same shape at lower stakes (I’ll give you a call on Monday so we can see what works best for you guys), as does Call 6 (when I hear back from him, I’ll reach out to you). What would strengthen it: a lead created in the home survives the technician’s memory, his schedule and the customer’s change of mind. An intention to call on Tuesday does not, and on Call 18 it did not.

Gap 4 — Pressing a customer who has disclosed a financial obstacle (Moderate)

On Call 2 the customer states that she was declined for finance because of late mortgage payments. The response is to encourage re-application — it’s worth always seeing what that can look like to see if you’ve been approved, because I see that we even have financing options as low as $150 a month — and to keep going after she repeatedly asks for time. Benjamin’s own account afterwards is candid about it: I did have to push for that one… she kept on saying, I want to think about it. On a separate call he states the underlying rule out loud: it’s either the same day or no day… if someone says no, let’s schedule it out, be like, you know what, never mind, not even worth my time. What would strengthen it: under the manager’s doctrine a lead is the win, and a lead scheduled for the first of the month is still a lead. Treating a later date as a loss is what turns a patient handoff into pressure, and pressure is the one thing that can undo the credibility his diagnosis earns.

Gap 5 — Off-microphone conduct about customers (Weak)

Several recordings capture disparaging remarks about the customer, made on the live line during or immediately after the visit. The clearest is Call 15, where an extended derogatory comment about a customer’s adult daughter, including a gendered slur, is recorded after the conversation ends. Call 3 contains profanity directed at a third-party contractor, and Call 10 is largely off-topic conversation with a ride-along colleague inside the customer’s property, ending with his own verdict on the visit: well, that one cooked the call. What would strengthen it: nothing in these recordings reached a customer’s ear, and none of it changes a band in this report. It is recorded because it is on a company recording during a paid visit, and because Call 10 is the one no-move in his week that the recording itself attributes to the distraction.

Gap 6 — No review ask anywhere in eighteen recordings (Weak)

The close is otherwise the most structured on the roster, and the goodwill is real — Call 13’s customer brings him water mid-diagnosis, Call 4’s ends warmly, Call 5’s customer thanks him twice on the way out. He asks about the membership plan; he does not ask about a review. What would strengthen it: the sign-off ritual already has three set pieces in it. A fourth costs nothing.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Seventeen scoreable calls make this a reliable read. The diagnosis is close to invariant across the week — the same blood-pressure frame, the same tolerance arithmetic — and the split lives entirely in whether the third option becomes an appointment.

  • Ready: Neutral. History is checked on flips and no-moves alike; Call 18, a no-move, contains the most thorough preparation in the week.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong on the calls he opens himself.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 3 and Call 14, both no-moves, carry diagnoses at least as complete as any flip.
  • Decision: The whole difference, and the pattern is specific: on the flips he names the specialist and asks about timing; on Calls 3 and 14 the option is absent or hypothetical, and on Calls 5, 6 and 18 it is converted into a promise to call later, which on Call 18 produced nothing.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The paperwork ritual, the membership ask and the absent review ask look the same whichever way the call ended.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Benjamin builds the strongest case for Option C on the roster and then, on his losses, stops one sentence short of an appointment. Call 14’s customer volunteered that they would rather replace the system and left with a hypothetical. Call 3’s customer needed three major components replaced on unobtainable refrigerant and was given a list. Call 18’s customer asked for a replacement from Sierra and the lead was deferred to a phone call that ServiceTitan shows never produced one. The gap is not the argument, the diagnosis or the customer’s willingness — all three were present. It is that the handoff is finished with an offer rather than with a time.

Beyond the Guide

The service fee waived when a Comfort Advisor attends

Call 1 records the practice explicitly on a call to a colleague: We were supposed to charge $79 to get out here, but since the CA came out, we don’t charge them a dime… Because they agreed to have the CA come out? We don’t say that, but that’s kind of what we do. Manager-level observation, not a technician coaching point: an undisclosed fee waiver contingent on accepting a specialist visit is a commercial policy question. It is recorded here because it appears to be operating as an informal incentive rather than a stated offer, and the manager should decide whether it is one.

Energy-cost analysis as a standing part of the options conversation

Calls 8 and 9 both include a quantified running-cost argument: I did do an energy analysis of the system. You are overpaying by about 10% on your utility bills every single month. Now, it doesn’t sound like a lot, but everyone loves a 10% discount. Coaching implication: it reframes the decision from a repair bill to an ongoing cost and it is delivered without exaggeration. Worth standardising across the team, provided the percentage is derived the same way every time.

Brand and reliability guidance offered before the specialist arrives

Call 9: Lennox with the new refrigerant has really jumped up to the top for reliability. And Trane, Carrier, those have dipped down since the new refrigerant came out. Call 1 and Call 12 carry similar comparisons. Coaching implication: it is confident and customers respond to it, but it spends the Comfort Advisor’s opening material and commits Sierra to a brand position before anyone has sized the job. Worth a conversation about where the technician’s remit ends.

Honest handling of home-warranty limitations

Call 7: our experience with home warranty, they don’t like to deal with expensive parts because this is a very expensive part. Call 18 he walks a customer through the warranty process properly rather than around it — we have to follow the process. If they want the reimbursement, they have to have one of their licensed contractors out — even though it delays the work. Coaching implication: this is the right instinct and it is worth naming, because it is the same instinct that should have produced a lead on that call and did not.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • When the customer says replace, book rather than offer. Call 14 is the single most expensive sentence in his week: the customer volunteered replacement intent and received a hypothetical. Call 7’s do you want to schedule at least for the time when you and your wife are available? is the fix and it is already his.
  • Create the lead in the home, not on Tuesday. Call 18 shows what the deferral costs — a customer who wanted a Sierra replacement, and no record of it anywhere in ServiceTitan.
  • Build a Section C whenever the coil cannot be sourced. Call 3 listed three component replacements on a system with an unobtainable coil and never reached a third option.
  • Treat a later date as a win, not a loss. The same day or no day rule is what turns his patience into pressure on calls like Call 2, and it is at odds with the doctrine that the lead is the outcome being measured.
  • Add the review ask to the existing sign-off ritual. Eighteen recordings, no ask, alongside a membership question that is asked every time.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 to 3 account for the three clearest lost flips in the week; Priority 4 is about how the wins are won; Priority 5 is upside on calls already going well.
