Bottom Line

Strengths

1. He draws the whole system and then checks whether it landed (Strong)

On Call 8 he opens with a measurement — on a scale of 1 to 10, how well would you guys say you know how the AC works for you? — draws the outdoor box, the line set, the coil, the heat exchanger and both mixing boxes, and then closes the loop: are we close to 10, or where would you say we are? Or is there anything that I can elaborate a little better for? The same drawing appears on Calls 3, 6, 9, 11 and 13, always with the same self-deprecating line — my John Madden is not the best — that gets the customer relaxed enough to interrupt. Coaching value: this is the single most transferable thing on the roster this week; it converts a service call into a teaching session and is a large part of why five of his eight calls end with the customer asking to see replacement numbers.

2. He removed his own immediate repair because he checked a warranty nobody asked about (Strong)

Call 6 opens with an outdoor fan motor leaking oil and ends with no immediate repair on the invoice: while I was out there, I took an extra moment to check, and you're still under warranty. So essentially what that means is… you can wait till that motor fails and then replace it so that you're not putting money into it right now. The customer states it back plainly — if I do it just because I want to do it right now, it's going to cost me. But if tomorrow it seizes up, it's under warranty and you guys take care of it — and Nathan confirms it. He then still booked the advisor conversation for Friday at ten. Coaching value: he gave away a thousand-dollar repair and kept the lead, which is exactly the trade the flip doctrine asks for.

3. He routes every replacement price to the specialist and explains why the phone quote is worse for the customer (Strong)

Call 3's customer asks directly who prices a new system, and Nathan does not guess: a lot of companies will be like, call and get a quote… The reason this is inaccurate is because we attribute it to like a doctor not seeing the body entirely and giving a diagnosis. Call 8 adds the reason the visit is short: it's not going to be as long as I'm here. So they typically take maximum around an hour. Call 12 adds the urgency: they'd be able to come out here in the next couple, cuz I want you guys to get cooling immediately. Coaching value: this is correct SILO process executed with a reason the customer can accept, and it is why the handoff lands rather than reading as a brush-off.

4. He works the safety check that is not in season, every time (Strong)

The heat exchanger is a heating-season component and he inspects it on every attic visit anyway — Call 11: this isn't used until heating season, right? But the only reason I check it, because I'm like, I'm already up there for you, I would hate to leave a home and not go over everything with you. On Calls 6, 8, 9, 11 and 12 he states the result both ways, always confirming the safe finding first: there is no cracks in it, so there's not currently a crack that's gonna make it to where you guys have carbon monoxide. Coaching value: the out-of-season find is what opens the replacement conversation on three of his five flips, and it arrives as a safety statement rather than a sales one.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether the Option C offer survived the customer's first answer: on Calls 6, 8, 11 and 12 he asks again, offers a day, and books it, and on Calls 3, 7 and 9 the first no or the first silence ends the conversation.

  • Ready: Strong on both sides — Call 3's pull of last year's amp reading is the best Ready evidence in his week and it is on a no-move. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Strong on both sides. The agenda, the tarp and the shoe covers are identical on the flips and the no-moves; Call 7's customer got the same visit as Call 12's.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides — Call 3 and Call 7 contain his most detailed teaching of the week and both produced nothing. His diagnosis has never been the problem.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Weak — the whole story. Call 11's Is there a specific day within the next 7 days? against Call 7's No. Okay.
  • Deliver: Solid on both sides, and identical — the final walk of the property and the restored thermostat happen either way, and the review is never asked for either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same drawing on the same notepad, same three hours of teaching — but two endings, and the fork is whether he asked a second time. On Call 11 the customer says I don't have the time today and he does not accept it as an answer: I can schedule that out for you just so you know what's going to work for you guys in the long run… Is there a specific day within the next 7 days?, then a Monday slot, then a confirmation on the doorstep. On Call 6 a customer who has just declined every repair still ends up booked for Friday at ten. On Call 8, after nearly fourteen thousand words and a customer explaining he will be in radiation isolation for a fortnight, Nathan works around the treatment schedule and still lands the appointment — I circled C. On Call 7 the same offer gets No and stops there; on Call 9 it is offered to a customer already late for a 12:45 meeting and no answer is ever recorded; on Call 3 the customer asks for the whole-system conversation herself — I think I need to talk to somebody about the entire system — and the recording ends with Nathan waiting on a text about a coupon rather than booking her. The variable is not the teaching, the honesty or the care — those are constant and they are the best on this roster. It is whether the ask survived one no.
