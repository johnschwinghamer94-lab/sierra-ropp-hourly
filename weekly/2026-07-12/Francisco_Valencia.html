Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Francisco hands off Option C on 64% of his scoreable calls (14 of 22) and wins repairs on 5% (1 of 22) — the highest raw handoff count in the week, fourteen verified turnover leads off twenty-three recordings. What produces it is mechanical rather than persuasive: he shows the customer the third option with a zero next to it and takes a signature before he leaves. Call 20: the only thing that they ask me to do is just an autograph to acknowledge that you want to get an estimate from them so that way they can put it onto the ticket for you. And just to show you, there’s no charge for that. This is it right here. So it’s talking about a system update, and it’s $0 for that. He also does the one thing nobody else on this roster does at all — Call 21 contains the only review ask in the entire week’s corpus. His single biggest gap is that on the calls where the fault is severe but the visit is short, the third option is never built: Call 23, a system losing refrigerant with a negative suction reading, ends with the customer declining a $700 repair and no specialist offered. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: the signature page he uses fourteen times should not depend on how long he has been in the house.

Strengths

1. Turning the handoff into a signature, not an intention (Strong)

This is the mechanism behind fourteen verified leads. Call 20: just an autograph to acknowledge that you want to get an estimate from them so that way they can put it onto the ticket for you… it’s $0 for that, followed by the arrangement being made while he is still on site — the guy is already making him a ticket to your address so that way they can come out here. And then I’m just gonna leave my ladder set up for him. Call 15 he explains why the signature exists, which removes the suspicion it might otherwise create: it’s just that way they know that I’m not scheduling appointments on my own. Call 18 he compresses the whole thing into one move: let’s have someone come out today right now and give you that price. Coaching value: this is the single most copyable habit in the week. Other technicians on this roster make a better argument for Option C and finish with a suggestion; Francisco finishes with a booking.

2. Anchoring the recommendation to how long the customer is staying (Strong)

He asks, or listens for, the customer’s horizon and lets it decide the recommendation rather than deciding for them. Call 9: it would make more sense that way if you are keeping the house. If you guys are selling, then this would make more sense, because this will get you guys the best chance of it making it through that process… But if this is a house that you guys see yourselves a couple more years, then this is only going to be patch-up work. Call 13: based on what you’re saying that you’re going to be here for 10 years, it’s going to be a hard decision to do the repairs just because you’re gonna just add a bandage here and then a bandage there. Coaching value: the customer is never told what they need; they are shown which answer follows from a fact they supplied. It is the least resistible form the Option C argument takes anywhere in this corpus.

3. Asking for the review (Solid)

Call 21 is the only recording in the entire week — across every technician on the roster — that contains a request for a review, and the delivery is self-deprecating enough that it costs nothing: if you have time, you can leave a review, be like, hey, Francisco is the worst technician out here, I never send them out here. So it just helps us a lot when the homeowners do that. Coaching value: this is worth lifting verbatim into team training. It is also worth noting that it appears on a no-move — the ask does not depend on the outcome, which is exactly why it should be routine.

4. Explaining the refrigerant transition rule accurately and without drama (Strong)

He gets the technical constraint right every time and states the consequence plainly. Call 13: if you replace the outside, you have to replace the inside. If you replace the inside, you have to replace the outside, cuz we have to transition you over to the new refrigerant. Call 5 he diagnoses why a previous company’s cheap fix was wrong: they tried putting a 410A refrigerant… but that 410A has higher pressure. If they would have put that 410A, then it would have leaked, it would have made those coils burst because of the high pressure that it’s not rated for… they’re like, well, it’s the lowest cost, quickest fix. Call 7 he identifies the one case where a partial swap is legitimate. Coaching value: the argument for a full system is being made on engineering grounds rather than sales grounds, which is why it survives the customer asking why.

5. Working the call in the customer’s language (Solid)

Call 1 is conducted substantially in Spanish, including the diagnosis of a frozen coil, the ageing-equipment conversation and a full explanation of the finance process — nosotros no somos bancos, pero trabajamos con… te van a mandar un email… si quieren pagar más rápido, lo pueden hacer — and it produced a verified turnover lead. Coaching value: a capability the roster should know it has, and one that removes the most common reason a customer defers a decision, which is not understanding it.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Short calls with severe faults never reach a third option (Moderate)

Call 23 is the clearest loss. The system is losing refrigerant, and the pressure readings are not marginal — you have a negative, and then you have one that’s supposed to be in the 8 to 12, but instead at 37. This one’s supposed to be 8 to 12, but it’s negative. The customer declines the repair on cost: no, I can’t do it. 700, that’s too much. The recording ends there. No A/B/C sheet is built, no specialist is named, and no turnover lead exists. Calls 17 and 19 have the same shape at lower severity — a diagnosis delivered, no options constructed. What would have flipped it: a customer who has just said a $700 repair is beyond them is the customer with most to gain from a free comparison. The signature page from Call 20 takes under a minute.

Gap 2 — Letting the customer edit the options sheet down without re-anchoring (Moderate)

Call 9 he builds the full sheet properly, names Option C out loud, and then lets it dissolve line by line. The customer works through the estimate crossing items off — it would just be crossing out the fan motors. No fan motor, no fan motor. And then just be the motor start device and then the contactor — and closes on so the total is $907, correct? The compressor was reading cautious on the winding test and the blower-plus-compressor future was priced at roughly $6,900 on the same call. No lead was created. What would strengthen it: the customer choosing the cheapest line in Section A is the moment Section C matters most, because they have just told him the budget. Re-stating C once, after the edit, costs nothing and would have made this a flip and a repair rather than only a repair.

Gap 3 — Quoting replacement scope, tiers and discounts himself (Moderate)

Call 18 he moves well past the technician’s remit and into the Comfort Advisor’s. He gives system price bands (it’s 18 to 25, but that’s for the low tier), tiers the brands (Goodmans are going to be cheaper, but they both come with the same warranty. It’s just Goodmans are also lower-tier systems), prices optional scope (it’s only about $1,000 to $2,000 extra to do the zoning completely), explains the multi-unit discount mechanics (at that time we’re only pulling one permit, we’re only doing one drywall repair) and finishes by committing a figure on the specialist’s behalf: I’ll tell him that it’s a 10 grand off. The call flipped, so this cost nothing on the day. What would strengthen it: under the SILO process the replacement conversation belongs to the Comfort Advisor, and every number spoken before they arrive is one they must either honour or walk back. His own instinct on other calls is exactly right — Call 5, that would have to be talking to them and figuring out exactly what that price looks like — and that is the line to hold on every call, not most.

Gap 4 — The expectations block is missing from the front of the call (Moderate)

Across twenty-three recordings the parking question and introduction are reliable, but the block the rest of the roster runs — here is what I will check, here is what I will report, nothing happens without your approval — is rarely stated. Call 3 comes closest, and only as a description of what will happen next: I’ll take a look at everything, and then once I’m done, I’ll have a report written out for you, and then I’ll put the price of those filters, and then if you approve them, then I’ll swap them out. What would strengthen it: the permission-to-report question costs one sentence and pre-authorises everything the options sheet later contains. Given how strong his Decision step is, this is the cheapest available upgrade to his front half.

Gap 5 — The review ask appears once in twenty-three recordings (Moderate)

Call 21’s ask is the best in the corpus and it is also the only one. Calls 5, 13, 15, 16 and 20 all end warmly, with signatures taken and appointments booked, and none of them contains it. What would strengthen it: nothing about the sentence needs changing. It needs attaching to the same page as the autograph, so that it fires whenever the paperwork does.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Twenty-two scoreable calls makes this a reliable read, and the split is unusually mechanical. Francisco’s diagnosis is consistent across the week; what varies is whether the options sheet gets built at all.

  • Ready: Neutral. The original-versus-replaced inventory appears on flips and no-moves alike.
  • Welcome: Neutral. The introduction and parking question are present on both outcomes; the expectations block is thin on both.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 23, a no-move, contains as clear a refrigerant diagnosis as anything in the week.
  • Decision: The entire difference, and it is binary rather than qualitative. Where the A/B/C sheet exists, Option C is named and usually signed; where the call ends at the diagnosis (Calls 8, 17, 19, 23), no lead is created. Call 9 is the one call where C was named and still lost, and it was lost during the customer’s edit.
  • Deliver: Neutral, other than that the single review ask happens to fall on a no-move.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Francisco has the most reliable handoff mechanism on the roster — a printed third option, a zero, and a signature — and fourteen leads to show for it. His losses are not calls where the mechanism failed; they are calls where it was never started, because the visit was short or the customer declined the repair before the sheet was written. Call 23’s customer said a $700 repair was too much, which is precisely the sentence the free comparison exists to answer, and the sheet was never built. The gap is not persuasion, diagnosis or paperwork discipline. It is that the options sheet is currently triggered by how the visit is going rather than by what the equipment is doing.

Beyond the Guide

The waived service fee explained as removing commitment

Calls 8 and 13 both record the service fee being waived by the office and framed to the customer, or about the customer, as removing obligation — they wave the service fee, so there’s no commitment to us, cuz we coming out here for free (Call 8), today there’s no cost, they waived your service fee (Call 13). Manager-level observation, not a technician coaching point: the same practice appears in another technician’s recordings this week. It is worth the manager confirming when the waiver applies and how it should be described, so that it is consistent across the roster.

Diagnosing other companies’ work without disparaging them

Call 5 he explains exactly why a previous installer’s cheap fix would have failed, on engineering grounds. Call 8 he identifies a compressor booster fitted badly and a capacitor mounted loose rather than bracketed. Call 10 he generalises it carefully: when we see units out there that they reuse things, most of the time it’s like a hack job. Coaching implication: the technical content is accurate and valuable, and the framing is close to the line. The version on Call 5 — explain the physics, let the customer conclude — is the one to standardise.

Naming the pressure risk out loud

Call 18, after pushing for a next-day repair: the only reason I was pushing tomorrow is just so that way you can have air conditioner. It doesn’t mean that I don’t want you guys feeling pressured or anything like that either… the reason that we give you guys different options is because we don’t want you to feel like you’re cornered to only one option, followed by an explicit blessing of second opinions. Coaching implication: naming the pressure defuses it, and this is the healthiest handling of urgency anywhere in the week’s corpus.

Energy and comfort coaching that reduces the callback

Call 3 he corrects a thermostat habit with a reason: 85 is mainly for people that are like on vacation… what affects your power bill is gonna be how hard you run your system or how old your system is, because the older it gets, the less efficient it becomes. Where you were comfortable at 80 before, now you’re having to drop it to 78 because it’s losing capacity. Coaching implication: he turns a comfort complaint into evidence of declining efficiency, which is Option C groundwork delivered as helpfulness.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Build the options sheet on every call with a real fault, regardless of how the visit is going. Calls 8, 17, 19 and 23 all ended at the diagnosis. Call 23 is the one to replay: a customer declining $700 on a system losing refrigerant is the strongest Option C setup in his week.
  • Re-state Option C after the customer edits Section A. Call 9 dissolved into a $907 parts order with a cautious compressor and a $6,900 future still on the page. The edit is the budget signal, not the end of the conversation.
  • Stop pricing replacements. Call 18’s tier bands, zoning add-ons, discount mechanics and 10 grand off all belong to the Comfort Advisor. His own line from Call 5 is the right one on every call.
  • Add the expectations block to the front of the call. One sentence asking permission to report findings pre-authorises the options sheet that is already his strongest step.
  • Attach the Call 21 review ask to the signature page. It is the best review ask on the roster and it has been used once.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for five of the seven no-moves; Priority 3 protects the Comfort Advisor’s position on calls that are already flipping; Priorities 4 and 5 are cheap upside on calls going well.
