Bottom Line

Andrew hands off Option C on 50% of his scoreable calls (2 of 4) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 4), on the thinnest verifiable base of anyone on the roster this week — five of his nine recordings carry no ServiceTitan job number and cannot be scored either way. I’m not just going to do repairs on the system and give you guys a price — and his Assessment is anchored to the manufacturer’s red line on every single call. Strengths

1. Setting the rules of the visit before touching the equipment (Strong)

Call 2 he states the whole shape of the visit in one block, including the thing customers most fear: after that point, if there are any concerns to have and address, I’ll step in my van, I’ll have a written report for your system. That way we know exactly where your options are at. We’ll make sure to get your approval before we start any work. I’m not just going to do repairs on the system and give you guys a price. Call 4 he asks whether the time is convenient before he asks to come in. Call 8 he will not park until the homeowner has confirmed the spot. Coaching value: by the time he presents options the customer has already been told, in advance, exactly how the options will arrive and that nothing happens without their signature. This is the reason his objection handling later in the call is so calm — the ground rules were agreed before there was anything to argue about.

2. Grounding every claim in the manufacturer’s own red line (Strong)

He never says a part is failing without naming the number that says so, and he applies the same discipline when the news is good. Call 8: Today when the system turned on, running 122, nearly 123. Where should it be at? Anywhere below 113… That’s our red line. Call 4, on a system he decides is healthy: the reading red line is 109. Today we’re reading 108. So we’re just under that threshold. Call 2 he does it on a motor start device: there are two readings on here that give us a baseline of power of where that should be… this should be about 7.5 lowest… Right now we’re reading 6.8. He is also honest about the limit of the method: the only thing that we can’t determine is really, hey, you’re only going to have 2 months left (Call 1). Coaching value: a customer who has watched the meter cannot later feel sold to, which is exactly the ground a Comfort Advisor needs to walk onto.

3. Correctly declining to manufacture an upgrade where none exists (Strong)

Call 4 is worth studying because it is a no-move that reflects well on him. The microprocessors are healthy, the capacitors are good, and both readings sit just under the manufacturer’s threshold. He reports that accurately to his manager — this one’s more than likely not gonna flip only because I don’t really even have any immediates. I mean, they have two microprocessors. All their caps are good — and when pressed on the line about flipping it anyway, the honest answer is that it would take force. The customer’s own position was already settled: there’s nothing to do. Why trouble myself and think about it? Coaching value: under the manager’s doctrine a call with no upgrade potential that ends with no handoff is not a failure, and reading that correctly protects the credibility that makes the genuine flips land. This judgement should be affirmed, not corrected.

4. Absorbing a price objection without losing the room (Solid)

Call 6 the customer challenges him directly — why is the control board so expensive, Andrew? They’re like $200 online — and the answer neither retreats nor bristles: he explains the warranty that comes with the work, you’re also getting a 2-year warranty when it comes to our installation with that. So if anything should happen within the 2 years of us doing that repair, we’ll be right out here to make it right as rain, then walks through the company’s cost structure when the customer keeps pushing. Call 8 handles a comparison-shopping declaration the same way, without argument: the customer says let me shop this around and Andrew keeps building the report. Coaching value: both calls stayed open. Neither customer disengaged, and Call 8 still produced a turnover lead.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The big future number is named, and then nothing follows it (Moderate)

Call 7 is the clearest lost flip in the scoreable set. Multiple systems, compressors approaching their limits, condenser fan motors leaking oil, and a customer who asks the buying question outright: How much are those replacements for those guys? What follows is a repair total — across all the systems, as these compressors age out and they’re going to give way, on top of the condenser fan motors that are leaking oil, you’re definitely looking at anywhere from $6,000 to $8,000 in terms of repairs — and an acknowledgement that the customer needs to consult his wife. No specialist is offered, no appointment is proposed, and tgl_truth confirms no turnover lead was created off that job. The same shape appears on Call 5, where you’re already looking at over $6,000 is followed by a follow-up call offer rather than a specialist. What would have flipped it: a number in that range is the moment a Comfort Advisor becomes relevant, not the end of the technician’s remit. Call 1 shows he knows the sentence.

Gap 2 — Pressing after the customer has closed the door (Moderate)

On Call 4 the customer has already stated his position — there’s nothing to do. Why trouble myself and think about it? — and Andrew’s own assessment agrees there are no immediate repairs. He returns anyway with escalating figures: for what it’s worth, you’re $8,000 just to replace that blower up there… for both the compressor and the coil, you’re looking at over $8,000. Just over — the word is over. Could be $16,000. 20,000. The call still ends without a handoff. What would strengthen it: the correct move on a call with no immediates is the one he had already identified — there is no flip here — and stacking hypothetical totals afterwards spends the trust his red-line discipline built without changing the outcome. If an upgrade conversation is warranted at all on a healthy system, the route is a no-cost specialist visit, not a larger number.

Gap 3 — The specialist offer is not tied to any trigger (Moderate)

Across nine recordings the handoff appears twice as a fully executed close — Call 1, while we’re waiting for our friend to come on by so that way they can get you the quotes, I’ll just need a couple signatures from you, and Call 3, where a specialist visit is already arranged and the customer is planning for it (when your guy comes out, I’m just going to say, it’s really nice up there). On the four scoreable calls it is not named to the customer at all. There is no discernible rule governing when it happens, which is what separates his Decision step from Mario Castro’s on the same week. What would strengthen it: a stated trigger. The candidate that fits his own habits is the future-repair total — whenever he says a number above roughly the cost of a system, the next sentence names the specialist.

Gap 4 — No review ask anywhere in nine recordings, against the best close on the roster (Weak)

The close is otherwise complete: photos and estimates emailed, a business card with a named route back through the office, an explicit follow-up offer (Would you like me to follow up with you in some time just to see if you have any questions?), and the door held on the way out. Call 9’s customer volunteers the relationship openly — it’s always good to see you, but I hope we’re going to see you tomorrow — and Call 3’s customers are, by his own description, a good ray of sunshine. Not one recording contains a request for a review. What would strengthen it: he already asks permission for a follow-up call; the review ask belongs in the same breath.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Andrew’s scoreable base is four calls, so the pattern below is stated with that caveat. Across all nine recordings the front of the call is uniformly excellent and the variance sits entirely in what happens after the future numbers are read out.

  • Ready: Neutral. History is read on flips and no-moves alike; Call 8, a flip, and Call 7, a no-move, both open with a review of previous technicians’ findings.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong. The parking, purpose and approval block is present regardless of outcome.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 4, a no-move, contains the most careful reading in the week — two systems measured against their red lines and correctly cleared.
  • Decision: Where the variance lives, though not cleanly. The specialist is closed properly on Calls 1 and 3 and never named to the customer on the four scoreable calls; Call 7 substitutes a repair range for the offer.
  • Deliver: Neutral and consistently good. Documentation, follow-up offer and courtesy appear whichever way the call ended.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Andrew earns the right to open an upgrade conversation earlier than most technicians on this roster — the approval promise, the red-line discipline and the hand-drawn diagram do that work before any price is spoken. What he does not have is a rule for converting that credibility into a turnover lead. On Call 7 the customer asked the replacement question directly and received a repair total; on Call 4 he pressed for a flip that his own assessment said was not there. The gap is not confidence, technique or objection handling, all of which are present. It is that the specialist offer is currently improvised rather than triggered.

Beyond the Guide

Handling the online-price objection with the company’s cost structure

Challenged on Call 6 with they’re like $200 online, Andrew describes how the price is built — we run the 50/40/10 business model. 50% is for the cost of the board, and the labor for me to do it, 40% goes to overhead. So for the van, the gas, the insurance, all the people in the office, the dispatch, the managers — and repeats the same framing unprompted on Call 7. Observation: both customers stayed engaged and neither pursued the objection further. Recorded here as his standard response to that objection; whether the company wants its cost structure discussed at the door is a policy question for the manager, not a technician-level coaching point.

Repeating internal training talk to customers

Call 3 he tells the customer what the shop discusses in its meetings: we have meetings and trainings all the time at our shop, and they were telling us like, be careful about who you’re quoting for… Coaching implication: it is offered as candour and reads that way, but it hands the customer a frame in which quoting decisions are strategic rather than needs-based — which is the opposite of the impression the rest of his call builds. Worth retiring.

Open-mic conduct between calls

Several recordings capture extended technician-to-technician conversation on the property, including profanity and, on Call 8, a relayed account of a homeowner’s off-colour remarks. Call 9 is almost entirely a live troubleshooting conversation with a colleague, which is legitimate work but is recorded on a customer line. Coaching implication: low coaching value, non-trivial risk, and a habit change rather than a skill change.

Analogy quality worth borrowing

Two images in this week’s corpus are strong enough to standardise. Call 2 on an oil-leaking motor: it’s kind of like your engine, you know, if your engine was leaking oil and you go on a road trip, eventually if you have a leak, it’s going to stop, it’s going to seize up. Call 8 on why used refrigerant cannot be reused with a new compressor: it’s like you got a whole new heart but you use a different blood type. Coaching implication: both make an invisible consequence concrete in one sentence and are worth lifting into team training.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Attach the specialist offer to the future-repair total. Whenever the number he names approaches the cost of replacing the equipment — Call 5’s over $6,000, Call 7’s $6,000 to $8,000, Call 4’s over $8,000 — the next sentence should be Call 1’s: a no-cost specialist visit to give them the comparison. Call 7 is the whole cost of not having that rule.
  • Stop selling after the assessment says there is nothing to sell. Call 4 was read correctly the first time. Escalating hypothetical totals on a system he had just cleared is the one place in the week where his own credibility works against him.
  • Add the review ask to the follow-up offer. He already asks permission to call back; nine recordings contain no review request, on the most complete close on the roster.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priority 1 is the only one that changes an outcome; Priorities 2 and 3 protect and convert the trust the front of his calls already earns.
