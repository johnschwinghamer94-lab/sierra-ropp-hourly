Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Noah hands off Option C on 75% of his scoreable calls (6 of 8) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 8), which by the manager’s doctrine is the strongest flip week on this roster. What drives it is the best technical teaching in the group (Strong): he reads the manufacturer’s own data tag out loud and then shows the customer the number he is actually pulling — Call 8: the maximum amount that we’re supposed to pull is 134, and once we start to exceed that number, that’s when the rotor can get locked up on the compressor, and that’s what has happened. The customer is never asked to take his word for anything. His single biggest gap is that on the one call where the customer asked the buying question first, he answered it with a repair estimate instead of a fork — Call 6, facing a dead motor start device on both sides, burnt voltage-switch contacts and a scorching control board, the customer asks so how much it costs to correct all of this? and the answer is at least give you prices on what it would be to repair. No specialist is named, no upgrade conversation opens, and no turnover lead exists for that job. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: the sentence he already uses perfectly on Calls 8, 9 and 10 — I did give you the option to go ahead and speak to one of our equipment specialists — belongs on every call where the future-repair number is bigger than the immediate one, not only on the calls where the customer opens the door himself.

Strengths

1. Reading the data tag out loud before naming a failure (Strong)

This is the engine of Noah’s week and it is the reason his flips hold. He never asserts a part is bad; he shows the number it is rated for, the number it is drawing, and what happens in between. Call 4: this is the motor start device, so it’s rated for 45 for the compressor and 5 for the fan motor… those two numbers are allowed to rise or fall by 6%. And right now we’re way below that on both sides. Call 7: right now we are pulling 143.2, so the compressor is exceeding on the manufacturer specification for the power draw… RLA is running locked amps. So when this compressor is running, once it draws 24.4, that’s when the compressor will really start to lock up — and then, measured, we are right there at 23.001. Call 1 he does it with a temperature probe instead of a meter: I put my temperature probe out there on the cabinet where the sun hits where the inverter board is, and right now it’s 123 degrees on that cabinet… that’s probably why the inverter board got cooked up. Coaching value: by the time the options sheet comes out, the customer has already agreed with the diagnosis, which is why six of these eight calls produced a turnover lead.

2. Analogies that make a future failure feel present (Strong)

Noah’s Option C conversations are carried almost entirely by two images he repeats deliberately. On the rusted coil, Call 3: it’s like taking a cold water bottle, putting it outside when it’s hot outside, it starts to sweat. Over time, that water eats away at the coil and makes rust — and then why it cannot be fixed: with all the rust on the coil, it’s like trying to weld a dirty surface. So if it’s not successful, they stopped manufacturing these coils in January of 2025… we can’t get them. On the heat exchanger, Call 8: if you take a paperclip and you bend it back and forth, eventually it’ll snap, followed by the safety consequence stated flatly — there’s no difference between that, parking your car in the garage while it’s running and shutting the garage door. Coaching value: these are the sentences that convert a diagnosis into a reason to talk to somebody about a new system, and they are already word-perfect; they should be the backbone of the flip on every ageing R-22 and pre-2025 system he touches.

3. Sending the pricing question to the specialist instead of guessing at it (Strong)

Asked point-blank for replacement pricing, Noah consistently declines to answer and routes the customer to somebody whose job it is. Call 2, the customer presses — like really, what do you — like, and I know you can’t say, but like what is the ballpark? — and the answer is I’m just mainly repairs… I would just call Ryan and see if one of the guys can just come out. Call 9: if you guys are actually looking at replacing this unit, I’m not the guy who makes the calls on equipment pricing. I would have to get one of my equipment specialists out here. Call 8 he goes one step further and asks for the appointment: did you want to go ahead and, uh, at least get an estimate scheduled for like when sometime he could be here? That way one of our equipment specialists can go over everything with you. Coaching value: this is exactly the SILO behaviour — the tech is not the person who prices a replacement, and every time he holds that line the conversation moves toward a Comfort Advisor rather than toward a number he would have to defend. Nothing here needs changing; it needs repeating on the calls where he currently stops short.

4. Escalating to a manager on the call rather than guessing (Solid)

On Calls 2, 3, 8 and 9 he phones a manager mid-call with the readings in hand and the objections named before he goes back to the customer. Call 8: Compressor shot. It’s 10-year-old Goodman… outdoor, indoor motors leaked oil, voltage switch is burnt up, the coil’s rusted, and the heat exchanger has metal fatigue. Objections I’m looking at getting is he needs to speak to one of his family members to see how he’s going to pay for it, and then also it’s only 10 years old. Call 3 he does the same on a zone-board fault he had not seen before rather than replacing parts on a hunch. Coaching value: he arrives at the options conversation with the objection already anticipated, which is why his handoffs rarely stall once he opens them.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — When the customer asks the price question first, the answer is a repair estimate (Weak)

Call 6 is the clearest lost flip of his week and the only scoreable call where Option C is never mentioned at all. The system is a 2019 package unit whose warranty was never registered, the motor start device is completely dead on each side, the voltage-switch contacts are burnt, and the furnace control board is starting to burn up. The customer asks the buying question directly — so how much it costs to correct all of this? — and Noah answers with process: what I can do is at least give you prices on what it would be to repair. And then if, you know, at least let me finish writing my report. He repeats it twice. No future-versus-now comparison, no specialist named, no appointment offered, and tgl_truth confirms no turnover lead was ever created off that job. What would have flipped it: the customer had already asked to be sold something. The three-part structure he uses on Calls 8, 9 and 10 — immediate number, larger future number, specialist — needed only to be started rather than deferred to a report.

Gap 2 — Handing the customer two numbers and asking them to choose (Moderate)

On Call 1 Noah lays out a $2,100 inverter board and a $3,499 fan motor that has already leaked its oil, adds a rusting coil whose replacement no longer exists, and then closes with So what do you want me to do then? He follows it with an explicit refusal to steer: I’m not telling you to do one thing or one or the other. Because at the end of the day… you guys are the ones that live here. I can’t make decisions for you. Call 5 carries the same neutrality — my thing is, like, I’ll never tell you what you need to do — against an immediate repair of $6,284.75 and a compressor-plus-refrigerant future of $9,760. Both calls did eventually produce a turnover lead, so the neutrality is not fatal; it does mean the flip depends on the customer doing the arithmetic themselves. What would strengthen it: neutrality about which option to buy is correct, but the recommendation to speak to somebody is not a sale and does not need hedging. I can’t make decisions for you should be followed by what I can do is get our specialist out here at no cost so you have both numbers in front of you.

Gap 3 — The estimate walk-off breaks the moment the options land (Moderate)

On Calls 3, 6, 8 and 9 the options conversation is interrupted by Noah leaving to write the report — what I’m gonna do is go out to my van and just draw up some estimates (Call 3), I’m gonna go into my truck and type everything up (Call 6), I’m gonna go ahead and go to my van and just write my report (Call 9). On Call 6 the call never recovers. On Call 8 it does, but only because the customer volunteers the upgrade question himself while Noah is still in the room — So you’re looking at $15,000, $16,000 just in repairs. What’s a new package unit cost? What would strengthen it: the Option C sentence costs ten seconds and does not require the estimate to be written first. Naming the specialist before he walks out to the van removes the risk that the momentum is gone when he walks back in.

Gap 4 — No review ask anywhere in ten recordings (Weak)

Noah hands out a branded gift bag on at least three calls with an explicit line about being remembered — Call 7: this is for you guys… nail files, teaspoon, tablespoon, and a refrigerator magnet. That way you guys can hang my picture up on the refrigerator — and customers respond warmly, including Call 5’s It was a pleasure to meet you and a colleague’s Noah, you did good. Not one of the ten recordings contains a request for a Google review. What would strengthen it: the gift already creates the pause the ask needs; the sentence is missing, not the goodwill.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Across the eight scoreable calls the split is unusually clean, because Noah’s diagnostic work is near-identical on every call. What differs is whether the upgrade conversation is opened by him or waited for.

  • Ready: Present on both outcomes — warranty and history are checked on flips and no-moves alike, so this is not what separates them.
  • Welcome: Present on both outcomes where he opens the call; absent on the recordings he joins mid-visit, which correlates with neither result.
  • Assessment: Present on both outcomes and consistently Strong. Call 6, a no-move, contains as good a teardown as any flip in the week.
  • Decision: This is the whole difference. On all six flips the specialist is named out loud and the future number is set against the immediate one. On Call 6 neither happens; on Call 8 it happens, and the customer declines for a reason outside the process — I own a plumbing company and I have AC guys that I do stuff on the side for me.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The gift, the return window and the absent review ask look the same whichever way the call ends.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Every one of Noah’s eight scoreable calls contained a system old enough or degraded enough to justify an upgrade conversation, and his Assessment earns him the right to open one on all eight. He opened it on seven and won six. The one call where he did not open it — and where the customer had already asked the price question for him — is the only call in the week with no turnover lead and no repair. The gap is not knowledge, credibility or objection handling; it is that the specialist sentence is currently triggered by the customer raising replacement rather than by Noah finishing his diagnosis.

Beyond the Guide

Open-mic conduct inside the customer’s home

Several recordings capture extended technician-to-technician conversation, with heavy profanity, while inside or immediately outside a customer’s property — unrelated talk about ticket prices and travel on Call 2, an aside on Call 3 before re-entering the home, and on Call 8 a manager’s remark about the customer that Noah repeats back on the line (Said tell this guy he’s a bozo) moments before returning to that customer to present options. Coaching implication: nothing in these exchanges reached a customer’s ear in these recordings, but all of it is on a recorded line during a paid visit. This is the one item in the week that carries risk disproportionate to its coaching value, and it is a five-second habit change.

He explains the January 2025 refrigerant change better than anyone on the roster

The R-22 and pre-2025 equipment story appears on Calls 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8 and 10 in near-identical form: they stopped manufacturing the equipment for this refrigerant in January of 2025. So when that coil leaks, unfortunately it’s game over. You would at least have to replace the outdoor unit and the indoor coil. On Call 5 he adds the pricing consequence honestly — the refrigerant is the most expensive — and on Call 8 he explains what the customer gets on the other side: all the new systems, everything’s aluminum, so they never rust. Because that was one of the biggest complaints that we get. Coaching implication: this is a transferable asset. It is the single most reliable Option C opener in this week’s corpus and should be lifted into team training verbatim.

Energy-cost framing that nobody else uses

On Calls 3, 7, 8 and 10 he converts age and condition into an ongoing number the customer is already paying: I compared the manufacturer rating for the energy consumption versus what we use today… you guys are paying about 11% over just to run this air conditioner (Call 3), rising to 41% on Call 7 and 25% on Call 10. Coaching implication: it reframes the decision from a repair bill to a running cost, which is a Comfort Advisor’s argument being made for free before the Comfort Advisor arrives. Worth standardising, provided the percentage is sourced the same way every time.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Open Option C at the end of the diagnosis, not when the customer raises it. The trigger should be mechanical: whenever the future-repair number is larger than the immediate one, the next sentence is the specialist sentence. Call 6 is the whole cost of not doing this — a dead motor start device, burnt contacts, a burning control board and an unregistered warranty produced no turnover lead because the conversation stopped at prices on what it would be to repair.
  • Ask for the appointment the way he already asked for it once. Call 8’s did you want to go ahead and at least get an estimate scheduled for like when sometime he could be here? is the strongest handoff language in this week’s corpus and it appears on exactly one call. Naming a time turns an offer into a booking.
  • Add the review ask to the gift handoff. The gift bag already creates the moment on Calls 5, 7 and 8 and the goodwill is audible; ten recordings contain no ask. Attaching one sentence to a habit he already has is the cheapest win available to him.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priority 1 is the only one that changes an outcome; Priorities 2 and 3 change the rate at which good outcomes convert.
