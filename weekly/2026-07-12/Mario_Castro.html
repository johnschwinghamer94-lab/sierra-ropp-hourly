Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Mario hands off Option C on 64% of his scoreable calls (9 of 14) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 14), and he is the only technician in this week’s corpus who says the words Option C out loud to the customer — Call 5: it’s a lot of money, so I did give you that option, Option C, to speak to an install specialist. That way you can compare repair pricing to replacement and see what makes the most sense to you. His teaching device is a body (Strong): the compressor is a heart, the blower motor is the lungs, a clogged filter is trying to run with your nose plugged in. His single biggest gap is that the offer stops appearing on precisely the calls where the customer has already asked for it — Call 14, the customer’s representative instructs him directly, here’s what you need to do. You need to give 2 options. One, what it’s going to cost to get it up and going… And failing that… a whole new unit, and against a completely failed motor start device and a compressor reading caution no specialist is named and no turnover lead exists for that job. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: the two sentences he already owns — that’s not my department, we would have to get someone out here and coming out here is no cost and no commitment — belong on every call where the customer says a price word, not only on the calls where he is already presenting an options sheet.

Strengths

1. Naming Option C to the customer, out loud, as a named thing (Strong)

Most technicians describe the handoff. Mario labels it, which turns a vague offer into a defined product the customer can accept. Call 5: I did put those options there for you, uh, for immediate and for future. What we seeing coming down the line. All right, it’s a lot of money, so I did give you that option, Option C, to speak to an install specialist. The effect is visible on Call 6, where the customer picks the label back up unprompted — your company didn’t do that? The Option C? — and Mario closes on it immediately: Yes, we can get someone out here right now and then they’ll be able to talk to you about it and give you all your options. Coaching value: a named option gets asked about later; an unnamed one gets forgotten in the truck. This is the single most transferable habit in his week.

2. Removing the cost of saying yes before the customer can object (Strong)

The phrase no cost, no commitment is attached to the handoff on Call 6 (I will say the information they give you, them coming out here is no cost, no commitment. You just get all your information), Call 12 (coming out here and getting all your options is at no charge either… just to get all your information) and Call 17 (coming out here is no cost and no commitment. You just be comparing all your information). He pairs it with same-day availability — we can always get someone out here today (Call 12) — and on Call 6 he asks for the calendar directly: So is there a day you want me to reach out? Coaching value: he pre-empts the two objections a turnover appointment actually attracts, which is why nine of these calls produced a lead.

3. Refusing the replacement price and routing it, in the customer’s own moment (Strong)

Asked for a replacement number, Mario declines cleanly and says why. Call 5, immediately after presenting $7,800 of repairs, the customer asks How much do you think that’s gonna cost? and the answer is That’s a good question for them, uh, just because I don’t — it’s not my department and I don’t want to, you know, cross that line and get in their territory. Um, I just do the repairs. Call 12, the customer pushes on price and he answers that’s not my department. We would have to get someone out here. We can always get someone out here today. That way they can talk to you about, um, those prices, and they tailor everything to you. Coaching value: this is exactly right under the SILO process — the technician does not price a replacement, and every time he holds the line the conversation moves to a Comfort Advisor instead of to a number he would have to defend. Nothing here should change.

4. One analogy set, used consistently, that customers can repeat back (Strong)

The body metaphor runs the entire week and it is coherent rather than decorative. Heart on Calls 6 and 8 — this is your compressor, which works like the heart… the higher the blood pressure, probably heart attack. Lungs on Calls 8, 13 and 18 — this is your lungs. This is how the unit breathes, grabs all the air… when it starts getting dirty, starts causing the motor to start working harder. Filters as breathing on Call 5. On Call 12 he switches register for a customer who builds vehicles: do you know what LRA is? Is it similar to vehicles? Locked rotor amperage… It’s like the Jeep when it has the torque when it starts. Coaching value: a customer who can restate the diagnosis to a spouse is a customer who can defend the appointment after the technician leaves.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — When the customer asks for the two options, the offer disappears (Weak)

Call 14 is the clearest loss of his week. The motor start device has completely failed, the compressor is reading caution right now, the coil is starting to rust, especially at the bottom, and the customer’s representative gives him the instruction verbatim: here’s what you need to do. You need to give 2 options. One, what it’s going to cost to get it up and going. If it needs a new compressor, new compressor and components. And failing that, you know, he said a whole new, a whole new unit. Well, yeah, then you got to give him a price for that. Mario answers with process — These components, whatever route he wants to go, um, we’ll, we’ll see what he wants to do — and leaves to write it up. No specialist is named, no appointment is offered, and tgl_truth confirms no turnover lead was created off that job. What would have flipped it: the customer had asked for the replacement conversation in plain words. The two sentences he uses perfectly on Calls 5 and 12 needed only to be said.

Gap 2 — Fielding the replacement price himself instead of handing it over (Moderate)

Call 16 is the one call where the discipline he shows on Calls 5 and 12 breaks. The motor start device is out of range on both sides — we’re at 63… that fan is rated for 7.5, it’s reading 0.5 — and the blower motor is not in good condition. The customer asks about replacement cost and instead of routing it he answers it: I’m not trying to scare you with prices or anything like that. The compressor itself now ranges between like $5,500 to like $7,500. The call ends at give me 20 minutes, 30 minutes, and I’ll put everything step by step with no specialist offered, and no lead exists. The same pattern is visible on Call 9, where How much overall does that cost, the whole thing? goes unanswered by a handoff, and on Call 17, where he volunteers manufacturer pricing history (they try to increase everything 15-20% every year) before reaching the offer. What would strengthen it: the price question is the buying signal, not an information request. Answering it with a range spends the moment; answering it with that’s not my department, and I can have someone here today at no cost converts it.

Gap 3 — Speculating about what the customer will decide instead of making the offer (Moderate)

Call 4 is the largest upgrade opportunity in his week and it produced nothing. Four indoor units, and by his own description all the coils are looking extremely rusted, on R-22 equipment he correctly identifies as unsupportable: if it’s a refrigerant and it’s a leak, they don’t make parts for these units anymore. It’s R-22. They phased it out in early 2000. Instead of an offer, he narrates the customer’s likely decision: I don’t know what your son’s gonna want to do. Um, he’s gonna just take a lower bid and have the other buyer replace that unit, or wait, I don’t know. What would strengthen it: four dead-refrigerant coils on one property is the strongest Option C setup available to anyone on this roster in this week. Guessing at the decision-maker’s intentions is a substitute for putting the specialist in front of them.

Gap 4 — No review ask anywhere in eighteen recordings, against unusually warm calls (Weak)

The goodwill is plainly there — Call 8 the customer offers him water twice and volunteers it’s gonna cost a little money, but… oh yeah, it’s your future; Call 12 he waives the visit charge outright, today’s a courtesy visit, okay, no charge. Not one recording contains a request for a Google review, and Call 14’s recording captures an obscenity after the customer steps away. What would strengthen it: the courtesy-visit line already creates the pause the ask needs, and the customers who received it are the most likely on his week to say yes.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Mario’s diagnostic work is close to identical across the fourteen scoreable calls — rated number, measured number, body analogy — so the separation is not technical. It is whether the offer is made before he leaves to write the estimate.

  • Ready: Neutral. Preparation is comparable on flips and no-moves; Call 8, a flip, and Call 13, a no-move, both open with a full reconstruction of the property’s history.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently strong. The parking question and the permission-to-report block appear on both outcomes.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Calls 14 and 16, both no-moves, contain diagnoses as complete as any flip in the week, including a failed motor start device measured on both sides.
  • Decision: The entire difference. On all nine flips the specialist is named, framed as free and often offered same-day. On Calls 4, 9, 14 and 16 the customer raised replacement or price first and no offer followed.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The tidy close and the missing review ask look the same whichever way the call went.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. On four of his five no-moves the customer opened the replacement conversation themselves — a direct instruction to present two options (Call 14), a price question (Calls 9 and 16), and a property with four dead-refrigerant coils and a decision-maker in the wings (Call 4). Mario has the strongest handoff vocabulary on this roster and did not use it on any of them. What separates his wins from his losses is not skill, confidence or diagnosis quality; it is that the offer is currently triggered by him reaching the options sheet, rather than by the customer saying a price word.

Beyond the Guide

He rebuts a competitor’s misdiagnosis without attacking the competitor

Call 8 the customer had been told by another company that she needed entirely new ductwork. Mario finds the actual cause and states it plainly — the ductwork that they were saying that you needed new one, it’s because they ripped a hole in the ductwork — alongside they left your coil open, so you were also paying to cool your attic up, and he never names or disparages the other firm. Coaching implication: this is the highest-trust moment in his week and it produced a flip. It is also the exact circumstance in which a review ask would land hardest.

Brand comparisons offered on his own initiative

Call 6 he closes on equipment preference before a specialist is scheduled — we focus on Lennox. It’s one of those units that lasts longer… If you want something similar to what you have right now, we do have Goodman too — and Call 18 he compares manufacturers unprompted: we usually see a little bit more problems with these train units… for efficiency, quiet, the Lennox, those help out a lot, especially with utility bills. Trains, they’re more sturdy. Coaching implication: it builds credibility, and it also spends the Comfort Advisor’s opening material and risks committing Sierra to a brand position before anyone has sized the job. Worth a conversation about where the technician’s remit ends.

Safety framing that is accurate and appropriately blunt

Call 9 on a rusting heat exchanger: Not if it cracks, but when it cracks, carbon monoxide can get pushed inside your home… home warranty won’t cover any of this until one, it’s cracked. Call 5 on a frozen coil, he traces the water damage path before it happens: when this thaws out, it could potentially overflow that pan underneath, causing it to spill onto the ceiling. Coaching implication: both are legitimate, evidence-backed and stated without theatre. They are also, on those two calls, the strongest available reason to bring a specialist in and were not used that way.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Treat any price word from the customer as the trigger for the handoff. Calls 9, 14 and 16 all contain the customer raising cost or replacement, and none produced a turnover lead. The response is already written — that’s not my department, we would have to get someone out here plus no cost, no commitment — it simply needs to fire on the question rather than on the options sheet.
  • Make the offer before leaving to write the estimate. On Calls 14 and 16 the last thing the customer hears is that paperwork is coming. Naming the specialist takes ten seconds and does not require the estimate to exist yet.
  • Attach a review ask to the courtesy-visit line. Eighteen recordings, no ask, on a week that includes a waived charge, a competitor correction and repeated offers of water. The moment already exists on Calls 8 and 12.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priority 1 accounts for four of the five no-moves in the week; Priorities 2 and 3 change how reliably good calls convert.
