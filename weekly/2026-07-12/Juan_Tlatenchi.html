Bottom Line

They should be out here in the next 45 minutes to an hour — and then files the recording in a way no report can read. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: attach the job number to every recording before the visit starts, because on current evidence he is one of the strongest diagnosticians in the window and the company's own data says he did nothing.

Strengths

1. He proves the failure with a measurement before he names a part (Strong)

Call 2's compressor is not bad, it is out of range against three separate readings quoted to the customer: it's drawing 74 on startup… the manufacturer gives us a maximum amount that it can draw. You can see there, it's 73 max, then the capacitor at 0 against a rating of 35.5, then the winding resistance across all three pins. Call 3 does the same and Call 1 adds a temperature: I took an internal temperature of the motor. You can see it's running at 200 degrees… typical range we like to see them is between 100 and 115. Coaching value: customers cannot argue with a number against a data plate, and this is why his option sheets get read rather than negotiated.

2. He offers Option C on the no-moves too, not just the winners (Strong)

Call 6's customer declines the appointment and still hears the whole offer: Do you want me to set an appointment so you can at least consider all the options, and then that way you have that number and see what it would cost? Call 10's customer intends to call the office himself and Juan still says being the last second that I'm being here, I can call someone out and work with your schedule. Call 7 raises it off the customer's own Costco remark: we actually work with Costco… if you guys are interested in getting pricing for equipment, we have a guy that can help. Coaching value: this is why Decision bands Strong rather than Strong on wins — the offer does not disappear when a call is going badly, which is the failure mode on most of this roster.

3. He books the specialist same-day and prepares the house for them (Strong)

Five calls end with an advisor dispatched rather than promised. Call 1: They can be out here in the next 30 minutes… he's going to send someone. They should be out here in the next 45 minutes to an hour. Call 5 lands a specific slot — I texted them that you guys would like an appointment Thursday, 8 AM — and Call 13 leaves the ladder in place for the advisor: He should be here any minute. I'm gonna leave my ladder so that way he can go up there because he'll have to look. Coaching value: leaving the ladder up is the guide's handoff standard executed without being told, and it is worth naming because it is the difference between a lead and an appointment.

4. He tells customers the repair might not fix it, before they spend the money (Strong)

Call 2's customer asks the obvious question — as long as we change the capacitor, whatever you're talking about, it'll work? — and gets an honest no: It might not, because it's already exceeding that max rating at startup… if it doesn't work, you'll be out the $1,784. Call 3 repeats it: there is no guarantee because I can't run the system… you spend the $1,784, there's no way to get that back. Coaching value: both of those calls flipped, and the reason is that the customer trusted a person who had just talked them out of the easy sale.

Call 2's customer asks directly whether anything needs replacing and gets the question returned: I mean, a whole new unit, that really comes down to your decision. Impact: the flip on Call 2 arrived late and as an appointment for the next morning, on a two-hour call where it could have been on the doorstep in thirty minutes.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether Juan made the office call while he was still standing in the room: on Calls 1, 3, 5 and 13 a specialist is dispatched, named or already arriving, and on Calls 6, 7 and 10 the same offer is made and then left with the customer to action themselves.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — his door-step questioning is identical on the flips and the no-moves. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides. The approval statement is on every call either way, and the missing Agenda Card boxes are missing on the flips too.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides — Call 10 is his longest and most detailed diagnosis of the week at 10,442 words and it produced nothing. The teaching has never been the variable.
  • Decision: Strong versus Strong — and this is the unusual part. The offer is present on both sides; what differs is whether he dialled. Call 3's I can have someone out here in the next 30 minutes against Call 10's I'll call them because I got to call them anyways — the customer taking the action.
  • Deliver: Moderate on both sides. No review is asked for on any call, flip or no-move.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same three-pin winding test, same drawing on the same pad — but two endings, and the fork is whether the phone came out before the customer answered. On Call 1 he does not wait to be asked: They can be out here in the next 30 minutes, then he's going to send someone. They should be out here in the next 45 minutes to an hour. On Call 3 he works the customer's post-office deadline into a 3:30 slot and gets the signature. On Call 5 he texts the manager from the kitchen and leaves with Thursday at 8. On Call 10, after ten thousand words and a full second-opinion diagnosis, the close is the customer's — I'll call them because I got to call them anyways — and nothing was booked. On Call 6 the offer is made properly and declined, and a $39 diagnostic fee is collected instead. On Call 7 it ends on we'll see what they say about a home warranty. The variable is not whether Option C was offered — it was offered on every single one of these. It is whether Juan picked up the phone while the customer was still in front of him.

Beyond the Guide

Six weeks in the door and already the deepest diagnostician in the window

Call 2 puts his tenure on record — A month and a half now. Yeah, I've been doing it for 8 years, but I gave up on the previous company I was on and looking for something a little better — and the same week he is running three-pin winding resistance, locked-rotor amp comparisons, capacitor microfarad checks against rating, motor-temperature-against-ambient and refrigerant pressure-to-temperature conversions, and teaching all of it. Coaching implication: the coaching he needs is administrative and closing-mechanical, not technical, and treating it as a new-hire ramp would miss that entirely.

He explains the refrigerant history well enough that customers finish his sentences

Call 1: this was phased out in 2010, and then completely banned in 2020. All production was banned, and that was replaced by R-410A, which was banned in 2025. And so now we're using R454B. So you're behind 2 refrigerants. Call 3's customer picks it up unprompted — it's like super expensive now, right? — and Call 2's customer supplies the year himself. Coaching implication: the two-generations-behind framing does more to open the replacement conversation than any price does, and it belongs in the team's shared language.

His self-talk between calls is about revenue, not the customer

Call 1, while writing the report in the van: $128,000 in revenue. Holy s***. Call 3, on the phone to a colleague between rooms: What I tell you, back at it like a crack addict… First one, I just left my first one, flipped and sold. Both are audible on customer job recordings. Coaching implication: these are harmless in themselves and they show he is tracking the flip as the win, which is exactly right — but on the same recorder that captured Call 12, and the coaching point is that the recorder is always on and the file belongs to the customer.

Twelve of thirteen visits run past an hour, and five past two

Call 7 runs 170 minutes, Call 13 100, Call 6 146, Call 5 125 and Call 10 109 — on a week where five recordings are demand breakdowns. Call 10's customer names it at the end: I gotta pay for too much other s***. Coaching implication: the depth is genuinely his differentiator, but with no time-constraint question asked on any call he has no read on which customers can afford the visit he is giving them.
