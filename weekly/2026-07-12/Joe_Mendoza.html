Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Joe hands off Option C on 38% of his scoreable calls (5 of 13) and wins repairs on 23% (3 of 13), and his defining strength is the way he frames the specialist visit as the customer's right to refuse — Call 11: I'm a consumer of options. That's how I do my purchases… I want to know what's available to me so I have the ability to say no. And that's what Option C provides you guys — the opportunity to say no, or the opportunity to say, hey, this makes sense to us. His single biggest gap is that the advisor appointment only gets booked when he books it in the room (Strong on wins) — on Call 11 he signs the customer up and then refuses to leave, I'm staying here till they get here… I'll be in my van, and on Call 13 he makes the identical case and then hands the booking to a future phone call: I'll give you a call on Thursday, so we can get something on the books for you. That call produced no lead. The pattern is that his Decision step is complete and near-identical on the calls that flip and the calls that do not — the same option sheet, the same second number, the same information framing — and what changes is whether a specialist is physically dispatched before he packs the ladder. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: treat the Option C conversation as unfinished until an advisor is either in the house or en route, the way he already does on Calls 11 and 14.

Strengths

1. Selling Option C as permission to refuse it (Strong)

Joe's version of the system-update conversation never asks for a decision, which is exactly why customers accept it. Call 11: I'm a consumer of options… I want to know what's available to me so I have the ability to say no. Call 5: Today's visit is just information… you guys don't have to spend a single dime with me today. You guys don't have to put any money out of pocket today. Call 13: that's what this option keeps your money in your pocket because it's just a conversation. All three sit on calls where the customer had already told him money was tight. Coaching value: this framing is the reason five of his thirteen calls produced a lead, and it costs him nothing to deploy — it should be the first thing he protects.

2. Quoting the cost of the next failure, not just today's (Strong)

He never presents an immediate repair without immediately pricing what is queued behind it, and that second number is what makes Option C reasonable rather than pushy. Call 7: with $2,751 now, potentially $8,300 coming down the line, I would hate for you to be $2,000, $3,000, $4,000 into the system trying to keep it alive and band-aiding it. Call 11: the compressor is drawing in 23.1% more power than what it's rated for. These additional repairs will cost you in excess of $8,500. Call 10: $8,600 into a 13-year-old system is a big gamble, which is why I [have] this option on here for you. Coaching value: this is a repeatable structure other techs on the roster do not have, and it belongs in the team's Decision-step drill.

3. Holding the line on replacement pricing and sending it to the specialist (Strong)

Asked directly for a replacement number on four separate calls, Joe declines every time and routes it where it belongs. Call 2: I don't know because I'm— that's not my department. My job is to fix what you guys currently have. Call 4: I'm your fix-it guy. If you guys want to have a conversation about what it's going to cost to replace it, I'm going to have someone else come out. Call 11, pressed again: I don't want to give you a wrong number. Let's wait for them to get here. None of these customers disengaged; three of the four became leads. Coaching value: this is the correct SILO move executed under direct pressure, and it is worth showing the rest of the roster as the model answer.

4. Refusing work he does not believe in (Strong on wins)

Call 5, on a system he had just finished evaluating: I'm a fix-it guy. I'm a tinkerer. I get paid to fix equipment. That's how I get paid. But I also have morals, and I wouldn't feel right taking any of your money to fix what you guys currently have because it's so bad. Call 4 carries the same instinct in the other direction — he tells a customer being pushed toward a cheap refrigerant charge that cheaper isn't always better and explains why the drop-in doesn't work at the same pressures and it's going to ruin that compressor. Both calls flipped. Coaching value: the credibility he buys here is what makes the Option C ask land as advice rather than a pitch.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The advisor gets booked only when he books it in the room (Strong on wins)

Call 11 and Call 13 are the same conversation with two different endings. On Call 11 he signs the customer up on the spot — Sign for the equipment specialist to speak with a comfort advisor. Doesn't cost you anything… Total is zero — and then physically waits: I'm staying here till they get here… I'll be in my van. On Call 13, having made an equally complete case, he leaves the booking to himself later: let me make a phone call… it would be this Sunday if you wanted that appointment, but I'll give you a call on Thursday, so we can get something on the books for you. No lead was ever created on that job. Impact: the difference between his flips and three of his five no-moves is not the pitch, it is whether an appointment existed before he left the driveway.

Gap 2 — Offering the customer an exit inside the Option C question (Strong on wins)

On the calls that flip he states Option C and moves to the signature. On Call 9 he instead builds the escape hatch into the sentence: option C, speaking with an equipment specialist. Do you want to talk to somebody just to see what that would look like, or you want to leave it alone for now? The customer left it alone, and the call ends with him telling them to just turn it off… turn it on tonight and see if it works. Nothing resembling or you want to leave it alone for now appears anywhere on Calls 4, 5, 7, 11 or 14. Impact: the phrasing costs him leads on exactly the calls where he has already done the diagnostic work to earn one.

Gap 3 — Taking the first no as the final answer (Moderate)

Call 6 is the most complete Option C presentation in his week — he delivers it twice, once in English to the son and once in Spanish to the mother: en vez de empezar a ponerle dinero a una unidad que ya está por fallar, prefieren tomar la decisión de platicar con el especialista de equipos. He then offers a same-week slot, I can get someone out here tomorrow for you guys to at least give you guys the information. The answer is Not at the time, and he moves straight to the filter transaction and the signature without a second route. Impact: a call with a fourteen-year-old system and an $8,900 repair horizon closed as a $180 filter sale.

Gap 4 — The five-star seed is never planted and the review is asked for once (Weak)

Across fourteen recordings the Welcome block covers parking, permission, purpose, evaluation and approval, but never the five-star line — Call 13's version ends at we don't do any work without your approval and stops. The single review ask in the week is on Call 5, unseeded and delivered as a joke: Google review goes a long way for me. You guys can go on there and say, hey, Joe was terrible. I really would appreciate that. Impact: on Calls 2, 7 and 14 he had customers volunteering praise at the door, and none of them were asked for the review the guide has him seeding an hour earlier.

Gap 5 — Rapport that displaces the evaluation rather than setting it up (Moderate)

Joe's rapport is genuinely exceptional and on most calls it earns him the room. On Call 1 it consumes the call: the entire recording is a conversation about the customer's mother's cancer, a custody hearing and a credit rebuild, and the only technical content is the closing line let me get started on that outdoor unit… let me take a look at the refrigerant and everything in that, then I'll hop upstairs. No finding, no option sheet, no outcome. Call 2 shows the balanced version — the Chargers and the cruise sit around a full diagnosis. Impact: this is the one recording of fourteen with no assessable Decision step, and the difference from Call 2 is where the rapport sat, not how much of it there was.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference between Joe's flips and his no-moves is whether a Comfort Advisor was dispatched before he left: on Calls 11 and 14 an advisor was signed for and en route while he was still in the house, and on Calls 9, 10 and 13 an identical option sheet was left behind with the booking postponed to a phone call that never produced a lead.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides. He arrives prepared on repeat visits — Call 10's we got the rusted coil, things of that nature, the hot spots on the control board — and that preparation is just as present on the no-moves as on the flips, so it does not discriminate.
  • Welcome: Strong on both sides. The parking-permission-name-purpose block is near-identical on Calls 9 and 13 (no-moves) and Calls 11 and 14 (flips); the only thing missing everywhere is the five-star seed.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides. Call 9's motor walkthrough — it's a bushing that's running dry… inevitably it'll start to wobble, and then you'll just hear a grinding noise coming from upstairs — is as good as anything on his flips and produced no move at all.
  • Decision: Strong on the flips, Strong on the no-moves too — and that is the finding. The option sheet, the second number and the information framing are all present on Calls 9, 10 and 13; what is absent is the booking.
  • Deliver: Strong on wins, absent otherwise. On Call 11 he stays in the van until the advisor knocks; on Call 9 he leaves the customer instructions to turn it on tonight and see if it works.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same option sheet, same second number — and two different endings, separated by a signature. On Call 11 he asks for it plainly, Sign for the equipment specialist to speak with a comfort advisor… Total is zero, and then removes every reason to change their mind by refusing to leave: I'm staying here till they get here. On Call 14 he does it before the customer can hesitate — we have Jeremy on the way. He's the equipment specialist. We're definitely going to take care of you — and hands over with I leave you guys in great hands. On Call 13 the identical case ends with I'll give you a call on Thursday, and on Call 9 it ends with or you want to leave it alone for now? Neither produced a lead. The variable is not whether Option C was explained — it is explained beautifully on every one of them — it is whether an advisor had a time and a signature before he packed the ladder.

Beyond the Guide

He runs the entire Decision step in Spanish when that is the customer's language

Call 6 is delivered twice over: the son gets the control-board explanation in English, and the mother gets the full options walk in Spanish, including the Option C case — en vez de empezar a ponerle dinero a una unidad que ya está por fallar, prefieren tomar la decisión de platicar con el especialista de equipos, ver más o menos… — and the electrical-bill argument built from her own numbers: estamos hablando que la diferencia de $200 entre el invierno y el verano es el aire acondicionado. Coaching implication: this is a roster-level asset that is currently only used when Joe happens to be dispatched to the home; it is worth routing Spanish-speaking Option C candidates to him deliberately.

He priced a replacement himself rather than make the customer wait

Call 4, told the next advisor slot was 5 PM: I have my tablet in the truck, I can measure it up real quick, give you a price on it. That way you guys don't have to wait all day. The call flipped, so the instinct was right — but it is the one moment in the week where he steps across the line he defends everywhere else. Coaching implication: the urgency is correct and the workaround is not; the same instinct pointed at getting an advisor moved earlier is worth more than a tablet estimate.

He takes reputational hits without defending the company

Call 2's customers tell him to his face, your reputation around the neighborhood is not good, and later, we've had hurt, not good. He neither argues nor apologises for Sierra; he reframes it as staffing — you can go to a car dealership and have a great experience, or you can have a terrible experience. And it's upper management's and our owner's jobs to filter them out — and closes with I hope I've given you a good experience so far here today. That call ended in an approved repair and a membership. Coaching implication: this is the cleanest objection-handling on the roster this week and should be recorded as the model response to a reputation objection.

He gives away small work to buy trust

Call 4, on ductwork he had just criticised: if you guys just wanted me to reseal that, I can reseal it. It won't look pretty… I wouldn't charge you guys to do that. Call 3 he changes two filters a customer cannot reach and disposes of them. Call 9 he diagnoses a frozen coil and sends the customer away with a free instruction rather than a charge. Coaching implication: each of these is a Future-repair line item being converted into goodwill; the goodwill is real, and at least the filter cabinet belongs on the option sheet as a B item.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Do not leave a call with upgrade potential without an advisor booked. On Call 13 he makes the complete case and then says I'll give you a call on Thursday, so we can get something on the books for you — no lead was created. On Call 11 he asks for the signature in the room, Total is zero, and waits in the van until the advisor arrives. The Call 11 sequence, applied to Calls 9, 10 and 13, is the whole difference between his week and a much better one.
  • Cut the exit out of the Option C question. Call 9's Do you want to talk to somebody just to see what that would look like, or you want to leave it alone for now? invites the answer he got. His own better version already exists on Call 11 — state what Option C is, state that it costs nothing, and go to the signature — and it appears on every call that flipped.
  • Plant the five-star seed in Welcome and harvest it at the door. The Welcome block is otherwise complete on nine of fourteen recordings and stops at we don't do any work without your approval. Adding the seed there gives him something to collect on Calls 2, 7 and 14, where customers were already volunteering praise and were never asked.

Trimming the opening rapport is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — on Call 2 the same volume of personal conversation sits around a full diagnosis and an approved repair, so the problem on Call 1 was where the rapport sat rather than how much of it there was, whereas the unbooked advisor is the whole story of his week.

Then you finished with I'll give you a call on Thursday, so we can get something on the books for you. No lead was ever created on that job. Same you, same pitch, two endings — the only difference is that on Call 11 somebody was dispatched before you left."
  • Contrast two calls: Put Call 11 (Lam) next to Call 9 (Butcher) and have Joe describe what was different. Both were systems around a decade old with a large repair queued behind the immediate one, and both got a full option sheet. Let him find the sentence — or you want to leave it alone for now? — himself.
  • Drill booking the advisor in the room (Priority 1) and cutting the exit clause out of the Option C ask (Priority 2). Role-play three scenarios from his own week: the customer who wants a replacement number from him (what does that whole unit cost typically, Call 2), the customer who says the advisor visit is not for today (Not at the time, Call 6), and the customer who wants to think about it after the visit (Call 13).
  • Commit to one rule for the week: "If I have shown a customer what the next failure is going to cost them, I do not leave that house until a Comfort Advisor has a time and a signature."
