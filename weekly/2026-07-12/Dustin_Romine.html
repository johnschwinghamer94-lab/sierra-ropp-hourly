Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Dustin hands off Option C on 44% of his scoreable calls (4 of 9) and wins repairs on 22% (2 of 9), and his defining strength is that he makes the office call from inside the house and keeps the customer company until the specialist walks in (Strong on wins) — Call 8: he's gonna send over Jordan. So this is talking to our guy. It's going to be no charge to you. I just need a signature for the office, then Jordan's heading on his way right now… I'll let him know what we looked at on Friday, what we've seen today, and then I'll show him everything around. His single biggest gap is that he plants the five-star seed on nearly every call and harvests it on two (Solid) — the Agenda Card ends above all, make sure I get you a 5-star experience on Calls 1, 3, 7, 9, 12 and 13, and only Calls 1 and 13 ever come back to it. He proved on Call 13 how little it costs: Last thing, we talked about getting a 5-star experience on today's visit. Safe to say I've done that for you today? — You did. The pattern behind the gap is that his closes are administrative rather than personal: signatures, an email address, an invoice promise, goodbye — Call 7 ends I'll send over my photos, invoice, all my information to that email and nothing else. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: in the Deliver step, treat the five-star line he already says at the door as a promise he has to collect on, and close every visit by naming it the way he does on Call 13.

Strengths

1. He gets the specialist into the house while he is still standing in it (Strong on wins)

Three of his four flips have a Comfort Advisor physically on site before Dustin leaves. Call 8: Oh, he's gonna send somebody right now. He's gonna send over Jordan… I just need a signature for the office, that way I can have them make up that ticket for you, and then he prepares the handover — everyone set up the ladder, get the attic ready for him, that way we can get everything streamlined for you with the right information. Call 2 is the same sequence with Sean twenty minutes out, and Call 11 has him walking the advisor through the attic and the two condensers. Coaching value: this is the flip executed to the letter of the guide — office call, signature, stay on site, brief the advisor — and it is the single behaviour most responsible for a 44% handoff rate.

2. He teaches with objects, not adjectives (Strong)

Call 2's heat exchanger becomes a paper clip — when you start to bend a paper clip back and forth, it gets weaker and weaker — and the evaporator coil becomes a car radiator: It's like a car radiator, as the air passes through it cools off the air… any moisture in the air is going to create condensation. Call 13 turns start-assist kits into a crutch to kind of help it limp along. Every one of these lands next to a measured number and its rated ceiling — Call 13: for the upstairs, we tested at 79.7, and then for the downstairs, we tested them at 89.7. The max for both of them is going to be 79.0. Coaching value: customers on his calls can repeat back what is wrong with their system, which is why his option sheets get read rather than pocketed.

3. He refuses the replacement ballpark and explains why in the customer's interest (Strong)

Call 2's customer pushes twice for a number and gets the specialist instead: I don't really want to give you a ballpark today… the numbers always change every single quarter… Now, the information for that is free and available to you. Call 8 repeats it almost verbatim — the last thing I want to do is tell you, give you a ballpark and I'm way off. It's not only free, the information is free to you. There's also no commitment. Both calls flipped. Coaching value: this is exactly the SILO process — replacement pricing belongs to the specialist — and he does it without sounding evasive, which is the hard part.

4. He absorbs a complaint about the company without getting defensive (Strong)

Call 2's customer spends a long stretch on dispatch: You don't even have a time frame for customers. You expect me to retirement, stay at home, wait for you for a whole day? Dustin answers I completely understand, explains the volume honestly — we're dealing with about 150 service calls per day — and then names the reason plainly: keeping it all open, it's at least the best way to not give a false promise. The customer flipped later in the same visit. Coaching value: he did not let a legitimate grievance about the office become a reason the customer said no to him.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The five-star seed is planted six times and harvested twice (Solid)

The Agenda Card ends above all, make sure I get you a 5-star experience on Calls 1, 3, 7, 9, 12 and 13. Only Call 13 comes back to it — Safe to say I've done that for you today?, answered You did — and only Call 1 asks in any form: I know I did everything I could to get you a 5-star service on today's visit… if you can leave me a review. Call 7's customer volunteers I really appreciate what you've done… it's very kind of you to stop by and is never asked. Call 13's customer volunteers I appreciate you guys are always so consistent and thorough — that one he did ask. Impact: the promise is made on nearly every visit and collected on a fifth of them, and the two customers who did get asked both said yes immediately.

Gap 2 — On no-moves he hands the next step to the customer's calendar (Strong on wins)

Call 13 does the Option C offer properly — I've included Option C at the bottom, which is just to talk to one of our equipment specialists… it's also no charge to you guys — and then accepts a deferral without a date: we will call and find out and get some price comparisons. His own suggestion, we can set up a time slot next week and see if you guys are both available, dies on a family holiday and nothing replaces it. Compare Call 8, where the same moment becomes let me go ahead and grab a signature from you real fast. Oh, he's gonna send somebody right now. Impact: Call 13 carried $4,642 of immediate repairs and two ten-year-old compressors over their amp ceiling, and it left the building with no appointment on anyone's calendar.

Gap 3 — Two maintenance visits end with no option of any kind offered (Moderate)

Call 7 finds two healthy Bosch units and closes on in the fall we come back — no Option C mentioned, no membership conversation, nothing. Call 1 finds a two-year-old Lennox in good order and a customer who says out loud I know that other units will be replaced at some point, but we're not ready yet — a stated future upgrade that is never picked up. Call 3 shows he knows the move: we always provide Option C, which is a no-charge, no-commitment discussion with one of our equipment specialists about updating the system to today's energy standards. Impact: Call 1's customer told him a replacement is coming and was not offered the free conversation that exists for exactly that customer.

Gap 4 — He answered the replacement question himself on the one call where a specialist was already coming (Moderate)

Call 10's customer is actively shopping — reading the 5,000 rule off their phone, asking what's the price of other companies? and how much is the new AC? Dustin answers it himself: I can guarantee you the system's gonna run you more than $10,000, then talks the customer out of the conversation — definitely at 4 years old it'd be a little bit overkill. On Calls 2 and 8, facing the identical question, he routed it to the specialist and both flipped. Impact: the one call this week with an openly comparison-shopping customer produced a repair and no lead, and it is the only call where he took the replacement question rather than passing it.

Gap 5 — Six of fifteen recordings stop before the evaluation is presented (Moderate)

Calls 4, 5, 6, 14 and 15 all end within a minute or two of I'll get started right away for you, and Call 9 runs 2,085 words and still ends at I'm going to lower the thermostat that way everything's running. Call 12 is a confirmed flip whose recording stops during the walkthrough, so the moment that produced the lead is not on tape at all. Impact: 40% of his week cannot be assessed for Decision or Deliver, which is why those two bands rest on nine calls rather than fifteen.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether Dustin dialled the office while the customer was still in front of him: on Calls 2, 8 and 11 a specialist is named, dispatched and met at the door, and on Calls 1, 7 and 13 the visit ends with a signature, an email address and a promise that someone will be in touch eventually.

  • Ready: Strong on both sides — he quotes the history file just as accurately on the no-moves (Call 13's they were high last year too. 75.1… and then 86.4) as on the flips. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Strong on both sides. The fullest Agenda Card of his week is on Call 1, a no-move. Expectations are not what separated them.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides — Call 13's fan-motor teaching is as good as anything on the flips, and it still produced nothing. His diagnosis is never the problem.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Moderate — the whole story. Call 8's call up my manager, get him out today against Call 13's we will call and find out and get some price comparisons.
  • Deliver: Solid on both sides, with the one difference running the other way — the two review asks in his week are both on no-move calls, Calls 1 and 13.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same paper-clip explanations, same Agenda Card at the door — but two endings, and the fork is whether he picked up the phone before he packed the ladder. On Call 8 he does it in front of the customer and then prepares the house for the advisor: everyone set up the ladder, get the attic ready for him. On Call 2 the specialist is about 20 minutes out and Dustin writes the repair report while they wait. On Call 11 he walks the advisor through the attic himself. On Call 13, with $4,642 of immediate repairs and two compressors over their rated amp draw, the close is we just call and set up an appointment? — the customer's question, not his; on Call 7 it is in the fall we come back; on Call 1 a customer who has just said a replacement is coming is not offered the free conversation. The variable is not the diagnosis, the teaching or the courtesy — those are constant and genuinely strong. It is whether an appointment existed before he walked out.

Beyond the Guide

He explains the price redirect instead of just performing it

Most technicians deflect a pricing question; Dustin gives the customer a reason they can accept. Call 2: the numbers always change every single quarter and they're always going to be different, especially with the newer coolants… it's gonna be different from house to house. Not every single house is gonna be the same installation. Call 8: It's not only free, the information is free to you. There's also no commitment. Coaching implication: this is the correct SILO redirect delivered well, and it is worth recording as the team's reference wording for the moment a customer asks a service tech what a system costs.

He runs a comfort survey he was not asked to run

On Call 2 he goes through set points, hot and cold rooms, electric bills, occupancy plans and health — Does anybody in the home suffer from any asthma, allergies, breathing challenges?, You planning on staying in the home for a while? — and hands all of it to Sean when he arrives. Coaching implication: he is doing the advisor's discovery for them, which is why his handoffs arrive warm; this should be named as a standard rather than treated as his personal habit.

Three recordings attribute the Comfort Advisor's speech to Dustin

On Calls 2, 8 and 11 the specialist's entire conversation — the comfort survey, the Costco credit, the tier discussion — is labelled as Dustin. Call 11 has both men on tape and a single speaker label across the whole exchange. Coaching implication: quotes in this report were attributed by content rather than by label, and anyone auditing these files for tech behaviour will over-credit him unless they know that.

A safety find nobody had reported in four years of visits

Call 2's attic disconnect had been bypassed with tape and wire nuts since before Sierra's first visit: someone along the lines has come through and bypassed the switch with tape and wire nuts. The customer's reaction — I didn't know this, Dustin, at all, Nobody tell me about the switch — is the moment the visit turns. Dustin's own answer is honest about it: we've been trying to focus on the main thing going on… things like this can easily get overlooked. Coaching implication: the thorough evaluation earned the flip on that call more than any option sheet did, and it is a concrete argument for the evaluation being run in full on repeat-visit homes.
