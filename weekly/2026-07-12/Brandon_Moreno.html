Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Brandon hands off Option C on 67% of his scoreable calls (12 of 18) and wins repairs on 11% (2 of 18), on the largest verified base on the roster this week — twenty-four recordings, eighteen of them scoreable. By the manager’s doctrine he is the strongest flipper in the corpus, and it is not luck: he runs the same opening block on every call (Strong) — I won’t do any work without you guys’ permission. We’ll just walk out of here holding a part. And then we do all this for a 5-star experience, so if you feel like you don’t like it, let me know, make the corrections — and closes the handoff with a booked appointment rather than an offer: Call 16, we’ll be back out then Tuesday, 8 to 10. They’ll give you a ring when they’re on the way. His single biggest gap is that when a customer names money as the obstacle, the free specialist visit — the one thing that answers that objection — stops being offered. Call 8, the customer says financially we’re not ready yet to like, cuz it’s costly. Yeah, it’s like thousands of dollars, and no specialist is mentioned; no turnover lead exists for that job. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: treat a budget objection as the trigger for the no-cost visit he already sells so well, instead of as the end of the conversation.

Strengths

1. Booking the specialist instead of offering one (Strong)

This is why his handoff rate is the highest in the corpus. He does not leave the appointment as an intention. Call 24: let me just call the office. I just need a couple signatures so that they could book his appointment… one down here, and this is just that we spoke on this, and then the next one will actually be so he could come out. Call 16 he names the slot — we’ll be back out then Tuesday, 8 to 10. They’ll give you a ring when they’re on the way — and pre-empts the availability objection first: the best thing I can do is if it’s not today as far as availability, we could definitely get you on a better date for both you folks… there’s no cost as long as I set it up for you guys. Call 23 makes the routing explicit: if it’s through me, you get — it’s no charge for the booking. Coaching value: the difference between a lead and a good intention is a signature and a time slot, and he takes both on the call.

2. Framing Option C as information rather than a decision (Strong)

The offer is consistently stripped of commitment before the customer can object to it. Call 18: system update, that’s going to be where we have an equipment specialist come out… It’s not set in stone where you got to make a decision there and then. It’s more of, here’s the information, this is what it looks like, so you could compare what that buyback cost looks like versus outside of your warranty, and at least you could weigh out your options then. Call 19 ties it to a concrete benefit the repair cannot deliver: it’s no cost. We can have our equipment guy come out, show you what it looks like… That way you can actually have a warranty after and then compare and contrast the cost. Call 7 he even offers it on a system with no immediate faults at all: it’s no-cost visit for them to come out, provide you that info. It’s no obligation, and that way you can see what’s changed in the last year. Coaching value: the customer is never asked to buy anything on the technician’s visit, which is exactly what the SILO process asks of him.

3. Handing the customer the evidence, literally (Strong)

He photographs and films everything and then puts the phone in the customer’s hands. Call 8: feel free to hold this so I could go through the photos for you, and we’ll just scroll through… these are the gauges that we hook up to the system to see basically the vitals on it. Call 20 the video does the arguing for him on a failing fan motor: these have a sealed grease pack. At one point in time, a while back this thing got way too hot. You could see where all this grease basically leaked out… these things normally they should roll like a skateboard. This thing’s stiff as a board. And then you can even see how much shaft play there is. Call 10 he repeats the evidence deliberately: here’s like another video showing this, just because I want to be very straightforward with you. Coaching value: a customer holding the phone is not being told, they are looking. It is the fastest route to the credibility a turnover lead depends on.

4. An analogy set that covers every part of the system (Strong)

Brandon has a comparison for each component and reuses them consistently. The compressor is a heart under strain — it’s basically, you know, ready to have a heart attack because all this stuff is at its limit or exceeding it (Call 10) — and replacing it is open heart surgery on an AC unit (Call 21). Excess amperage is a tachometer: on your car engine you have like their RPM, that red area… We’re past that red area (Call 10). Voltage-switch contacts are almost like brake pads (Call 18). A preventative-maintenance visit is a check-up: it’s like you go to the doctor for a visit, they check your blood pressure, but they’re not going to give you medicine right there and then (Call 6). And on damage already done: next step you’re gonna need a time machine to go back in time (Call 14). Coaching value: the vocabulary is portable and worth standardising across the team.

5. Working the call in the customer’s language (Solid)

Call 3 is conducted in Spanish throughout, including the diagnosis and the ageing-equipment conversation — ahorita también con la edad, este es de 2012, so ya cuando ya pasa como unos 10 años ya con las máquinas ya va a estar teniendo — and it produced a verified turnover lead off a 686-word visit. Coaching value: this is a capability the roster should know it has. It is also the shortest recording in his week that flipped, which says the handoff does not depend on call length.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — A budget objection ends the conversation instead of triggering the free visit (Weak)

Call 8 is the cleanest example. The customer states the obstacle plainly — it’s just that financially we’re not ready yet to like, cuz it’s costly. Yeah, it’s like thousands of dollars — and the recording moves on to household small talk. No specialist is mentioned, no comparison is offered, and no turnover lead exists for that job. Call 13 repeats the shape with a customer who is explicit about timing: I wasn’t anticipating putting up that kind of cost in any capacity. So yeah, if I do have to allocate that, it would probably have to be like in 2 months. What would have flipped it: Brandon’s own Option C script is the answer to exactly this objection — it costs nothing, commits to nothing and produces the comparison a cash-constrained customer most needs. On both calls the sentence he uses a dozen times elsewhere simply was not said.

Gap 2 — Naming the right conclusion without converting it (Moderate)

Call 13 he reaches the correct judgement out loud and then stops: the unit is a 2011 R-22 system past its lifespan, the compressor is overworking, the coil is rusting and the blower motor is leaking oil, and he tells the customer this thing has a high chance of failing even after we do this because it’s not addressing — it’s the compressor. Basically, the system’s ready to just kick over… it may be wise to just look into the replacement options just because this is a very expensive Band-Aid. The call then ends at I’m gonna send you an email with a report, but you’re good to go. No specialist, no appointment, no lead. What would have flipped it: look into the replacement options is advice; I’ll have our specialist here Tuesday at no cost is a lead. He was one sentence away and the sentence is already in his repertoire.

Gap 3 — Anchoring on a repair number before the options exist (Moderate)

Call 22 opens with a price before any diagnosis has been shared: just idea-wise, ballpark, because I don’t want to waste your time, seems like you’ve been dealing with this stuff, we prepare you looking around maybe $1,500 to $2,000. The system turns out to be badly low on refrigerant with a leak and a coil in poor condition, and the visit ends on the attic tour with no third option ever built. What would strengthen it: the intention — not wasting the customer’s time — is sound, but the effect is that the customer is deciding between repair and no repair before Option C has been introduced. The ballpark belongs after the options sheet, not before it.

Gap 4 — Multi-system properties presented as two repair lists (Moderate)

Call 11 covers two systems and produces two well-built immediate lists — these two come out to $1,768 with your Sam’s discount for the living room, $1,294 for that for the bedroom — with the ageing compressor correctly noted under future on both. What never appears is the observation that a household facing roughly $3,000 of immediate repairs across two systems, both with compressors drawing excess power, is the strongest possible candidate for a comparison visit. No lead was created. What would strengthen it: on multi-system homes the totals should be added out loud, once, and the sum handed to the specialist.

Gap 5 — No review ask anywhere in twenty-four recordings (Weak)

The goodwill is unusually explicit in this week’s corpus. Call 12: No, I appreciate you, man. Take the time, explain everything to you, man. Call 16 the customer offers water twice and thanks him for the advice. Call 4: Oh, you’re amazing. Thank you. Not one of the twenty-four recordings contains a request for a Google review. What would strengthen it: he already ends calls with a knock and a summary; the ask fits in that moment and nowhere else in his routine needs to change.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

With eighteen scoreable calls this is the most reliable split on the roster. The front of Brandon’s call is essentially invariant — same opening block, same data-plate method, same photo walkthrough — and the variance sits almost entirely in whether the third option is built.

  • Ready: Neutral. History and warranty status are checked on flips and no-moves alike; Call 18, a no-move, contains the most careful warranty work of the week.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong. The expectations block and the permission-to-report question appear on both outcomes.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 11 and Call 22, both no-moves, carry diagnoses as complete as any flip.
  • Decision: The entire difference. On all twelve flips a third option exists, is priced at zero and is usually booked on the call. On Calls 8, 11, 13 and 22 no third option is built at all.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The knock, the window and the emailed report are the same either way, as is the absent review ask.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Brandon’s four no-moves share one feature: money was raised as an obstacle, and the free specialist visit was not offered in response. On Call 8 the customer said the repairs were unaffordable; on Call 13 the customer needed two months; on Call 11 two systems produced roughly $3,000 of immediate work; on Call 22 a ballpark was volunteered before a third option existed. In every other call in his week the answer to that pressure is the same and it works: a no-cost, no-commitment comparison visit, booked before he leaves. The gap is not the script, which is the best on the roster. It is that the script is currently triggered by reaching the options sheet rather than by hearing the objection it was designed to answer.

Beyond the Guide

Explaining permits and insurance exposure to a customer with prior bad work

Call 12, on a system installed without inspection: if the AC unit, let’s say they put it on the roof, and it starts leaking water, whatever happens — the home insurance isn’t going to cover it because they’re going to see no permits. The customer had already been burned by an out-of-network installer and responds by describing the whole history unprompted. Coaching implication: this is a genuine differentiator against cut-price competition and it is stated as fact rather than as a scare. It belongs in the team’s standard vocabulary.

Refrigerant economics explained with the customer’s own two systems

Call 20 he uses the property as its own demonstration: you got a good example right here with the two units. So this, the one that’s down, it’s that R-22 Freon. So that one, two phases behind, banned in 2010, against the other system on current refrigerant, with the cost consequence attached — we’re looking around $550 per pound for just that Freon… the other one I checked out, that one’s got the updated stuff, that’s closer to $80 to $100 a pound. Coaching implication: a side-by-side inside one house is more persuasive than any general statement about phase-outs, and it flipped.

Open-microphone conduct

Call 9 captures a joke about a fatal aviation incident, with profanity, moments after the customer walks away, on a live recording inside their home. Call 3 captures an unrelated argument in the background of a customer visit. Coaching implication: no customer is heard reacting in either recording, but both are on the record during a paid visit. This is the one item in an otherwise exemplary week that carries risk disproportionate to anything it gains.

A scripted promotional read mid-visit

Call 16 contains a verbatim marketing read for a seasonal upgrade package delivered while the customer waits — that’s why we created the… Summer Survival Kit, a complete system upgrade designed to help your air conditioner perform more reliably when demand is at its highest. Coaching implication: it sits oddly against the evidence-first style that makes the rest of his call work, and it is doing a job his own diagnosis already does better. Worth a decision at manager level about where scripted promotional material belongs in a service visit.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Make the budget objection the trigger for the free visit. All four no-moves involve money being named as the obstacle, and in none of them was the no-cost comparison offered. Calls 8 and 13 are the two to replay: in both, the customer had already explained precisely why they needed the specialist.
  • Convert “look into replacement options” into a booked appointment. Call 13 reached the correct conclusion out loud and stopped. Compare it to Call 16, where the same conclusion becomes we’ll be back out then Tuesday, 8 to 10.
  • Add the totals out loud on multi-system homes. Call 11 produced two separate repair lists and never stated the combined figure, which is the number that makes the comparison visit obviously worth taking.
  • Add the review ask to the closing knock. Twenty-four recordings, no ask, on the warmest set of customer responses in the corpus.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for all four no-moves in the week; Priorities 3 and 4 raise the ceiling on calls that are already going well.
