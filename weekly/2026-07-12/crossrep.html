Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Across 209 recordings and 163 scoreable calls, the team handed off Option C on 57% of calls and won repairs on 9%, for a combined close rate of 66%. By the manager’s doctrine that is a good week: 93 turnover leads were created and verified in ServiceTitan. The variation between technicians is not explained by diagnostic quality — every one of the thirteen bands Assessment at Strong, without exception. It is explained almost entirely by what happens in the last two minutes of the call. Technicians who convert the offer into a time and a signature sit at the top of the handoff table; technicians who convert it into an emailed estimate, a callback or a hypothetical sit at the bottom, with identical diagnoses behind them.

Step Leaderboard

Counting bands across the thirteen technicians, the five SILO steps are not close to each other:

  • Assessment — the team’s strongest step by a wide margin. Thirteen of thirteen band Strong. Every technician on this roster reads the manufacturer’s plate value aloud, measures against it, and explains the consequence. This step is solved and should not consume coaching time.
  • Welcome — strong and improving. Nine Strong, four Solid. The permission-to-report question (if I notice anything out of the ordinary, do you want me to let you know or keep it to myself?) has clearly propagated across the roster and appears in near-identical form from at least six technicians.
  • Ready — strong. Eight Strong, five Solid. Warranty registration checks and prior-visit comparisons are now routine.
  • Decision — the split step. Four Strong, eight Strong on wins, one Moderate. “Strong on wins” is the single most common band on the roster and it means precisely one thing: the technician can build the third option, and does not build it every time.
  • Deliver — the team’s weakest step, and not one technician bands Strong. Nine Solid, four Moderate, zero Strong. The reason is uniform and is set out below.

Top Third vs Bottom Third

Taking the top four by handoff rate (Robert Silinzy and Noah Weng at 75%, Brandon Moreno at 67%, Mario Castro and Francisco Valencia at 64%) against the bottom four (Joe Mendoza 38%, Juan Tlatenchi 42%, Nikko April 43%, Dustin Romine 44%), the bands are essentially level:

  • Assessment: Strong in both tiers. No separation.
  • Ready and Welcome: Strong-to-Solid in both tiers. No separation.
  • Decision: Strong or Strong on wins in both tiers. No separation on band — but a large separation in mechanism, which the band key does not capture.
  • Deliver: Moderate appears twice in the top tier and twice in the bottom. No separation.

In other words, the banded scorecard does not predict the handoff rate this week. What does predict it is a single binary behaviour, examined next.

The Positive-Outcome Predictor Test

Reading every no-move in the week against every flip, one behaviour separates them more reliably than any band: whether the Option C offer ends in a time.

  • Offer ends in a time or a signature → lead created. Brandon Moreno, Call 16: we’ll be back out then Tuesday, 8 to 10. They’ll give you a ring when they’re on the way. Francisco Valencia, Call 20: just an autograph to acknowledge that you want to get an estimate… it’s $0 for that, with the ticket raised before he leaves. Cole Pantol, Call 15: it takes them about 30-40 minutes to get out here. I stay here until they get here. Nikko April, Call 16: I could have someone come out today… can I get a signature on the bottom that you want to look at that option?
  • Offer ends in an intention → no lead. Nikko April, Call 3: I’ll send you the estimate to speak to someone. When you guys are ready, just go to my email. Benjamin Wyllie, Call 18: I’m calling him at noon on Tuesday to get a CA out immediately — ServiceTitan shows no lead was ever created for that job. Cole Pantol, Call 2: I’ll have them call you. Benjamin Wyllie, Call 14, after the customer volunteered at these prices, I think we would just like replace the whole thing: if that’s something that you guys want to at least learn to get to know… we can always do that.
Decisive finding

Decisive finding. The technicians on this roster are not losing flips because they cannot diagnose, cannot explain, or cannot handle objections — the transcripts show all three are present on the losses as much as on the wins. They are losing flips in the last sentence, by handing the customer a decision to make later instead of an appointment to keep. Every high-handoff week in this corpus ends its offers with a clock time; every low-handoff week ends them with a condition.

Team-Wide Recurring Gaps

1. The money objection is treated as a stop rather than a trigger

This is the most expensive pattern in the week and it appears in at least six technicians’ no-moves. Brandon Moreno, Call 8: the customer says financially we’re not ready yet… it’s like thousands of dollars and no specialist is mentioned. Mario Castro, Call 14: the customer’s representative asks in plain words for two options, one repair and one replacement, and receives neither. Cole Pantol, Calls 3 and 7: What’s it cost to get a whole new air conditioner? and what is it the cost to replace a compressor?, both answered with refrigerant scarcity and no handoff. Francisco Valencia, Call 23: no, I can’t do it. 700, that’s too much. In every one of these cases the customer had just named the exact problem that a free comparison visit exists to solve.

2. Nobody asks for a review — one recording in two hundred and nine

Across every technician on the roster and 209 recordings, exactly one contains a request for a Google review: Francisco Valencia, Call 21, and he does it on a no-move. If you have time, you can leave a review, be like, hey, Francisco is the worst technician out here, I never send them out here. So it just helps us a lot when the homeowners do that. This single omission is why no technician on the roster bands Deliver at Strong. The goodwill is not the constraint — the corpus is full of customers offering water, thanking technicians by name, and in one case saying thank you for being honest after a competitor misled them.

3. Replacement pricing given by technicians who should be routing it

The SILO process assigns replacement pricing to the Comfort Advisor, and most of the roster holds that line well — Cole Pantol declines four separate times in the same words, Noah Weng and Mario Castro both redirect cleanly, Benjamin Wyllie states plainly that he does not have that pricing. Against that, Francisco Valencia’s Call 18 supplies system price bands, brand tiers, zoning add-ons and a discount figure committed on the advisor’s behalf, and Andrew Trujillo names replacement-scale totals on four separate calls. Where this happens the advisor arrives having to honour or walk back a number they did not set.

4. Recorder discipline between and after customer conversations

Several technicians’ recordings capture extended technician-to-technician conversation on customer property, and in a small number of cases disparaging remarks about the customer after they leave the room. This changes no band in any report and no customer is heard reacting in any recording. It is raised here once, at team level, because it is on company recordings made during paid visits, and because in at least one case the recording itself attributes a lost call to the distraction.

What the Roster Already Does Well

  • Rated-versus-measured is universal. Every technician anchors a failure claim to a plate value. This is the reason customers accept the diagnosis and it should be protected.
  • The refrigerant transition is explained accurately and consistently. The January 2025 change, the unobtainable coils, the requirement to replace indoor and outdoor together — the roster tells one coherent story, which is unusual and valuable.
  • Two technicians work in Spanish. Brandon Moreno and Francisco Valencia both conducted full diagnoses in Spanish this week, and both flipped.
  • Competitors’ misdiagnoses are corrected without disparagement. Cole Pantol’s Call 3 and Mario Castro’s Call 8 are the two clearest examples and both are worth using in training.

Management Action Summary

  • One rule, roster-wide: no Option C offer ends without a time. Either the advisor is coming today, or a slot is named, or a signature is taken. This is the single highest-value change available and it requires no new skill from anyone — four technicians already do it and their language can be lifted verbatim.
  • Make the money objection the trigger. When a customer says a price is too much, cannot afford it, or asks what a new system costs, the next sentence is the no-cost comparison visit. This accounts for the majority of the week’s no-moves.
  • Add one review-ask sentence to every technician’s close. Francisco Valencia’s wording is the template. This is the only change that will move Deliver off Solid for anyone.
  • Confirm the policy on service-fee waivers. Two technicians’ recordings describe the visit fee being waived in connection with a Comfort Advisor attending, in one case explicitly as an unstated practice. This needs a stated position rather than an informal one.
  • Fix the untitled-recording problem in dispatch. Thirteen recordings this week carry no ServiceTitan job number and therefore cannot be scored in either direction. Five of them belong to one technician, which is why his verifiable base is four calls against a roster average of twelve.

Appendix — Method and Data Coverage

Corpus. 209 recordings across thirteen technicians, 2026-07-06 to 2026-07-12, drawn from transcripts/2026-07-06 through transcripts/2026-07-12. 163 recordings are scoreable; the remainder are arrival fragments, preventative-maintenance visits with no options, duplicate visits, or recordings with no ServiceTitan job number whose outcome cannot be verified. Each per-rep report lists its own exclusions.

Outcome ground truth. Every Option C handoff counted in this report is confirmed against tgl_truth/<date>.json by source job number, including turnover leads created the following day. None is inferred from transcript impressions, and where a transcript reads like a flip but no lead exists in ServiceTitan it is counted as a no-move — Benjamin Wyllie’s Call 18 is the clearest instance and is documented in his report. Repair wins were determined by reading each call ending for an approved Immediate or Future repair.

Data gaps. tgl_truth/ytd_sources.json is not present in this checkout, so source-job attribution relies on the per-day tgl_truth files alone. Per-call scorecards do not exist for this week — scorecards_full begins 2026-07-14 — so no call in any report carries a pre-existing outcome tag, and every band is derived from the transcripts directly. This week was graded as a backfill; the five per-rep reports already on file were left untouched and their figures are carried into this report as published.

Method. Assessed against the SILO Field Strategy Guide using the five-band key, with the Option C handoff treated as the primary win and a won repair as a secondary outcome. No numeric scoring of any SILO step anywhere. The only numbers in this report are verified outcome rates and plain counts. Routing a replacement pricing question to a Comfort Advisor is the correct move under the SILO process and is credited as a strength throughout; where a gap concerns pricing it concerns the absence of that routing, never a technician’s candour about cost.
