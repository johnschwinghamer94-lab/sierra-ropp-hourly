Strengths

1. He removes every barrier to the advisor visit at once (Strong on wins)

Noah's booking sequence is the most frictionless on the roster because he clears the customer's wallet before asking. Call 3: I'll just go ahead and waive the diagnostic fee, so you won't pay— you won't owe me anything today. And then his appointment is free too, then for his visit and my visit, I made my visit courtesy. His visit is courtesy as well. You owe me $0 today, closing with I just need a signature, initial, whatever you want to give me. You could draw a smiley face, it wouldn't matter. Call 4 runs the identical play and lands a window the same evening: he just texted me we have 10 to 12 or 4 to 6. Coaching value: six flips from eight calls is the best conversion in the window, and the "signature can be a smiley face" line is a genuinely disarming way to make the commitment feel small.

2. He teaches better than anyone else in the window, and customers say so (Strong)

Call 11's customer tells him outright: asked whether the explanation landed, the answer is Yeah, more than anybody else has. The line that earned it is a piece of real insight about why bad equipment gets installed — when you come in and you buy a house, what are you looking at? … You're not looking at the air conditioning. Nobody's in the backyard high-fiving each other because they have an AC with all the bells and whistles — followed by a clear single-stage-versus-two-stage explanation for a zoned system. Call 3 does the same with evidence in hand: you can see how it's brown and it looks oil slick. Like, your compressor has leaked its freon. And then this is where our pressure should be, and this is where we're at. Coaching value: he converts a technical failure into a story about the customer's own house, which is exactly what earns the right to present — and it is currently earning him appointments rather than approvals.

3. He tells customers when the equipment is not the whole problem (Strong)

On Call 1 he stays on the real cause rather than the sellable one — It's cuz you're getting all that, all that air in the attic — after a full hour of watching a thermostat refuse to move. On Call 11 he gives the customer a free protective habit rather than a part: if you have it set to 80 and it's 70 degrees in here, and that thing's still running, you're— go turn it off… not only can you damage your components, but it becomes an electrolyzer. Coaching value: this is the credibility that makes his diagnoses land without argument, and it is worth protecting explicitly, because the coaching below is all about asking for more, not about softening what he says.

4. He handles the language gap before it becomes a misunderstanding (Strong)

Call 4: I have a little bit of an accent… whenever I say, people mix up my N and my M sometimes, so I end up just saying, you know, November or Maria. He raises it himself, early, and turns it into a shared joke rather than letting a customer mishear a part name or a price. Coaching value: small, deliberate and entirely his own — it removes a friction point most technicians would let run all visit.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — He never asks for the repair on any call, won or lost (Weak)

Across eleven recordings there is not one instance of Noah asking a customer to approve Immediate or Future work. Call 3 presents the immediate repair, being the leak search and repair, and the R-22 by the pound for refrigerant, it's $7,518.75 and the next thing he does is waive his own fee and book the advisor. Call 1 explains a $6,000 compressor in detail (Compressor itself is $6,000. That includes Freon, labor, everything) and closes with a promise to phone. Call 10 finds concerns, goes over them, and takes $39. Impact: a 0% Repair-Won rate is the arithmetic result — on Call 3 a customer who had said if we could repair parts, I'll be grateful with that was never asked whether she wanted the repair.

Gap 2 — Every flip is bought with the money already in the room (Weak)

The waiver is not an occasional concession, it is the mechanism. Call 3: I'll just go ahead and waive the diagnostic fee… You owe me $0 today. Call 4: I'll make tonight's, uh, I'll make tonight's visit courtesy… I won't charge you anything since you get an estimate. Both are flips; both collected nothing. Contrast Call 10, where he charged the $39 and there was no advisor. Impact: the two calls that converted best this week are also the two that billed nothing, so his strongest behaviour and his weakest are the same act — and Call 5's van conversation shows he knows exactly what the trade is: at the end of the day when everything's done, whatever it's gotten up to, that's how much you get paid per flip.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether Noah waives the fee: on Calls 3 and 4 he makes both visits free and books an advisor inside a minute, and on Calls 1 and 10 he charges normally, offers the advisor as information, and leaves with nothing scheduled.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides. Call 4's accent handling is his best preparation of the week and Call 1's file review was equally good — one flipped, one did not. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Strong on both sides, and delivered in full on Call 1, a no-move. Expectations are not what is missing.
  • Assessment: Strong across the board and strongest on Call 11 and Call 1 — one flip, one no-move. His teaching is a constant, not a discriminator.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Moderate — the whole story, and it splits on a single sentence. Call 3's You owe me $0 today against Call 1's It doesn't cost anything for him to come out here, which was offered but never converted into a booking.
  • Deliver: Weak on both sides. Every call ends with photos, videos and a gate closed behind him; none ends with a review ask or a revisited membership.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same compressor teaching, same warmth — but two endings, and the fork is whether he gives his own money away. On Call 3 a customer with a $7,518.75 repair in front of her says she cannot afford a new unit, and Noah's answer is to make everything free — I'll go ahead and waive the diagnostic fee… his appointment is free too… You owe me $0 today — and the advisor is booked for after five. On Call 4 the identical sequence lands a 10-to-12 window for the next morning. On Call 1 he offers the same visit at the same price — It doesn't cost anything for him to come out here, then go ahead and get the estimate. That way you can take that to her — and then, instead of booking it, ends the visit with I'm going to give you a call, okay? Probably tomorrow or for the next 2 days. On Call 10 he charges the $39 and no advisor is mentioned. The variable is not the diagnosis, the explanation or the rapport — those are the same on every call. It is whether the free visit turned into a time slot before he left.

Beyond the Guide

He is doing the install engineer's job on site, unasked

Call 8 is not a sales call at all — it is Noah scoping a replacement in a tight condo closet with a colleague: I have 60 inches to where we can adapt to the top of the trunk, This thing is touching the water heater, dude. It's 22 inches wide, so we'll be okay there, and then the solution — if I do a pancake unit, this can all be closed up for her because the pancake units pull through the front and that filter slides in. Coaching implication: this is real installation judgement that makes the advisor's quote deliverable rather than optimistic, and it is worth knowing he has it — but it is also several hours of a SILO technician's week spent on someone else's step.

The pay structure he describes explains the behaviour the numbers show

On Call 5, off-customer, Noah explains the compensation to a colleague: at the end of the day when everything's done, whatever it's gotten up to, that's how much you get paid per flip. A technician paid per flip, who can waive a $39 diagnostic to secure one, will rationally trade the fee for the appointment every time — which is precisely the pattern in his results: the highest handoff rate in the window and no revenue collected on the calls that produced it. Coaching implication: this is an incentive-design observation for management rather than a coaching point for Noah; he is responding correctly to the scheme he is under.

Long dead time is being recorded on site

Call 9 has him waiting for a decision-maker who is in a meeting: I'm writing my report, so I got time, then I've been at this call for an hour and 46 minutes and 15 seconds. Um, I'm completely done, then I'm going to sit in here for about 20 more minutes. Call 2 involves a similar wait for an owner locked out of her own email. Coaching implication: two of eleven visits were spent largely waiting on absent decision-makers — a dispatch and confirmation problem that is costing him calls, not a motivation problem.

He gives customers the physics of their own bill

Call 3 closes the future-repairs case on efficiency rather than fear: given the fact this thing's been running for 19 years, and you are overpaying on the utility by about 29%. Call 7 turns the desert into an argument the customer finishes himself: when it's 115 outside… this is like putting somebody in a nice cold car. Coaching implication: the efficiency framing is the Option B statement the guide asks for, delivered naturally — it just needs to be followed by a request rather than by a waiver.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Ask for the repair before offering to waive anything. On Call 3 Noah presented $7,518.75 of immediate work to a customer who had just said if we could repair parts, I'll be grateful with that, and moved straight to You owe me $0 today. Present Immediate and Future, ask which option works best today, and wait. If the answer is the upgrade, the free advisor visit is still available thirty seconds later — and if it is not, there is a repair on the board.
  • Turn "it doesn't cost anything" into a time slot in the same breath. Call 1 contains the exact same offer as his two best flips — It doesn't cost anything for him to come out here — and ends with I'm going to give you a call… probably tomorrow or for the next 2 days. Call 4 shows the finished version: we have 10 to 12 or 4 to 6. The offer is not the close; the window is.
  • Stop the recording when the customer work stops, and keep the music off while presenting. Call 5's file has no customer on it at all and carries remarks about a colleague that should never sit on a company recording; Calls 8 and 11 have music loud enough to replace his own voice in the transcript. This costs him nothing to fix and it is currently the single biggest professional risk in his week.

The two visits largely spent waiting on absent decision-makers are worth raising with dispatch, but they are a distant fourth as a coaching item — Calls 2 and 9 were not his to fix, whereas the repair he never asks for is entirely within his control and is the whole of his zero.
