Strengths

1. He books the Comfort Advisor himself, on the phone, in the room (Strong on wins)

All three of Andrew's flips this week were produced by the same mechanism, and it is not a script — it is a phone call he makes rather than a promise he leaves behind. On Call 6 the customer asks the question every SILO tech waits for — would it just make sense to get a whole new system? — and Andrew is dialling within two turns: Hey, Ryan. Looking to see if we have availability for tomorrow afternoon for a CA. He then calls the customer back with real options (I only have a 1 to 3 for tomorrow. Is that going to work out for you?), takes her counter, and lands it: I'll book you down for that 8 to 10 then. On Call 8 he does it again for an absent homeowner: Okay, let me quickly make a call just while I have you on the phone because I just want to confirm that we have that availability with you. Coaching value: this is the primary win of the SILO role executed end-to-end without being asked, and it is the single behavior that separates his week's outcomes — it should be named back to him as the thing he already knows how to do.

2. A repeatable teaching script that customers visibly follow (Strong)

The voltage-switch explanation appears on Calls 1, 2 and 4 in almost identical words — This turns it on, turns it off. When the system is engaged, that presses in, the metal is touched, and that sends in the power. All right, now over the years, this thing cycled thousands of times (Call 2) — and it works because it converts an invisible component into a light switch. On Call 1 the customer picks the language straight back up and starts diagnosing with him: graying brown spots right here… they're going to be the first to go… we should still be able to piecemeal it, right? Coaching value: he has already built the hardest asset in Assessment — a customer-language explanation he can deliver identically every time — and it is the foundation the Decision step should be resting on.

3. He finds the decision-maker at the door instead of at the close (Strong)

Three of this week's seven calls had the real decision-maker somewhere else — a neighbour minding the house on Call 3, a son leaving for work with his mother arriving later on Call 6, an absent wife on Call 8 — and on every one of them Andrew establishes it in the first minutes. Call 6, turn one: If I happen to come across concerns, who would be the best person to bring that information to? Call 3: I'll need to give her a call because she'll need to be aware of, hey, if we're installing filters, I need her to be aware of the price. Coaching value: the diagnostic half of the absent-decision-maker problem is already solved; protect it, because it is what makes the Call 6 handoff possible at all.

4. He offers to put the missing spouse on the phone rather than waiting for them (Strong on wins)

On Call 2 the customer reaches for the standard deferral — let me talk to the wifey and all that, and then we'll go from there — and Andrew does not accept it at face value: Do you want to give her a call really quick? I mean, this is a lot of information to break down. I would hate for you to have to—. On Call 6 the same instinct is carried all the way through, with the ABC sheet photographed and sent to the absent parent mid-call: I have everything uploaded on Titan right now if you want to take a look at the ABC sheet… send her a picture of the ABC sheet. Coaching value: the reflex is right and it is already in his mouth; the gap is that on Call 2 he offered once, was declined, and stopped — one more attempt is the difference between his Call 2 and his Call 6.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Booking himself for a follow-up call instead of booking someone into the home (Strong on wins)

This is the whole week in one behavior. On the four no-moves the close is converted into a future phone call with Andrew on the other end of it: Call 1, When I give you a call tomorrow, do you know if James is going to be available as well too?; Call 2, I'll give you a call later tonight, and that way, if you have any extra questions; Call 3, we can absolutely schedule a time so that way we can all talk while he's not busy with work; Call 4, Email and we will go from there. On the three flips he never once offers his own callback — he offers a Comfort Advisor's calendar. Impact: four calls with fully presented, fully priced option sheets produced no outcome at all, and on Call 1 that included a customer who had already said if everybody could just follow up with me like tomorrow — an open invitation to book someone that went unbooked.

Gap 2 — The acknowledgment signature standing in for the close (Strong on wins)

On every no-move Andrew ends by collecting a signature whose stated purpose is that options were presented: this just says acknowledging that we went over the concerns of the system and I provided you with these forms just going over your options (Call 2), and on Call 3, we acknowledge the findings that we have today. We addressed the concerns. I provided you with your options. The paperwork closes the visit; nothing closes the sale. Contrast Call 5, where the signatures come after a financing application and an install window — they'll, you know, 8 to 10 timeframe sound okay tomorrow? Impact: the ritual gives both parties a clean ending to a call with no outcome, which is why Calls 1–4 feel finished on tape despite producing nothing.

Gap 3 — Quoting replacement pricing that belongs to the Comfort Advisor (Moderate)

Andrew repeatedly puts a replacement number in the customer's head before a specialist has seen the home, and the number moves while he is saying it. Call 8: compressor itself for a first run is just shy of $5,000, then with the box on, we're looking at around $7K, then on the second system You're looking at at least $6,500… The lowest I've seen them is going around here. They can go as high as $8,000. Call 1 lands on $12,000 per system; Call 3 on roughly about $5,000 at minimum. Redirecting the upgrade question to the specialist is the correct SILO move and he knows it — Call 6, I can make a call and have one of our specialists come by so that way they can give you all the information that you need. Impact: on Call 8 the Comfort Advisor walked into a home already anchored to a $7,000 figure Andrew had estimated on the driveway, and on Calls 1 and 3 the replacement conversation was priced and closed before anyone qualified for it.

Gap 4 — The specialist visit sold as free information rather than as the next step (Moderate)

When Andrew names the upgrade path on the calls that stall, he frames it as costless browsing rather than as something to do: Call 3, talk to an install specialist for free to see what it could be like to get a fresh start on the system; Call 6 in its softest form, so that way they can give you all the information that you need, so that way you guys can make the best educated decision for yourselves. On Call 6 he rescues it by immediately proposing times; on Call 3 there is no time proposed and nothing happens. Impact: "free" and "information" give the customer nothing to say yes to, so the offer only converts on the calls where he follows it with a calendar in the same breath.

Gap 5 — Manufactured urgency where the honest urgency was already there (Moderate)

On Call 3 Andrew reaches past a real failing compressor for a structural argument the transcript cannot support: if it gets too warm in here, that can warp the walls, it can crack the floors. Like, we're talking structural damage to the home. Is that a concern at all? The customer's answer is a shrug — it's a concern and we'll watch it — and the call ends without a decision. On Call 4 the same reach appears against a customer who has already explained the house is empty and being gutted: even if it's set at 79, it's going to have to regulate that temperature, answered with whatever will happen will happen. Impact: both times the overreach replaced a stronger, simpler argument he had already made — the compressor is failing and the parts are getting scarce — and both calls closed with nothing.

Gap 6 — The five-star seed planted and never harvested (Weak)

Call 1 carries the seed in full: I just do this to make sure we're giving you a good 5-star experience. If at any point I'm doing something you don't want me to do, or if I'm not doing something you want me to do, feel free to stop me and let me know. Across all eight recordings there is not one review ask. Call 3 ends with the customer volunteering the review for him — did an excellent job. Thank you, Andrew — and Andrew answers Let me just grab my ladder and I'll be on my way. Impact: he does the difficult, early half of the review process and skips the ten-second half on calls where the customer has already said the words unprompted.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether Andrew makes a phone call from the customer's home: on Calls 5, 6 and 8 he dials the office and books a named person into a named time slot, and on Calls 1, 2, 3 and 4 — same warmth, same teaching, same priced sheet — he schedules himself instead and nothing happens.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — no discrimination whatsoever. He referenced customer history on Call 7, which produced nothing, and referenced none on Call 8, which flipped. This step is not the variable for him.
  • Welcome: Strong on both sides, and notably strongest on a no-move: Call 1 carries the fullest five-star seed of the week and ends with a callback. Expectations are not what he is missing.
  • Assessment: Strong across the board, with the deepest work on Call 5 — a 792-turn call covering three systems, ductwork and damper motors that ended in a financed replacement. The one call where Assessment genuinely thinned out, Call 7, is also the one he abandoned.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Weak on no-moves — the entire story. Call 6's let me make a call and I'll give you a call back to let you know what we got versus Call 4's Email and we will go from there are the same tech, ninety minutes apart in the same week.
  • Deliver: Moderate on both sides. Call 5 is the exception that proves what he can do — install window agreed, floors and tarping addressed unprompted (in case we damage your floors, that's all on us, I promise you) — while every other call ends at the signature page.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same tech, same voltage-switch teaching, same priced option sheet — but two different endings, and the fork is a phone call. On Call 6 a customer asks whether replacement makes more sense and Andrew is on the line to his manager within two turns, then back to the customer with times, then booked for Saturday 8–10; on Call 8 he makes the office call while the decision-maker is still on speaker, and on Call 5 he takes it all the way to a financing application and an install window for the next morning. On Call 1 he presents two systems, a $433 repair and a $12,000 replacement and ends with I'll be giving you guys a call tomorrow afternoon; on Call 2 he presents $1,300 and $900 of immediate work and ends with I'll give you a call later tonight; on Call 4 he ends with Email and we will go from there. The variable is not the diagnosis, the rapport or the sheet — all three are present on every call. It is whether he dialled the office before he packed the ladder.

Pre-call parts and equipment prep is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — the missing ladder on Call 7 cost one maintenance visit, whereas the un-dialled phone on Calls 1 through 4 is the entire difference between a 43% handoff week and a much better one.
