Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Joe hands off Option C on 39% of his scoreable calls (7 of 18) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 18), and his defining strength is that he answers the repair-versus-replace question with arithmetic the customer can check rather than an opinion (Strong) — Call 7: it's going to come down to dollars and cents and the warranties, right? We can take care of the immediate repairs and take care of the compressors. Those are going to come with a 1-year parts and labor warranty. So you put in, you know, $8,600 into the system to prolong it, but it only guarantees you a year. His single biggest gap is that the same clarity never turns into a request (Weak) — eleven of his eighteen calls end in warm, unhurried goodbyes with nothing asked for, and Call 18 substitutes his own diary for a decision: So I'll reach out to you on Monday… we'll get you guys on schedule and then we'll go from there. The pattern is a technician customers plainly trust — Call 8's household jokes that he has to leave his hat behind if they sign — who gets the specialist booked on seven calls and asks for nothing on the other eleven. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: in the Decision step, follow the warranty arithmetic with "so which option works best for you today?" and go quiet — he has already done the hard part on every one of these calls.

Strengths

1. He prices the repair-versus-replace decision in warranty terms (Strong)

Call 7's customer asks the question directly — what's your opinion if we just get the compressor replaced and the other immediate fixes done versus like a new system? — and Joe answers with arithmetic rather than advocacy: here's the kicker with that, it's going to come down to dollars and cents and the warranties, right? We can take care of the immediate repairs and take care of the compressors. Those are going to come with a 1-year parts and labor warranty. So you put in $8,600 into the system to prolong it, but it only guarantees you a year. He then gives an honest read on the equipment brand rather than a sales line. Coaching value: this is the strongest single argument any technician made this week, it costs him nothing in credibility, and it is entirely reusable — it should be written down and taught.

2. He runs whole calls in Spanish, including the technical and the political parts (Strong)

Calls 14 and 19 are conducted end to end in Spanish. Call 14 is not simple conversation — he explains ducting, unit sizing and a permitting problem dating to 1994 (la unidad todavía tiene que la mirada es del '94. Entonces la ciudad no sabe que esta mirada está existente ahorita), works through the customer's own theory that a past plumbing repair caused the damage, and hands over to a named specialist: ya viene Mr. Castillo. Call 19 closes with a small kindness in the same register. Coaching value: that call flipped, and the customer was able to interrogate the diagnosis properly — this is a capability the branch should be routing toward him deliberately.

3. He answers the "should I just wait?" objection with how pricing actually moves (Strong)

Call 12's customer asks whether it is cheaper to fix things in winter, and Joe neither dismisses it nor bluffs: a lot of homeowners believe that. They're like, hey, I'll wait until the wintertime, prices will be cheaper… You guys got to remember, manufacturers, contractors raise their prices 3 times a year. When the same customer asks whether a repair paid today would be credited against a replacement, he refuses to promise something that is not his to promise — that would be a question for them — while still reassuring: I've seen it time and time again where homeowners in your situation… Coaching value: he keeps his own credibility intact and keeps the specialist's options open, which is exactly the line the guide asks him to hold.

4. He hands the customer over rather than handing them off (Strong on wins)

On Call 1 he waits outside so the household is not disturbed, and tells the customer exactly what will happen: once they get here, I'll come inside… once the equipment specialist comes in here will knock. My ladder's still going to be right there. On Call 9 the transfer is spoken aloud by the customer — Joe's getting ready to take off, and Dan's going to go over our options — and Joe closes warmly with the advisor by name. On Call 12 he manages the sequencing honestly: this will most likely happen before I can get this done. Coaching value: the advisor inherits a warm room and a briefed customer on all seven of his flips, and the ladder is left in place every time.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Eleven calls end with a goodbye where a question belonged (Weak)

The closes are genuinely warm and entirely empty. Call 15: I'll take care of this for you guys, I'll throw it on top for you guys, and you guys have a wonderful rest of your day. Call 17: Have a wonderful day. Nice meeting you. Call 16: All right, all right, thanks for coming out. Absolutely, you take care. Call 13: Absolutely, it was a pleasure. Take care. None of the four contains a request of any kind. Impact: this is the whole of an 0% Repair-Won rate across the second-largest sample in the rollup — on Call 8 a household liked him enough to joke if we contract with you, you have to leave him your hat, and left without being asked for anything.

Gap 2 — He books himself when the customer needs someone booked (Moderate)

Call 18 ends with his own calendar rather than the specialist's: So I'll reach out to you on Monday… we'll get you guys on schedule and then we'll go from there. Call 3 routes it to the office (Yeah, I'll have them text it to you) for a customer who had just explained he does not use email. Call 11 ends with the decision-maker absent and a call promised — I'll give Adam a call and go over everything with him. Contrast Call 7, where the same moment produces a signature: an autograph here to get the equipment specialist out. Impact: three calls with live interest produced a follow-up owned by Joe rather than an appointment owned by the customer, and none of the three appears as a lead.

Gap 3 — No Agenda Card and no five-star seed anywhere (Weak)

No recording this week contains the expectations block, the approval box or the five-star line, and none contains a review ask. Call 10 comes closest — Give us some time to take a look at everything, and then we'll get you some answers — but there is no timing, no approval promise and no standard set. Impact: customers repeatedly close warmly (All right, thanks, Joe, Call 13; Thank you so much, Call 15) and there is nothing in place to convert that into a review or into permission to present.

Gap 4 — Frustration recorded immediately after a customer goodbye (Moderate)

Call 11 closes courteously — I buttoned everything up and I set the thermostat back up… If for any reason I got to come back inside, I'll knock — and the very next words on the recording are F*****' waste of time. Call 10's tail carries an exchange with a colleague that includes an argument about a diagnosis. Call 13 has him telling a customer he has been working since 6 o'clock this morning and will go until they send me home. Impact: minor relative to some peers, but these are retained records, and Call 11's line lands seconds after the customer's own thanks.

Gap 5 — He explains the economics and then does not use them (Moderate)

Call 7's warranty argument is the best in the week and it is delivered as information: after $8,600 into the system to prolong it, but it only guarantees you a year, the next move is I'm going to leave this here for you guys to read over. Okay, I'll be right back. Call 12 builds the same case about price rises and ends what questions do you guys have for me right now? Impact: both customers had just been given a reason to act and were handed a document instead of a choice; Call 7 flipped anyway, Call 12 flipped anyway, and neither produced any interim repair.

Gap 6 — The absent decision-maker is worked around rather than reached (Moderate)

Call 11's owner, Adam, is not present and the resolution is a phone call after the fact. Call 3's customer wants everything by text because he distrusts email, and the answer is to pass it to the office. Call 18's household is available and relaxed — that's why when you ask me what I'm doing as well, like nothing — and still ends with a Monday callback. Impact: on Call 18 in particular the customer was free, engaged and in front of him, which is the easiest possible condition for booking a specialist there and then.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether a specialist was physically coming: on Calls 1, 7, 9, 12 and 14 an advisor is en route, expected within the hour or already in the house, and on Calls 3, 8, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17 and 18 the visit ends on a courteous goodbye.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — the order-of-work framing on Call 10 is his most structured opening and appears on flips and no-moves alike. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides, with no Agenda Card on either. Whatever is separating his calls, it is not expectations.
  • Assessment: Strong throughout, and strongest on Call 7 and Call 12 — both flips — but Call 17, a 7,458-word no-move, contains a full evaluation too.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Weak — the whole story. Call 7's an autograph here to get the equipment specialist out against Call 18's I'll reach out to you on Monday.
  • Deliver: Strong on both sides. Attics buttoned up, thermostats reset, rubbish carried away and light bulbs changed on the calls that produced nothing just as much as on the ones that flipped.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same warranty arithmetic, same care on the way out — but two endings, and the fork is whether a signature came out. On Call 7 the customer asks repair or replace, gets the $8,600-for-one-year answer, and the next thing on tape is an autograph here to get the equipment specialist out; on Call 12 the specialist is here in 40 minutes and Joe manages the sequencing honestly while they wait; on Call 1 he waits outside with the ladder still up so the advisor can walk straight in. On Call 18 an available, relaxed household is told I'll reach out to you on Monday; on Call 8 a family joking about signing gets You guys take care, okay?; on Call 15 the visit ends with him carrying away their rubbish and wishing them a good day. The variable is not the diagnosis, the honesty or the warmth — Call 17 and Call 12 contain equally thorough work. It is whether anything was put in front of the customer to sign.

Beyond the Guide

He is a fully bilingual technician doing technical and regulatory work in Spanish

Call 14 covers duct sizing, a 1994 permit that the city has no record of, and a customer's own hypothesis about a prior plumbing repair — all in Spanish, all at a level that let the customer push back and be answered. Call 19 ends with him offering to change light bulbs before he leaves. Coaching implication: this is a scheduling asset, not a personality trait — Call 14 flipped, and it is worth knowing how many Spanish-preferred households are currently being routed to technicians who cannot do this.

His best sales argument is being used as a handout

The $8,600 into the system to prolong it, but it only guarantees you a year framing (Call 7) and the manufacturers, contractors raise their prices 3 times a year answer (Call 12) are both stronger than anything in the option sheet, and both are followed by him leaving the room. Coaching implication: these two lines belong in the team's objection library regardless of what happens with his own close rate — nobody else produced anything as concrete this week.

He is running long days and saying so to customers

Call 13: we work well past 8:30 if we're busy. Go until they send me home, and since 6 o'clock this morning, which the customer answers with Wow. All right, you take it easy. Nineteen recordings in five working days is the highest call count in the window. Coaching implication: worth checking against the schedule independently; it is also the most plausible explanation for eleven closes that are warm, quick and empty.

Customers offer him things, which is a signal worth using

Call 8's household jokes that a contract means he leaves his hat; Call 17's leaves him a drink by the flowers; Call 5's asks him to email a receipt before he offers. Coaching implication: these are all moments where the customer has already decided they like him — the ask that follows costs nothing socially and is currently never made.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Follow the warranty arithmetic with a question. On Call 7 Joe delivered the best repair-versus-replace answer of anyone's week and then said I'm going to leave this here for you guys to read over. The next sentence should be "so which option works best for you today?" — and then silence. Zero repairs across eighteen calls is not customers saying no; it is nobody being asked.
  • Get the signature out on the eleven, not just the seven. His flips all carry it — an autograph here to get the equipment specialist out (Call 7). His no-moves carry a goodbye instead. On Call 18 the customer was free, at home and enjoying himself, and got I'll reach out to you on Monday.
  • Set the agenda at the door and ask for the review at the end. Neither appears anywhere in nineteen recordings. Customers close warmly with him on almost every call — All right, thanks, Joe — and there is nothing in place to convert it. The seed at the start is what makes the ask at the end fair.

The frustration audible at the end of Call 11 is worth a quick word, but it is a distant fourth — it came after a call where the decision-maker was never present, which was not his doing, whereas the eleven empty closes are entirely within his control and are the whole of his zero.
