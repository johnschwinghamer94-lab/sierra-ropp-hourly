Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Robert hands off Option C on 67% of his scoreable calls (8 of 12) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 12), and his defining strength is that he refuses to make the decision for the customer and says so out loud (Strong) — Call 1, asked point-blank whether the system needs replacing: I don't want to just say replace it, you know. I want to have you share so you can have come up with your own decision. So you can see. He is equally disciplined about staying out of the advisor's territory — Call 8, on replacement scope: that's not my avenue, but for sure they would be replacing everything — which is exactly the SILO process and is producing one of the best flip rates on the roster. His single biggest gap is that the same restraint is applied to Option A, which he builds and then never asks for (Weak) — Call 3 ends I'm going to go out to the van, give me about 20 minutes or so. I'm going to write up the report and I'm going to bring it to you guys, Call 8 the same (give me about 20, 25 minutes), and across twelve scoreable calls not one Immediate or Future repair is ever put to a customer as a decision. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: come back from the van with a recommendation rather than a report — the trust that produces eight flips a week is more than enough to carry a direct ask on the repair.

Strengths

1. He refuses to make the decision for the customer, and tells them that is deliberate (Strong)

Call 1's customer asks the question every technician wants: Because it is a full replacement, right? Or, okay, can it be fixed? Robert will not take the shortcut: I don't want to— I'm gonna put the report together… I don't want to just say replace it, you know. I want to have you share so you can have come up with your own decision. So you can see. That call flipped. Call 3 is the same restraint under pressure — asked to rate a control board on a 1 to 10, he answers honestly rather than usefully: that wasn't showing signs of failure. When you start to see the heat spots on it, that's showing signs of failure. I can't personally put a time limit on it. Coaching value: customers who reach their own conclusion do not need to be closed, which is why his handoff rate is what it is — this is the foundation everything below should build on rather than replace.

2. He keeps the replacement conversation in the advisor's hands (Strong)

Call 8's customer pushes hard on scope — is what's in there now able to be reused, or is it going to have to come all out?, then What comes with a new unit? — and Robert answers only what is his to answer: that's like, that's not my avenue, but for sure they would be replacing everything. Uh, the ductwork, I'm not really 100% sure of. He then describes what he does know, physically: this is the emergency pan. So you can see— can you see the drain line right here? Coaching value: this is the SILO price rule executed without being told — the upgrade number is not his, and declining it cost him nothing and gained him a flip.

3. He answers the customer's real question, however many layers deep it goes (Strong)

Call 11's customer works through a three-part problem about running one of three thermostats, and Robert stays with him through every follow-up rather than redirecting to the sheet: first the rule (if you're in cooling season, you should never put one on heat and one on cool), then the mechanism (if you turn them off, then that takes away the unit trying to work harder to supply air), then the caution that matters most (the only thing that you want to make sure is that all the vents are open… don't mess with the vents), then the airflow arithmetic. The customer closes it himself: Okay. All right, cool. Thank you. That call flipped. Coaching value: he is building the kind of authority that makes an advisor's visit welcome rather than tolerated, and it costs him nothing but patience.

4. He makes the membership feel like a relationship rather than a subscription (Strong)

Call 4 closes with something most technicians would not say out loud: thank you for being a Sam's customer. I do appreciate it. It keeps me working. It's so I can, uh, you know, provide for my family as well, followed by a joke about coming back next year for the customer's autograph. Call 14 does the same for a household that had just declined everything: I do appreciate you guys for being stand— you know, keep me working so I do what you guys say. Coaching value: he thanks the customers who bought nothing as warmly as the ones who did, which is exactly what protects a renewal — and it is the natural place to attach the review ask he currently never makes.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether the upgrade question came from the customer's own mouth: on Calls 1, 8, 11 and 13 the customer raises replacement themselves and Robert routes it to the specialist, and on Calls 4, 10, 12 and 14 he presents findings, hands over paperwork and lets the visit end.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — no discrimination. Preparation looks the same on the flips and the no-moves.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides. The invitation to watch and the thermostat courtesy appear on Calls 2 and 7 (flips) and on Call 4 (no-move) alike.
  • Assessment: Strong throughout, and strongest on a flip — Call 11's zoning walkthrough is the best teaching of his week — but Call 14 carries an equally full option discussion and produced nothing.
  • Decision: Strong versus Weak — the whole story, and it turns on who spoke first. Call 8's that's not my avenue, but for sure they would be replacing everything follows a customer question; Call 14's we just got to talk to our son is met with an invoice and a thank-you rather than a second attempt.
  • Deliver: Solid on both sides. Every call — flip or not — ends with a sincere thank-you, the paperwork emailed and no review asked for.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same patience, same refusal to oversell — but two endings, and the fork is whether the customer opened the door first. On Call 1 the customer asks it is a full replacement, right? Or can it be fixed? and Robert's restraint becomes the flip; on Call 8 the customer asks What comes with a new unit? and gets the specialist rather than a guess; on Call 13 the customer is already there — I kind of just want to go with one, but you know, I'm going to do it — and Robert answers All right, yeah, let's go ahead and set up. On Call 14 the customer's own deferral, we just got to talk to our son there, see what he says, is met with everything's going to be an invoice and a warm goodbye; on Call 12 a full visit ends on a conversation about Robert's roster; on Call 4 the close is a signature and a joke. The variable is not the diagnosis, the teaching or the warmth — those are the same on every call. It is whether anything was asked for when the customer did not ask first.

He is working an unsustainable roster and it is on tape

Call 12: I already worked. This is the 12th day in a row, so this is it, and the customer's surprised follow-up — so you get Saturday, Sunday, and then back here Monday? Coaching implication: twelve consecutive days is worth checking against the schedule independently of anything in this report; it is also the most plausible explanation for a week where five recordings stop before the presentation.

He treats being asked to rate something as a trap and answers it too carefully

Call 3's how would you rate that, like right now, like on a 1 to 10? is a customer asking to be told what to do, and Robert declines twice over — no number, no timeframe (I can't personally put a time limit on it). Coaching implication: the honesty is right and should not be traded away; what is missing is the sentence after it — what he would do about it, and by when — which is a recommendation rather than a prediction and costs him none of his credibility.

Customers keep him in conversation long past the work

Call 9's household draws him into an extended music-industry story; Call 5's runs on the same theme at length; Call 4's ends in a running joke about a future autograph. Coaching implication: customers plainly enjoy him, and the pattern is that he stays for the conversation but leaves before the ask — the same minute spent asking for the repair would cost him none of the rapport.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Come back from the van with a recommendation, not a report. Robert currently promises I'm going to write up the report and I'm going to bring it to you guys and show you everything (Call 3). The sheet should arrive with a sentence attached — this is the immediate repair, this is what it fixes, which option works best for you today — and then silence. Zero repairs in twelve calls is not customers refusing; it is the request never being made.
  • Follow the honest "I can't put a time limit on it" with what he would do about it. Call 3's customer asked for a 1-to-10 and got a genuine non-answer. Keeping the honesty and adding the recommendation — what he would fix now and what he would watch — turns the same integrity into a decision the customer can act on.
  • Plant the five-star seed in Welcome and ask for the review at the door. No recording this week has either, yet Call 4's and Call 11's customers both closed the visit thanking him at length and Call 7's offered him fruit. His membership thank-you is already the perfect place to attach it.

The five recordings that end before the presentation are worth raising with whoever owns the recording pipeline, but as a coaching item they are a distant fourth — Calls 1 and 7 flipped despite the file cutting out, so the recordings are hiding his results rather than causing them.

On one the customer said I'm going to do it and he answered let's go ahead and set up. On the other nobody said it, and nobody asked. Let him name it himself.
  • Drill the recommendation sentence (Priority 1) and the honest-plus-actionable answer (Priority 2). Role-play three scenarios straight from his week: the rate-it-for-me question (how would you rate that on a 1 to 10?, Call 3), the family deferral (we just got to talk to our son, Call 14), and the customer who already knows the system is old and expects bad news (Call 1).
  • Commit to one rule for the week: "When I come back in with the sheet, I say which repair I'd do first and why, then I ask which option works best for them today — and I don't fill the silence. I keep letting them decide; I just stop making it optional to decide at all."
