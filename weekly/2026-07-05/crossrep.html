Executive Summary

Bottom Line

Across 190 recordings and 161 scoreable calls, the team flipped 48% (77 calls) and won repairs on 4% (6 calls). Every one of the thirteen per-tech reports independently landed its decisive gap on the same step — the Decision — and on the same behaviour inside it: whether the technician put a request in front of the customer before leaving the room. Ready, Welcome and Assessment do not separate this team at all; the top and bottom thirds are indistinguishable on all three. The single company-wide finding is that seven of thirteen technicians won no repair at all this week, and in almost every case the transcripts show the Immediate option was built, priced and then either argued against by the technician or never asked for. The second company-wide finding is that only one technician on the roster harvests the five-star review seed — Dustin Romine, on at least five calls, using a sentence any of the other twelve could say tomorrow.

Company-Wide Training Gaps

1. The Immediate repair is built and then abandoned — 4% team-wide

Six approved repairs across 161 calls, and seven technicians at zero. This is not customer refusal; the transcripts show the ask does not happen. Brandon Moreno talks a $1,200 fan motor off his own sheet — it's a lot of money. You're putting almost like $6,000 to $7,000 into something that's… older equipment — and the customer replies So it was just for pricing purposes? Juan Tlatenchi converts every A into a C: before you decide you want to put money into an old equipment, you can have the price and the option of considering—, and the customer finishes the sentence. Noah Weng never asks on any of eleven recordings. Robert Silinzy brings back a report rather than a recommendation on every call. Joe Mendoza delivers the best repair-versus-replace argument anyone made — you put in $8,600 into the system to prolong it, but it only guarantees you a year — and follows it with I'm going to leave this here for you guys to read over. Company action: a single drilled sentence — state the Immediate option, say what it fixes, ask which option works best today, then stop talking — would move this number more than any other intervention available.

2. Twelve of thirteen technicians never ask for the review

The five-star seed is planted in the Agenda Card by most of the roster and harvested by one. Dustin Romine does it on at least five calls, always by quoting his own agenda back: we talked about getting you a 5-star experience on today's visit. Is it safe to say I've done that for you today?, then I'd appreciate if you leave me a review, let the office know. Every customer said yes. Cole Pantol did it once in twenty-two recordings (Or if I did a good job, leave me a review, okay? — answered I will). Nobody else did it at all, on a week where customers volunteered the sentiment unprompted repeatedly: far thorougher than anybody else (Colquitt), You're a good guy, and I'd really have you out all the time (April), more than anybody else has (Weng), did an excellent job (Trujillo). Company action: record Romine's version and make it the standard close. It costs ten seconds and the evidence that it works is already on tape eleven times.

3. The Agenda Card is a habit for six technicians and absent for the rest

Benjamin Wyllie carries it on ten of sixteen recordings, Brandon Moreno on nine of seventeen, and Dustin Romine bookends it — setting it at the door and referencing it at the close. Nathan Colquitt, Mario Castro, Robert Silinzy, Nikko April and Joe Mendoza have no full expectations block in any recording this week, and Cole Pantol has it in exactly one of twenty-two. Company action: this is the cheapest fix on the list and it is a precondition for gap 2 — without the seed at the door there is no natural moment for the ask at the end.

4. Technicians are quoting replacement pricing that is not theirs to quote

Four technicians hold the line correctly and should be quoted as the standard. Benjamin Wyllie: I just know repair costs. They don't tell us new system costs, which is why I just had to put down talking to someone about what that looks like. Robert Silinzy: that's not my avenue, but for sure they would be replacing everything. Francisco Valencia: if you want an exact number of the replacement, then we will talk to somebody else. Joe Mendoza, asked whether a repair would be credited against a replacement: that would be a question for them. Against that, Andrew Trujillo moved from just shy of $5,000 to around $7K to as high as $8,000 inside one visit; Wyllie himself asked a customer to name a budget band between $15,000 and $30,000; Brandon Moreno quoted monthly payments; Nikko April offered a customer the home-warranty route outright. Company action: the correct behaviour already exists in four technicians' own words — it needs to be published as the rule rather than left to instinct.

5. A large share of the week's Decision steps are not on tape at all

Robert Silinzy loses five of fifteen recordings before the presentation, Noah Weng four of eleven, Mario Castro three of fourteen, and Cole Pantol has six of twenty-two that never became a service call. Dustin Romine is the only technician with sixteen complete visits out of sixteen. Company action: this is limiting coaching everywhere else — several flips confirmed by tgl_truth have no observable handoff to learn from. Establish what Romine does differently with the recorder and standardise it.

6. Non-customer audio is a live exposure, not a nuisance

Across the roster, customer recordings carry: a racial slur (Castro, Call 1); sexually explicit remarks and disparagement of a named colleague (Weng, Call 5, a file with no customer on it at all); a personal telephone call of a private nature (Castro, Call 8); an entire private conversation after the visit ended (Tlatenchi, Call 3); remarks about customers — f****** attack these m************ (Wyllie, Call 5), you could put this thing in a f****** Museum (Weng, Call 9), She's not being too cooperative (Weng, Call 2); and streamed music, podcasts and adverts replacing the presentation on Colquitt's, Weng's, Valencia's and Pantol's files. Company action: these are retained, reviewable business records. This needs a single clear instruction to the whole team, and the Castro Call 1 language needs handling separately and directly.

Individual Coaching Gaps — Not Company-Wide

  • Andrew Trujillo — books himself instead of the advisor. All four no-moves end with his own callback (I'll give you a call later tonight); all three flips end with him dialling the office. Nobody else on the roster shows this pattern as cleanly.
  • Noah Weng — buys every flip with the money in the room. I'll waive the diagnostic fee… his appointment is free too… You owe me $0 today. Highest flip rate on the roster and no revenue collected on the calls that produced it.
  • Nikko April — argues the customer out of the decision she just won. Best evidence on the team (cross-visit photo comparison, 37.69, it's dropping down more and more) followed by Before you start putting $500 into here, I would visit this first.
  • Nathan Colquitt — closes with a question about questions. Did you have any questions for me? after a 174-minute evaluation; on his best call the customer proposed the appointment date.
  • Cole Pantol — twelve calls end on a pleasantry. It's a beautiful color you got on that Tahoe. He proved on Call 10 he can ask; he did it once in twenty-two.
  • Dustin Romine — the first no is the last word. Nope. You got it. If I could just get a signature. Best Welcome and Deliver on the roster, no second ask anywhere.

The Positive-Outcome Predictor Test

Testing which banded behaviours actually predicted a positive outcome this week, across all 161 calls:

  • Predictive — a physical action taken before the customer's attention moved on. Every technician's flips share one of three concrete acts: a phone call to the office made in the room (Trujillo, Moreno, Castro, April, Pantol, Mendoza), an authorisation or free-estimate signature produced immediately (Wyllie, Tlatenchi, Weng), or a named advisor with a two-hour window (Moreno's 8 to 10, 10 to 12, 12 to 2, 2 to 4, Valencia's we'll see you on Tuesday from 4 to 6). Where none of the three happened, nothing happened.
  • Not predictive — Assessment band. Eleven of thirteen band Strong, and on nine of them the single best teaching moment of the week occurs on a call that produced nothing: Colquitt's airflow walkthrough, April's photo trend, Silinzy's zoning explanation, Weng's system tour.
  • Not predictive — Welcome band. Wyllie's fullest Agenda Cards are on no-moves; Romine's five-star bookend appears on his no-moves as reliably as on his flips.
  • Not predictive — call length. The four longest calls of the week (243, 241, 200 and 174 minutes) produced two flips and two nothings. Mario Castro's best week on the roster averages the shortest visits.
  • Weakly predictive — the customer raising replacement first. On Silinzy, Valencia and Tlatenchi the flips are disproportionately customer-initiated, which flatters the rate and hides how rarely the technician opens the door themselves.

Notable Individual Strengths Worth Spreading

  • Dustin Romine — the review ask. We talked about getting you a 5-star experience on today's visit. Is it safe to say I've done that for you today? Five calls, five yeses, and the only one on the roster doing it.
  • Nikko April — proving decline across visits. Retrieving prior-visit photographs mid-call to show a reading falling: let me go back to the last visit's photos… 37.69, it's dropping down more and more. Nobody else keeps a longitudinal record.
  • Francisco Valencia — video of an intermittent fault. Here, I'll show you. I took a video of it. This third time is when it does it — customer: Oh, I did see a little flash right there.
  • Mario Castro — putting the failure in the customer's hand. Opening the unit and showing the leaked refrigerant on the coil: you see how shiny it is on this end right here? That's all refrigerant.
  • Brandon Moreno — booking against Sierra's own weakness. They have 2-hour windows, because I know for us it was like an 8-hour window. It sucks. But for them, it's a 2-hour window.
  • Nathan Colquitt — the doctrine, stated correctly. Coaching a colleague on a live call: don't replace it, just try to stick with repairing so you can help them out. And then at the end, that's when you want to let them know… that way they don't think you're just trying to sell them something.
  • Joe Mendoza — repair-versus-replace as arithmetic. You put in $8,600 into the system to prolong it, but it only guarantees you a year.
  • Cole Pantol — holding the entry policy under pressure. Refusing three times to enter a familiar customer's empty home: I can't come in without someone being 18 on site.

Management Action Summary

  • Drill one sentence, team-wide, this week: "Here's the Immediate repair, here's what it fixes — which option works best for you today?" followed by silence. Seven technicians at zero repairs is a training gap, not thirteen individual coaching problems.
  • Adopt Romine's review close as the standard exit and record it. Twelve technicians are planting a seed they never harvest, on calls where customers are already volunteering the praise.
  • Publish the replacement-pricing rule in the four technicians' own words (Wyllie, Silinzy, Valencia, Mendoza) rather than as policy language. Four are already correct; four are crossing the line.
  • Issue one clear instruction on recordings — stop at the customer goodbye, no music during the presentation — and handle the Castro Call 1 language separately and directly.
  • Fix the data before comparing anyone again. Juan Tlatenchi's seven recordings carry no ServiceTitan job number at all, so his four flips — the joint second-best rate on the roster — are invisible to tgl_truth and would read as zero in any ServiceTitan-only report.
  • Look at dispatch, not technicians, for a share of the no-moves. Absent or unreachable decision-makers account for a meaningful part of Trujillo's, Wyllie's, Colquitt's and Pantol's week — trusts, rentals, out-of-state owners and one household with nobody over eighteen at home.
