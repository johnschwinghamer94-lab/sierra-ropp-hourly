Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Nathan hands off Option C on 33% of his scoreable calls (3 of 9) and wins repairs on 11% (1 of 9), and his defining strength is that he understands the flip better than he executes it — on Call 5, coaching a colleague through a live call, he lays out the correct SILO sequence unprompted (Strong): always just try to— don't replace it, just try to stick with repairing so you can help them out… And then at the end, that's when you want to let them know like, oh, doesn't make sense to you? Then we can always go this route… that way they don't think you're just trying to sell them something. His single biggest gap is that he presents beautifully and then waits for the customer to ask for the appointment (Moderate) — on Call 2 he lays out $1,044 immediate and a $6,500 compressor, and it is the customer who proposes the date (How about next week 6, he has to build. How about the 8th? July 8th?); on Call 12 a customer volunteers love to get the d*** thing off the roof and nothing is booked at all. The pattern is a technician whose evaluation earns him the right to ask — Call 3's customer says far thorougher than anybody else — and who then does not ask. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: in the Decision step, after the options are on the table, propose two specific advisor windows out loud before the customer speaks — the diagnosis and the trust are already doing their job.

Strengths

1. He can articulate the flip doctrine better than anyone on the team (Strong)

Call 5 catches Nathan mid-shift coaching another technician through a live call, and what comes out is the SILO sequence stated correctly and completely: they're trying to repair those stuff, always just try to— don't replace it, just try to stick with repairing so you can help them out. And then at the end, that's when you want to let them know like, oh, doesn't make sense to you? Then we can always go this route… that way they don't think you're just trying to sell them something. He also solves the practical problem on the same call — an out-of-town homeowner who cannot sign — by routing it through the office: Ryan's gonna send her all email information. So make sure all the emails correct. Coaching value: the understanding is not the missing piece for this technician, which changes what the coaching should be — it is a rehearsal-and-habit problem, not a knowledge one.

2. He shows the customer what their membership is actually saving them (Strong)

On Call 2 the pricing is not just stated, it is proved: I'll actually be able to show you the membership pricing versus the regular pricing as well… So it's going to be $1,044, and it looks like they'd be taking $183 off of that, essentially, and then That's already subtracted on as well for you. Call 3's membership customer is reassured the same way at the close: everything is taken care of as far as the price… there's no charge for you, not at all. Coaching value: the membership stops being a subscription and becomes a discount the customer can see on the page, which is the retention half of the SILO job done properly.

3. He never leaves without watching the system run (Strong)

This is the most consistent behaviour in his entire week and it appears on every call. Call 4: I just gotta grab my ladder. I like to make sure that things are running for you before they leave. Call 10: I'll just be in the backyard for one second to make sure it's, uh, running properly out there before I head out for you. Call 12: if you could drop it a couple more degrees for me one more time, uh, cuz I like to— before I leave, I like to make sure everything's running how it should. Call 7 resets a thermostat to the customer's exact prior setting. Coaching value: this is "left it better than found" executed literally on every visit, and it is the reason customers like Call 3's tell him he is more thorough than anyone they have had.

4. He takes a process complaint seriously instead of defending the company (Strong)

Call 4's customer objected at length to being asked to sign digitally without a printed copy — the procedure is not good. Nathan neither argued nor dismissed it: I completely understand. I'll do my best to mitigate that and make it right for you, sir… let me give them a call, then I'll make sure I give that feedback to the office so that we can be better, and finally an honest reassurance rather than a denial — from my personal experience, I haven't had any instance where this comes back and is saying that you are obligated to do anything. Coaching value: he defused a complaint that was already headed to Costco (I'll give it feedback also to Costco) without conceding anything untrue, which is a genuinely hard thing to do well.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

The presentation ends the same way on almost every call: Call 2, The price on that is $1,044. Okay. Did you have any questions about that?, and again after the compressor, Do you have any—. Call 3, after a two-and-a-half-hour evaluation: that would be $1,044 for that motor. Did you have any questions for me? On his single best call the appointment came from the other side of the table — the customer, not Nathan, said How about the 8th? July 8th? Impact: five no-moves this week all followed a complete, well-priced option set; on Call 3 a long-standing membership customer who volunteered I like the work that you guys have done was never asked to approve the $1,044 or to see an advisor.

Impact: the customers most likely to write a five-star review this week — the ones who called him more thorough than anyone — were never asked.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether somebody other than Nathan pushed for the next step: on Calls 2, 5 and 6 the customer, the office or a colleague drove the appointment, and on Calls 3, 4, 7, 10 and 12 an equally good evaluation ended with a question about questions.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — the history check on Call 11 was as good as anything on his flips, and Call 11 produced nothing. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides. No expectations block anywhere, flips and no-moves alike, so it neither helps nor explains the split.
  • Assessment: Strong throughout and strongest on a no-move — Call 3's walkthrough is the best of his week and it converted nothing. His teaching is a constant, not a discriminator.
  • Decision: Moderate versus Weak — the whole story. Call 2 ends with a date because the customer offered one; Call 12 ends with we're there for you after the customer described the system he wanted.
  • Deliver: Strong on both sides. Every call — won or lost — ends with the unit running, the thermostat reset and the tarp cleared.

Beyond the Guide

He is training other technicians on live calls

Call 5 is, in substance, a coaching session delivered from a customer's driveway: the correct order of options, the reason for it (that way they don't think you're just trying to sell them something), and a workaround for an out-of-state homeowner who cannot sign. He also tracks his own conversion aloud on Call 6 — Wow, I flipped 50% today. Coaching implication: a technician who can explain the doctrine this cleanly is a training asset; the obvious move is to give him that role formally rather than letting it happen on live recordings.

The digital-signature process is generating friction he has to absorb

Call 4's customer spent the end of a 149-minute visit objecting to it: it's not fair for the customer to sign something that they don't really have to read all those long things, and that's not good, and I'll give it feedback also to Costco. Nathan's private summary afterwards is short: Last thing we need is a callback. Coaching implication: this is a process complaint from a Costco-referred customer, not a technician failure, and it belongs with whoever owns the signature flow — his handling of it was the best available.

He explains the refrigerant transition without using it as a scare

Call 12's customer arrives sceptical, having been told to buy before the changeover: everybody's like, oh, put in this before the new refrigerant comes out, and I'm like, that makes no sense. Nathan agrees with the customer's logic and explains the real constraint instead — the 410A coils are so expensive and hard to source at times that we've talked a lot about pulling the thing off the roof and going to a split AC. Coaching implication: he wins the argument by conceding it, and the customer immediately describes the system he would buy — that is the exact moment an appointment should have been proposed.

Customers keep asking for him back

Call 7 ends with Nathan hoping to return (Hopefully they send me back out here for you, so I'd love to keep taking care of you, brother) and the customer joking about the revolving door; Call 3's customer volunteers praise for the plumbing side as well, which Nathan reinforces honestly (I actually am going to have them do some work at my home pretty soon). Coaching implication: he is building account-level goodwill across trades, which is worth capturing as a request-me-back and review ask rather than leaving as sentiment.

One customer, two job numbers, one visit

Calls 9 and 10 are the same household on the same afternoon under jobs 643049048 and 643052629 — one ending in a filter purchase on the card already on file, the other in we're not going to do anything today. Coaching implication: split job numbers make a single visit read as two calls in any rate calculation; worth knowing before his conversion is compared with anyone else's.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Replace "any questions?" with two specific appointment windows. Nathan currently ends with Did you have any questions about that? (Calls 2 and 3). After the options are on the table he should say what happens next and offer two times — on Call 2 the customer did that job for him and it became one of his three flips; on Call 3 nobody did and a two-and-a-half-hour evaluation produced nothing.
  • When a customer says the word "replace", book something before the sentence ends. Call 12's love to get the d*** thing off the roof and we're thinking like 2 units are the highest-intent lines anyone said to him this week, and the reply was we're there for you. His own Call 5 advice covers exactly this moment — the option is offered at the end, once repairing has been honestly explored — and it needs a calendar attached.
  • Plant and harvest the five-star seed. His Welcome has no five-star line and no call has a review ask, yet Call 3's customer said far thorougher than anybody else and Call 7's wants him back. Adding the seed to the opening and the ask to the close costs him nothing on a week where he already earns the sentiment.

The streaming audio and colleague conversation captured on live recordings are worth a direct word, but they are a distant fourth as a sales item — they cost reviewability rather than outcomes, whereas the unasked-for close is the whole difference between a 33% handoff week and a much better one.
