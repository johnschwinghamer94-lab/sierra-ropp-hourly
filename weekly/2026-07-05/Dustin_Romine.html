Strengths

1. He harvests the five-star seed, by name, on call after call (Strong)

Every other report in this window records the same gap — the seed planted in Welcome and never picked back up. Dustin is the exception, and the mechanism is that he quotes his own agenda back: Call 5, I know we talked about during the agenda giving you a 5-star experience on today's visit. Okay, did I do that for you tonight?; Call 7, on the last thing during our agenda, I talked about getting a 5-star experience on today's visit. Safe to say I've done that for you today, followed by You leave me a review, call up the office, let them know I did. Call 16 does it after a 10,500-word visit. Call 5's customer goes further and asks how — so when you send the email, does that ask for a rating then? — and gets a straight answer. Coaching value: this should be recorded and used as the team's reference example; it is the single most copyable behaviour in the entire week's data.

2. The Agenda Card is real, not remembered (Strong)

Call 8 contains the block in full — evaluation together, report written in the van, approval before any work, and then the goal question that most technicians skip: with today's maintenance, what's your number one goal you're looking to achieve out of everything? The proof that it happens every time is in the closes: five separate calls end with him referring back to during the agenda or when I got here, I talked about. Coaching value: the bookend structure is what makes the review ask land — the customer has already agreed the standard, so being asked whether it was met is a fair question rather than a favour.

3. He holds the refrigerant law without hedging or apologising for it (Strong)

Call 6's customer asks the obvious thing — you should top off the freon though, correct? — and Dustin does not soften it: with the active leak in the system, the EPA does not allow us to do that just because we're releasing more gas. We have to get that repaired before we can get anything charged up into it. When the customer declines everything, he keeps the door open on the customer's own terms rather than pressing: Hey, 100% understand. It's got a lot of money, especially right off the bat. Yeah, biggest concern is just when eventually it does go out, you're gonna have no AC. Coaching value: he converts a legal constraint into an honest reason to plan, which is exactly the right use of it.

4. Every one of his sixteen recordings is a complete visit (Strong)

Alone among the technicians in this window, Dustin has no fragments, no files that stop as he walks to the van and no recordings that run on into unrelated audio. Sixteen recordings, sixteen visits, sixteen endings — including the closing exchange on each. Call 14 even captures the handover detail — I'll be the equipment specialist. They have all that information — and Call 13 captures him waiting on site for the advisor and looking after the customer while he waits (You want some Sprite or something to drink?). Coaching value: his week is the only fully reviewable one in the rollup, which makes him the most coachable technician on the roster and the natural benchmark for the rest.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The first "no" ends the conversation (Weak)

Call 6 is the clearest instance: after explaining that the system has an active leak, cannot legally be recharged and will fail, he asks You don't want to do anything for today?, hears Nope, and answers You got it. If I could just get a signature at the bottom just saying we talked about everything. No smaller option, no future-repair alternative, no advisor window. Call 1 ends with the customer going online to order his own filters. Call 15 ends with a membership pamphlet handed over and nothing asked for. Impact: nine of sixteen calls are no-moves, and on Call 6 the customer had a failing system, no legal path to a recharge and a technician who agreed with him inside one turn.

Gap 2 — Zero repairs across the largest sample in the window (Weak)

Sixteen complete visits and not one Immediate or Future repair approved. The pattern is that the option set is described rather than offered: Call 6's you can at least get the numbers, compare it to the replacement cost, to the repair cost, see if it makes sense is information, not a request. Call 9 ends the same way — we'll hear from you guys tomorrow… get you guys information, that way we can make that decision. Impact: he is producing the most complete, best-documented visits on the roster and collecting none of the A/B revenue that sits inside them.

Gap 3 — Assessment is thinner than his peers' (Solid, and the weakest of his five steps)

Where other technicians teach the system before naming a fault — the airflow path, the compressor as the heart, the coil demonstration — Dustin's recordings mostly move from reading to conclusion. Call 6 is his fullest and it is three sentences about the EPA. Call 3, Call 11 and Call 15 contain no presented findings at all before the close. Impact: the customers who buy from other technicians on this roster have usually just been taught something; on Dustin's no-moves there is little for the customer to have changed their mind about.

Gap 4 — The review question is phrased so it cannot be answered honestly (Moderate)

The ask itself is excellent, but the wording is closed and slightly leading — Safe to say I've done that for you today (Calls 7, 10 and 16) and It was nice to say I've done that for you today (Call 9). Every customer says yes, which is pleasant and tells him nothing. Impact: the second half of the five-star box in the guide is to find out where the experience fell short; the current phrasing collects the review and forgoes the feedback.

Gap 5 — He matches customers' language rather than setting the register (Moderate)

Call 2's customer is enthusiastic and profane about the membership price, and Dustin mirrors it back on the recording rather than staying neutral. Call 6's recording opens with a household argument in progress and he works straight through it without acknowledging or stepping away. Impact: minor relative to his peers this week, but these are retained records, and the mirroring on Call 2 is a habit that will eventually land with a customer who does not share it.

Gap 6 — Membership offered as a leaflet rather than as an option (Moderate)

Call 15 has him fetching a pamphlet and comparing a competitor's plan favourably — they charge $100 for the initial and then $20 per extra per month… that's a great deal. Always keep in the back of your head — and then leaving it at that: Here's the pamphlet, a nice little chip clip for you. Call 2's customer had signed up before he arrived and was still enthusiastic about it on the doorstep. Impact: he clearly believes in the plan and can sell it in a sentence, and on Call 15 it became a handout instead of a signup.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether an advisor was already scheduled or arriving: on Calls 7, 12, 13, 14 and 16 someone else is booked, en route or on site, and on Calls 1, 3, 5, 6, 11 and 15 the visit ends with a signature, a review ask and no next appointment.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — the filter-count check on Call 1 is his most careful preparation and Call 1 is a no-move. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Strong on both sides, and delivered in full on Calls 5, 6 and 9 — all no-moves. The Agenda Card is not what is missing.
  • Assessment: Solid on both sides. Call 6, a no-move, contains his strongest technical stand of the week; several flips contain less teaching than that.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Weak — the whole story. Call 7's I'll look forward to that visit on Tuesday against Call 6's You got it in reply to a flat no.
  • Deliver: Strong on both sides, and this is unusual — he asks for the review just as reliably on the calls that produced nothing (Calls 5 and 9) as on the ones that flipped.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same agenda at the door, same review ask on the way out — but two endings, and the fork is whether anyone else was coming. On Call 7 the visit closes with a date already in the customer's mouth (I'll look forward to that, uh, visit on Tuesday); on Call 13 he waits on site for the advisor and keeps the customer comfortable while he does (I'm sure when he gets here you'll have plenty of questions); on Call 14 he hands over cleanly to a named specialist for the following week. On Call 6 a customer with an unrepairable leak says Nope and the very next thing on tape is You got it. If I could just get a signature; on Call 15 a membership conversation ends with a pamphlet and a chip clip; on Call 1 the customer decides to order his own filters online. The variable is not the expectations, the documentation or the courtesy — those are the best on the roster and they are identical on every call. It is whether Dustin asked for anything after the first refusal.

Beyond the Guide

Across this week's rollup, every other technician plants the seed and drops it. Dustin's customers respond warmly and specifically — Call 9's answers his ask with You're incredibly special; Call 10's says Yes, yes, you have and then I will to the review; Call 5's asks how to leave one. Coaching implication: this is a team-level finding rather than an individual one. The behaviour is not hard, it is not awkward, and there is now a recording of it working eleven times in one week.

He is the only technician whose week is fully on tape

Sixteen recordings, sixteen complete visits with beginnings and endings, no fragments and no files that cut out at the van. Given that five of his colleagues lost between a third and a half of their Decision steps to recording gaps this week, the difference is worth understanding rather than assuming. Coaching implication: whatever he does with the recorder should be established and taught, because it is currently the limiting factor on coaching everyone else.

He hands out competitor economics honestly

Call 15: I had one the other day, I drove out to Pahrump. He had 6 units and a mini split… Company out in Pahrump, they charge $100 for the initial and then $20 per extra per month, offered as context for why Sierra's plan is worth having rather than as a knock. Coaching implication: customers trust a comparison that includes someone else's numbers, and he is one of the few technicians willing to give one.

He stays and hosts while the advisor travels

Call 13 has him waiting on site, managing the customer's expectations for the advisor's questions (I'm sure when he gets here you'll have plenty of questions for that) and offering a drink while they wait. Call 14 makes the handover explicit and confirms the specialist already has the file. Coaching implication: the advisor walks into a warm room with the information already transferred, which is the part of the handoff the guide is most specific about and most technicians skip.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Answer the first "no" with the smaller option, not the signature. On Call 6 a customer with an active leak said Nope and Dustin replied You got it. If I could just get a signature. The next sentence should be the Future Repairs option, or two advisor windows, or the smallest thing that keeps the system running until a decision is made. Zero repairs across sixteen complete visits is not customers refusing twice — it is nobody being asked twice.
  • Teach the fault before pricing it. His Assessment is the weakest of his five steps and the only one below his peers. Call 6's EPA explanation shows he can do it; Calls 3, 11 and 15 contain no presented findings at all. A customer who has been shown the problem has something to change their mind about when the first answer is no.
  • Open the review question instead of closing it. Safe to say I've done that for you today gets a yes every time. How did I do against what I promised you at the start? gets the same review and tells him where he lost the other nine calls. Keep the ask — it is the best thing anyone did this week — and change six words.

Mirroring a customer's language on Call 2 is worth a quick word, but it is a distant fourth — it cost him nothing this week and no customer reacted to it, whereas the unasked second question is the whole of a zero repair rate across the largest sample on the roster.
