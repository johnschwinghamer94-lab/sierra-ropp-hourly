Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Francisco hands off Option C on 71% of his scoreable calls (10 of 14) and wins repairs on 7% (1 of 14), and his defining strength is that he proves intermittent faults on video rather than asking to be believed (Strong) — Call 7, on a control board that had already tripped and reset: here, I'll show you. I took a video of it. Try this. This third time is when it does it, and the customer sees it for herself — Oh, I did see a little flash right there. His single biggest gap is that he tells customers the specialist will call them instead of booking the specialist (Moderate) — Call 13: if you want an exact number of the replacement, then, then, then we will talk, um, to somebody else, with no date attached, and Call 8 ends with him relaying to the office that she can call in earlier to speak with me to go ahead and schedule one of our equipment specialists out. Compare Call 14, his cleanest flip, where the whole thing is settled in one line: we'll see you on Tuesday from 4 to 6. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: in the Decision step, offer two specialist windows before the customer leaves the room — he is already producing more upgrade interest than almost anyone on the roster, and a meaningful share of it is currently being handed back to the office to chase.

Strengths

1. He films the fault so the customer does not have to take his word for it (Strong)

Call 7 is the hardest kind of call to sell — an intermittent failure that has already reset itself. Francisco does not describe it; he replays it: So here, I'll show you. I took a video of it. Try this. This third time is when it does it, and the customer's answer is the whole point — Oh, I did see a little flash right there. He then closes the loop on cause and safety in one turn: it tripped because it kept sparking trying to turn on until they popped the breaker, which is a safety mechanism, ending with a clear instruction — I don't recommend turning it on until that control board gets changed out. Coaching value: a customer who has watched the fault happen does not need to be persuaded, and this technique is directly transferable to every intermittent complaint the team faces.

2. He does his job properly for customers who have already said no (Strong)

Call 5's customer cuts him off before he can price anything: we're not putting any money into that. We're getting a new system. We've already decided that because we're not satisfied with it. Francisco neither argues nor gives up on the visit: Well, I still got to do my job… I'll give you the options of the repairs. Um, but at the end of the day, like I said, this is your home. And we respect whatever decision it may be. He then finishes the attic, changes the filters and writes the report anyway. Coaching value: the customer keeps a complete record and a technician who did not push, which is exactly the condition under which a replacement conversation stays with Sierra rather than moving to a competitor.

3. He defends Sierra's price on value without disparaging anyone (Strong)

Call 15: I learned you pay for what you get for, unfortunately… so if you're looking for the lowest, we're not going to be it, but if you're looking for fair pricing with great quality work, we're the number one company for them. Call 1 makes the same case to a customer actively collecting competing quotes, and adds the channel benefit rather than a scare: just to understand the other companies, when they come out, if they're going to be a lot less than us, there's usually a reason… plus Costco gives you money back. So it is going to be a good quality install through us no matter what we do. Coaching value: both customers were comparison shopping and both stayed in the conversation — this is the price objection handled with confidence rather than discounting.

4. He books the specialist as a specific appointment when he books at all (Strong on wins)

Call 14 is the model and it takes one sentence: the only thing I would need is an autograph down here from you, and then, um, we'll see you on Tuesday from 4 to 6. No hedging, no "someone will call you", and the paperwork and the date arrive together. Call 11 does the same in person — a colleague takes over the upgrade half on site (Derek will take care of the other half). Coaching value: this is what his best flips look like, and it is the difference between the ten he got and the twelve he could have had.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The specialist is promised rather than booked (Moderate)

On several calls the upgrade interest is real and the next step is left to somebody else. Call 13: I'm gonna send you this report with the ballpark and the repair cost… but if you want an exact number of the replacement, then, then, then we will talk, um, to somebody else — no date, no window. Call 8 ends with Francisco relaying the situation to the office rather than closing it in the room: Homeowner is interested in replacement options. She is a school teacher, so weekends work better for her… she can call in earlier to speak with me to go ahead and schedule one of our equipment specialists out. Call 9 ends the same way, with a callback promised for fifteen minutes later. Impact: Call 13's household explicitly wanted repair and replacement numbers side by side for a decision the customer's wife would make, and left with only half of what they asked for.

Gap 2 — Option A is presented for the record rather than for a decision (Weak)

One repair approved in fourteen calls, and the framing is consistently informational. Call 5: I'll write up the report for the repairs just in case you guys still want to go with that. Call 2 ends with a signature whose stated purpose is that nothing happened — just that we're not doing any work or setting any appointments. Call 13 promises the ballpark and the repair cost by email. Contrast Call 4, his one win, where the next step is concrete and immediate: we'll just pay you when you show up with the thermostat. Impact: a technician producing this much upgrade interest should be collecting more of the interim repair revenue that sits alongside it; on Call 6, a 10,700-word visit, nothing was decided at all.

Gap 3 — No expectations block and no five-star seed (Weak)

No recording this week opens with the Agenda Card boxes or the five-star line, and no call closes with a review ask — despite customers repeatedly giving him openings. Call 13's customer says Thank you. You saved me; Call 3's household is exchanging drinks and neighbourly concern with him at the door; Call 15's ends Cool. Okay, thank you so much, Francisco. Impact: he generates strong personal goodwill and converts none of it into the review the guide asks him to plant for at the start of every visit.

Gap 4 — Non-customer audio on customer recordings (Moderate)

Call 5's file, immediately after a courteous exchange with the homeowner, carries Why the f*** are you guys not pushing up? Call 8 has music playing loudly enough that several minutes of the transcript are song lyrics rather than the visit. Call 1's tail is a conversation with colleagues about food and coverage. Impact: on Call 8 the audio that should capture the presentation captures a playlist instead, and these are retained records that managers review.

Gap 5 — The comfort problem the customer keeps raising is diagnosed and then dropped (Moderate)

Call 10's customer describes a room that has been wrong since they moved in — this has been since we moved… my daughter's room… it gets more freezing than any other — and Francisco identifies the cause immediately (that's actually right where that unit sits, so that might be why) and then moves on to attic access. Call 6 runs 10,700 words and ends on fireworks and family without a decision. Impact: Call 10's customer was already leaning toward buying through Costco; the one problem she cared most about was solved out loud and never attached to an option or an appointment.

Gap 6 — He answers replacement cost questions himself before handing them over (Moderate)

Call 12 works through replacement economics with the customer in some detail — that's the high end, right? The high-end stuff. It might, like I said, if they have any incentives, it might be lower than that… even then you guys don't have to pay it al[l] — against the customer's own $30,000 and $7,000 figures. His own Call 13 line is the correct standard: if you want an exact number of the replacement, then we will talk to somebody else. Impact: Call 12's specialist inherits a household already anchored on a number Francisco discussed rather than one the specialist set, which is avoidable and which he demonstrably knows how to avoid.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether a specific next visit exists when he leaves: on Calls 3, 7, 11, 14 and 15 an advisor is booked, already present or already engaged, and on Calls 2, 6 and 13 the report goes to email and the next move belongs to the customer.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — the service-history recall on Call 5 is his sharpest and appears on a flip and on no-moves alike. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides. The thermostat and timing courtesies are universal; the missing expectations block is equally universal.
  • Assessment: Strong throughout. Call 10's diagnosis of a persistently cold bedroom is as good as Call 7's video, and Call 10's outcome came from the customer's own Costco plan rather than from the diagnosis.
  • Decision: Strong versus Moderate — the whole story, and it splits on one sentence. Call 14's we'll see you on Tuesday from 4 to 6 against Call 13's we will talk, um, to somebody else.
  • Deliver: Solid on both sides. Pictures, summary, restored thermostats and removed filters on every call, flip or not; no review ask on any.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same willingness to prove the fault, same refusal to oversell — but two endings, and the fork is whether a date exists. On Call 14 he closes it in a single line while collecting the signature: we'll see you on Tuesday from 4 to 6. On Call 11 he hands the customer to a colleague in the house — Derek will take care of the other half. On Call 7 he leaves a household with a live safety instruction and a decision to take to the owner. On Call 13 a customer asks for repair and replacement numbers so his wife can decide, and gets if you want an exact number of the replacement, then we will talk, um, to somebody else with nothing attached; on Call 8 the interest is real enough that Francisco reports it to dispatch, and the booking is left for the customer to phone in; on Call 2 the signature says we're not doing any work or setting any appointments. The variable is not the diagnosis, the honesty or the customer's interest — all three are strongest on some of the calls that produced nothing. It is whether he named a day before he picked up his ladder.

Beyond the Guide

Costco is doing real work in his calls and he knows how to use it

Call 10's customer states the preference outright: a friend of mine got her air conditioning through Costco last year… whatever you buy, they stand behind the product for a long time… if it's going to be similar to what I'm going to get something else, I prefer to get it through Costco. Call 1 uses the same channel as reassurance against cheaper quotes: plus Costco gives you money back. Coaching implication: a meaningful share of his pipeline arrives already trusting the channel rather than the company, which is worth knowing when his flips are compared with technicians working colder leads.

He works alongside a second technician on several calls

Call 11 hands the upgrade half to a named colleague on site (Derek will take care of the other half), and Call 1's tail has him coordinating coverage with others. Coaching implication: partnered calls appear to convert well for him, and it is worth checking whether the pairing is deliberate — if it is, it is a model; if it is accidental, it is a scheduling insight.

He manages the customer's day rather than his own

Call 9: what time do you usually take your break, if you don't mind me asking?, followed by I'll just call you in about 15-20 minutes and then we can go over it — and an explanation of why the caller ID will look wrong (we call through the office and it's like an automated…). Call 4 confirms a membership customer owes nothing before they offer to pay (I don't see anything, so you guys owe us $0). Coaching implication: this removes friction his peers create, and it is the reason customers stay on the phone with him after he leaves.

He tells customers what not to do, plainly, when safety is involved

Call 7 ends with an unambiguous instruction rather than a recommendation: I don't recommend turning it on until that control board gets changed out, having just explained that the breaker trip was the safety mechanism doing its job. Coaching implication: this is the one place in his week where he is fully directive, and it is worth pointing out to him that the same directness applied to the option sheet is all that stands between a 7% and a much higher repair rate.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Name two specialist windows before leaving the room. Call 14 shows he can do it in one line — we'll see you on Tuesday from 4 to 6 — while Call 13's customer, who explicitly wanted repair and replacement figures for a joint decision, got we will talk, um, to somebody else, and Call 8's booking was handed back to the office. Ten flips came from fourteen calls; two of the four that did not had live upgrade interest and no date.
  • Ask for the immediate repair the way he asked on Call 4. We'll just pay you when you show up with the thermostat is his only repair of the week, and it converted because there was a concrete next action. Elsewhere the repair is framed as a record — I'll write up the report for the repairs just in case you guys still want to go with that (Call 5) — which is an invitation to do nothing.
  • Open with the expectations block and close with the review ask. Neither appears anywhere this week, on a technician whose customers say things like Thank you. You saved me (Call 13). The seed at the start makes the ask at the end natural, and he is currently doing the hard part — earning it — and skipping both ends.

The music and colleague audio landing on customer recordings is worth a quick word, but it is a distant fourth — it costs him reviewability, principally on Call 8, whereas the specialist appointment he does not book is the difference between his rate and the top of the roster.
