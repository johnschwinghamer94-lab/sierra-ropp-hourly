Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Cole hands off Option C on 33% of his scoreable calls (6 of 18) and wins repairs on 6% (1 of 18), and his defining strength is that he holds company policy under direct pressure from a customer who has every reason to expect an exception (Strong) — Call 6, refused entry to a familiar customer's home with no adult present: it's a company policy that if there's items in the home that belong to you guys, I can't come in — there's no one over 18 here, repeated three times as the customer pushed, and closed politely — I'm very sorry about that. I mean, we would have to reschedule. His single biggest gap is that twelve of his calls end with a courtesy where a question belonged (Weak) — Call 11 closes on the customer's car (It's a beautiful color you got on that Tahoe), Call 12 on you enjoy the rest of your day, okay?, Call 19 on have a great day — none carrying a request of any kind. He proved on Call 10 that he knows how to ask: Or if I did a good job, leave me a review, okay? — and did it once in twenty-two recordings. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: in the Decision step, put one request in front of every customer before the goodbye — a repair, an appointment or a review — because his week is currently thirteen warm exits and five bookings.

Strengths

1. He holds the entry policy when a customer pushes him to break it (Strong)

Call 6 is a short recording and the most impressive thing anyone did this week. A long-standing customer, comfortable and familiar, offers to let him work in an empty house: you've been there before and we're very comfortable with you being there, so I'll let you in the garage and then you can just go on through. Cole declines, explains, and does not waver as the customer presses: it's a company policy that if there's items in the home that belong to you guys, I can't come in — there's no one over 18 here, then we would have to reschedule because I can't come in without someone being 18 on site, and finally I'm very sorry about that. He then arranges for the office to call them back on the right number. Coaching value: he lost the call and protected the company and himself; this is exactly the judgement that should be praised out loud so other technicians know the answer is supported.

2. He books the advisor and then asks the customer how they want to be looked after (Strong on wins)

Call 14 does the whole sequence properly and finishes with a question most technicians never think to ask: We'll have Dan on the way. I just got to call the office and let them know to make a ticket for him. Just run this back out to my van, make that phone call. When I come back in and hang out with you, or I can be in the van, whatever works for you, then When he's here, is it okay to just come on in or do I need to knock first? Call 4 layers a booked specialist on top of approved work — they'll be here in a little while, and we'll see you in a few hours. Coaching value: the handoff is run to the guide's standard, and giving the customer control of how the wait is spent is a small, distinctive touch worth keeping.

3. He restores the house precisely and tells the customer he has (Strong)

Call 15: let me just hop up there, put your thermostat back down to the way it was… 78 okay up there? You want to lower it? Call 13 closes with thermostats are back to the way you had them, so everything's back to normal. Call 19 accounts for a door (I closed the door to the AC) and Call 5 for the gate. Coaching value: customers on maintenance plans notice being left exactly as they were far more than they notice the evaluation, and this is a large part of why his closes are so consistently warm.

4. He can ask for the review, and it works instantly (Strong on wins)

Call 10 ends with one added sentence: Or if I did a good job, leave me a review, okay?, answered I will. No hesitation from either side, no awkwardness, ten seconds. Coaching value: it is already in his repertoire and it converted first time — the coaching is not to teach it but to make it happen on the other twenty-one recordings.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Twelve calls end on a pleasantry instead of a request (Weak)

The exits are warm and completely empty. Call 11: It's a beautiful color you got on that Tahoe… Stay warm. Call 12: you enjoy the rest of your day, okay? Call 7: It's nice meeting you, and you enjoy the rest of your night. Call 16, after 10,114 words: Not a problem. You have a good one. Call 19: have a great day. None contains a repair ask, an advisor offer or a review request. Impact: this is the whole of a 6% Repair-Won rate — his customers like him at the door and are asked for nothing before it closes.

Gap 2 — The Agenda Card appears once in twenty-two recordings (Weak)

Call 20 has it complete, including the approval box and the five-star seed — won't do any work without the approval. If we get that, we'll get started as soon as today. Above all, we're here to give you 5 stars — and no other recording this week contains it. Impact: without the seed there is no natural moment to ask for the review, which is why Call 10's ask reads as an afterthought (Or if I did a good job…) rather than the harvest of something promised at the door.

Gap 3 — He books himself for tomorrow instead of the advisor for today (Moderate)

Call 8 runs 6,703 words and ends Okay, I'll call you tomorrow. Call 2 leaves the next move entirely with the customer: just give us a call and we'll come back out. Compare Call 14, where the same moment becomes We'll have Dan on the way. I just got to call the office. Impact: two of his longest no-moves ended with a future action owned by Cole or by the customer rather than an appointment owned by the calendar, and neither appears as a lead.

Gap 4 — Six of twenty-two recordings never became a service call (Moderate)

Call 6 could not be entered, Call 9 reached the wrong contact (Okay, we'll contact them then. Gotcha. Sorry about that), Call 18 ends as work begins, Call 20 is a 444-word visit, Call 22 contains no customer at all, and Call 3's productive portion is very short. Impact: more than a quarter of his week produced no assessable outcome, which is the highest churn on the roster — and Calls 6 and 9 in particular are dispatch and access failures rather than anything he did.

Gap 5 — Assessment is thinner than his peers' (Solid, and the weakest of his five steps)

Where other technicians on this roster teach the airflow path, compare readings across visits or hand the customer the failed part, Cole's recordings mostly move from evaluation to signature. Call 16 runs 10,114 words and Call 21 runs 10,958, and neither contains a presented technical finding in the passages read. His best explanatory moment this week — Call 21's account of tree roots lifting a driveway — is not about HVAC at all. Impact: the customers who buy elsewhere on this roster have usually just been taught something; on Cole's twelve no-moves there is little for a customer to have changed their mind about.

Gap 6 — Non-customer audio, including a full colleague call logged against a customer job (Moderate)

Call 22 is billed to a customer job number and contains only a conversation with a colleague about standby and dispatch. Call 21 ends mid-visit with What's up, bro? as a personal call begins. Impact: minor beside some peers, but Call 22 means a customer file carries no customer, and these are records managers review.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether Cole made the office call: on Calls 1, 4, 10, 14, 17 and 21 an advisor is booked, named or arriving, and on the twelve no-moves the visit closes with a signature, a restored thermostat and a friendly goodbye.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — scope-setting looks the same on the flips and the no-moves. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides, and the one full Agenda Card in his week (Call 20) is on a no-move. Expectations are not what separated them.
  • Assessment: Solid on both sides. Calls 16 and 21 are his two longest visits — one flipped, one did not — and neither is carried by teaching.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus Weak — the whole story. Call 14's I just got to call the office and let them know to make a ticket for him against Call 8's Okay, I'll call you tomorrow.
  • Deliver: Strong on both sides. Thermostats restored, gates closed and the signature joke land identically whether the call produced anything or not.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same care putting the house back exactly as he found it, same easy warmth at the door — but two endings, and the fork is whether he phoned the office. On Call 14 he does it in front of the customer and then manages the wait on her terms: We'll have Dan on the way… When I come back in and hang out with you, or I can be in the van, whatever works for you. On Call 4 he collects $350 of approved work and hands over to a specialist arriving the same afternoon. On Call 10 he books, and then adds the one sentence missing everywhere else: Or if I did a good job, leave me a review, okay? On Call 8, after nearly seven thousand words, the close is Okay, I'll call you tomorrow; on Call 2 it is just give us a call and we'll come back out; on Call 11 it is a compliment about the customer's car. The variable is not the diagnosis, the courtesy or the goodwill — those are constant and they are genuinely good. It is whether anyone other than Cole was scheduled to come back.

Beyond the Guide

He turned down work rather than break a safeguarding rule, and did it kindly

Call 6's customer was familiar, trusting and inconvenienced, and Cole still would not enter the home unsupervised. He absorbed the awkwardness himself — I'm very sorry about that — and then made sure the office had the right number to rebook. Coaching implication: this deserves to be named in front of the team, because a technician who is unsure whether the company will back him is a technician who eventually says yes.

The signature routine is doing real work

I wasn't screaming at you, yelling at you, punching holes in the wall, something like that appears on Calls 3, 13 and 15 and gets a laugh every time; Call 15's customer is joking about her own signature by the end. Coaching implication: he has a reliable, disarming moment at exactly the point in the visit where the review ask belongs — Call 10 proves it takes one extra sentence.

Twenty-two visits in five days, and a quarter of them never started

No adult on site (Call 6), the wrong contact (Call 9), a visit that ends as work begins (Call 18), a job number carrying only a colleague call (Call 22). Call 22's audio has him describing being put on standby and repeatedly called about coverage. Coaching implication: this is a dispatch and confirmation pattern rather than a performance one, and it is worth quantifying before his conversion is compared with technicians running twelve or fourteen visits.

He answers questions outside his trade rather than deflecting them

Call 21's customer worries about a tree damaging his property and gets a considered answer from experience — I've seen it, but on other homeowners' homes, the roots can come by and kind of move up the driveway. But it was a really old home, so the tree's been there a long time. Call 5 hands a plumbing question on properly (you'll probably get a call later on today about the plumbers). Coaching implication: customers ask him things because he is easy to talk to; the same openness pointed at the option sheet is the whole of the coaching below.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Never close a call without asking for one thing. Twelve of Cole's calls end on a pleasantry — It's a beautiful color you got on that Tahoe (Call 11). Every visit should end with a repair asked for, an advisor window offered, or a review requested. He already proved the third one takes ten seconds and works first time on Call 10.
  • Make the office call in the room, not the callback promise. Call 14's I just got to call the office and let them know to make a ticket for him produced a flip; Call 8's Okay, I'll call you tomorrow after nearly seven thousand words produced nothing. Same technician, same week.
  • Deliver the Agenda Card on every call, not one in twenty-two. Call 20 has it in full, including the approval box and the five-star line. Without it at the door, the review ask at the end has nothing to attach to — which is exactly why it happened once.

The four visits that could not be started are worth raising with dispatch, but they are a distant fourth as a coaching item — Calls 6 and 9 were access and contact failures he handled correctly, whereas the twelve calls that ended with no request are entirely within his control.

Suggested Coaching-Session Agenda

(hand to tech)

  • Open with the win: "Call 6, the Duffy job. A customer you know, comfortable, offering to let you into an empty house — and you said no three times, politely, and got the office to rebook it: it's a company policy… I can't come in without someone being 18 on site. You lost the call and you did the right thing. That is exactly what we want, and you should never wonder whether we back you on it."
  • Show the pattern: "On the calls that land, you call the office in front of them: Call 14, We'll have Dan on the way. I just got to call the office and let them know to make a ticket for him. On the ones that don't, you say something nice and leave: Call 11, It's a beautiful color you got on that Tahoe. Twelve of your calls ended that way this week."
  • Contrast two calls: Put Call 14 (Dunphy) next to Call 8 (George) and have Cole describe what was different. Call 8 was longer, the customer was engaged, and it ended with I'll call you tomorrow. Call 14 ended with Dan on the way. Let him name it himself.
  • Drill the one-request close (Priority 1) and the Agenda Card (Priority 3). Role-play three scenarios straight from his week: the customer who says the replacement is unaffordable (You can't just decide to lose $25,000, Call 1), the long maintenance visit with nothing urgent found (Call 16), and the customer who wants to call you back themselves (just give us a call and we'll come back out, Call 2).
  • Commit to one rule for the week: "Nobody gets a goodbye until I've asked them for something — the repair, an appointment, or the review. And I say the agenda at the door on every single call so the review ask at the end is something they already agreed to."
