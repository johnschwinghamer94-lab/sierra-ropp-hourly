Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Mario hands off Option C on 70% of his scoreable calls (7 of 10) — the best flip rate on the roster this week — and wins repairs on 10% (1 of 10), and his defining strength is that he puts the failure in the customer's hand rather than on a screen (Strong) — Call 2, holding the coil: this is all the refrigerant that was inside your unit… you see how shiny it is on this end right here, this corner? Yeah, that's all refrigerant… From here, this is your heart. This is what pumps everything inside. So right now it has no freon inside. His single biggest gap is that the recordings keep capturing him off duty, and on one call that includes a racial slur (Weak) — Call 1 ends on one, Call 5 on profanity about a lost hose, Call 4 on profanity immediately after a courteous goodbye, and Call 8's file contains a personal telephone call with no customer content at all. None of it is aimed at a customer, and all of it sits on customer records that managers review. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: on the sales side, ask for the immediate repair before introducing the advisor — seven flips and one $557 part is a technician whose Option A is being skipped, not refused; and on the conduct side, stop the recording when the visit ends.

Strengths

1. He hands the customer the evidence instead of describing it (Strong)

Call 2 is the best piece of physical proof anyone produced this week. Rather than reporting a low charge, Mario opens the unit and shows the customer where the refrigerant went: as I was hooking everything up, I recorded a video too, but this is all the refrigerant that was inside your unit… It comes here, it looks wet, right? And then down here, that's all that refrigerant that was inside your unit, then you see how shiny it is on this end right here, this corner? … All this right here that's shiny. The customer moves straight from doubt to understanding — Really? — and then to her own follow-up question. Coaching value: a customer who has seen the failure argues about the price of fixing it far less, and this is the single most transferable technique in his week.

2. He explores the repair honestly before offering the upgrade (Strong)

Mario runs the guide's sequence in the right order. Call 7: let's see what it costs to repair it first. Okay. Um, just to see what we can do to help out. Call 4 frames the advisor as information rather than commitment: with those guys, usually what they do, when they go out here, for them, it's no cost, right? No agreement, right? It's just to get all your options, and then you'll be able to actually have a full conversation… we want you to have all your information, that way you know what's going on, what you're up against. Call 2 offers the warranty route before the sale: I'll look at the warranties, I'll check it right now. Coaching value: seven flips in a week from a technician who never leads with replacement is proof the honest order converts better, not worse — this is worth showing the rest of the team.

3. He stays for the advisor and leaves his own equipment set up (Strong on wins)

On Call 13 the handoff runs exactly as the guide describes it: I'll wait for him to get here. I still have my ladder up there. I'll just let him use my stuff… that way he's not here all day, and then he offers to work the door — whenever he gets here, do you want me to knock on the door, let him in? Call 6 is the same: my ladder's still up there, so they'll just be able to peek up there, and he waits outside rather than interrupt a customer's meeting — I'll wait for them outside that way they don't just… not interrupt your meeting. Coaching value: leaving the ladder and drop cloth in place and staying until dismissed is written into the guide and almost nobody does it; he does it without being asked.

4. He answers the price objection with the customer's own priorities (Strong)

Call 14 is his only repair win and it converts in two turns because the number is delivered plainly and immediately: it would be $557, answered Yeah, so thank you, that's reasonable, and then Mario keeps the frame on the outcome rather than the invoice — let's try to get this up and running first and see what happens… I got faith in it too. I hope it's just that. The customer, who had just described paying $1,500–$1,600 elsewhere, signs on the spot. Coaching value: this is what his Decision step looks like when he simply states the price and asks — and it is the one call this week where he did.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Non-customer audio on customer recordings, including a racial slur (Weak)

This is the most serious item in his week and it is not a sales issue. Call 1's recording ends with Mario using a racial slur in casual conversation with a colleague. Call 4 ends with profanity moments after a courteous goodbye to the customer (Yeah, sounds good, sir. What the f***?). Call 5 ends the same way over a mislaid hose. Call 11 runs into a personal call, and Call 8's file contains a personal telephone call with no customer content in it at all. Impact: none of this is directed at a customer, and none of it changed an outcome — but these are retained business records that managers review, and the Call 1 language is a conduct matter that has to be addressed on its own terms before anything else in this report.

Gap 2 — Option A is built and then skipped (Moderate)

Nine of his ten scoreable calls produced no repair approval, and the pattern is not refusal — it is that the ask does not happen. Call 2 ends with a diagnosis, a parts-availability problem (to get these parts would take about 6 to 8 weeks) and then any questions for me?, any questions your husband might have for me? — twice asking for questions, never for a decision. Call 4 ends Any questions? No, no questions. Contrast Call 14: it would be $557… I do need a signature right here, 557, just in that box. Impact: the one time he asked directly, he closed it in two turns — the other nine calls carry the same quality of diagnosis and no request attached to it.

Gap 3 — Booking himself instead of the advisor when the customer wavers (Moderate)

Call 4 has the whole advisor pitch delivered well — no cost, no agreement, full options — and then no appointment: I'll reach out to you Friday, okay? … on the phone number that we have on file. Call 5 goes further and closes the door himself: on this one I'll just be canceling this. We're not doing anything today. On his seven flips the advisor is on the way, in the diary or already at the house. Impact: these are his only two no-moves, and both are calls where the option set was complete and the next step was a callback from Mario rather than a visit from anyone.

Gap 4 — Three recordings end before the presentation (Moderate)

Calls 7, 11 and 12 all stop at the same moment — Mario heading to the van. Call 7: just give me some time. I'll be in my van, and then once I'm done here, I'll get you booked and we'll go over everything. Call 11: I'll go write the report and then I'll come back. Impact: nearly a quarter of his recordings cut out immediately before the step that decides the outcome, so the part of his work most worth reviewing — including, on Call 7, a booking he says he is about to make — is unobservable.

Gap 5 — Uncertainty offered where reassurance was asked for (Moderate)

Call 12's customer asks how long the system has and gets a genuine but unhelpful answer: you'll feel some cold air, but it's just a matter of time… or a month, or— I wouldn't be able to say. I can't, I can't tell. It's something that— you know, we've seen it go months. Call 2 does the same on parts (6 to 8 weeks) and then softens it into a joke about the Strip. Impact: both calls left the customer with a problem and no timeframe to act against, and on Call 12 the recording ends without any option ever being presented.

Gap 6 — No five-star seed and no review ask anywhere (Weak)

Unlike most of his peers, Mario's Welcome carries no five-star line in any recording this week, and no call contains a review ask — despite customers giving him unusually warm closes. Call 3's customer says Okay, appreciate it, Mario after a long personal conversation; Call 10's household ends on pets and thermostat preferences; Call 14's customer calls the price reasonable. Impact: he generates more personal goodwill than almost anyone on the roster and converts none of it into a review.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference is whether someone other than Mario is scheduled to arrive: on Calls 1, 2, 3, 6, 10 and 13 an advisor is on the way or already at the door, and on Calls 4 and 5 the next step is Mario phoning back on Friday.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides — the warranty check on Call 2 is his best preparation and the file review habit shows up equally on his no-moves. Not the variable.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides. The permission-to-re-enter courtesy is universal across flips and no-moves alike, and the expectations block is missing from both.
  • Assessment: Strong throughout. Call 12, which produced nothing, contains the heart-and-lungs model in its clearest form; Call 2, a flip, contains the coil demonstration. Teaching is a constant, not a discriminator.
  • Decision: Strong versus Moderate — the whole story, and it splits on who has the calendar. Call 13's I'll wait for him to get here against Call 4's I'll reach out to you Friday.
  • Deliver: Strong on both sides. Every call ends with the thermostat restored to the customer's preference and the old filters taken away, won or lost.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same heart-and-lungs model, same care over the thermostat on the way out — but two endings, and the fork is whether anybody else was scheduled to arrive. On Call 13 he holds the site for the advisor and lends him the ladder (I'll just let him use my stuff… that way he's not here all day); on Call 6 he waits outside so a customer's meeting is not interrupted and the customer already expects the quote visit (you'll come to quote like what it costs to replace the AC unit); on Call 10 the customer says I was prepared for it. I thought I'm on borrowed time and the route is already agreed. On Call 4 the identical advisor pitch — for them, it's no cost, right? No agreement — ends with I'll reach out to you Friday; on Call 5 he cancels the sheet himself with we're not doing anything today; on Call 2 he asks for questions twice and for a decision never. The variable is not the diagnosis, the evidence or the warmth, all of which are on every call. It is whether the next visit was booked before he packed the ladder.

Beyond the Guide

He helps a customer with something that has nothing to do with air conditioning

Call 3's customer is a fellow Marine, and Mario spends the end of the visit on the customer's disability claim rather than his own paperwork — writing down two organisations for him (this one's heroeservingheroes.com, and a second that helped out my buddy go from 10% to 90%), then offering something well past the job: if you ever need anything, just feel free to reach out. I'll come help you out, and I'll even go to the VA with you. I'll tell them that I'm there to help you out… we'll try to get you at the 100. The customer's answer is Okay, appreciate it, Mario. That call flipped. Coaching implication: this is the opposite of adequate friendliness and it is worth naming out loud — but it also sits alongside the conduct issue in Gap 1, and both are the same person on the same week's tape.

He solves the advisor's logistics problem before the advisor arrives

Leaving a ladder set up sounds small; on Calls 6 and 13 it is the difference between a fifteen-minute look and an hour of setup — I'll just let him use my stuff, that way he's not here all day (Call 13). He also manages the customer's day around it, waiting outside a meeting on Call 6 and offering to answer the door himself on Call 13. Coaching implication: this materially raises the quality of the lead the advisor walks into, and it is invisible in any metric — worth crediting explicitly so it survives.

He tells customers what the industry did, not what they did wrong

Call 2 explains a stranded system without blaming anyone: the thing is with this one, they phased out all the stuff that's for this refrigerant here. They phased it out last year… to get these parts would take about 6 to 8 weeks. Call 12 traces a customer's own reported symptom back to its cause rather than starting from a part: I just traced back from the ductwork that you were telling me, ductwork that's making too much noise, usually goes back to the motor. Coaching implication: starting from what the customer already noticed is why his diagnoses are accepted without argument; it is the strongest foundation on the team for a direct ask that currently does not follow it.

Nearly a third of his recordings capture the visit but not the sale

Calls 7, 11 and 12 stop as he walks to the van, and Call 9 is a 337-word fragment against a 55-minute job. On Call 7 the recording cuts out immediately after he says once I'm done here, I'll get you booked and we'll go over everything — the booking he was about to make is simply not captured. Coaching implication: this is a recording-continuity problem worth raising with whoever owns the pipeline, because it is systematically hiding his best step.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Stop the recording when the visit ends — and treat the Call 1 language as a separate, immediate conversation. Call 1 closes on a racial slur, Calls 4 and 5 on profanity seconds after a warm goodbye, and Call 8's file contains a personal call with no customer on it. None of it reached a customer's ears, and all of it is on a retained record. This is first on the list because it is the only item here that is not about selling.
  • Ask for the repair the way he asked on Call 14. It would be $557… I do need a signature right here, 557, just in that box closed his only repair of the week in two turns. Call 2 and Call 4 both end with any questions? instead — same quality of diagnosis, no request attached. Present Immediate, say the price, ask for the signature, then stop.
  • Book the advisor rather than promising a callback. Call 4 has the entire pitch — for them, it's no cost, right? No agreement — and then I'll reach out to you Friday. His seven flips all have somebody else arriving. Two windows offered out loud on Call 4 would very likely have made it eight.

The uncertainty he offers when customers ask how long they have — a month, or I wouldn't be able to say on Call 12 — is worth tightening, but it is a distant fourth: it is honest, customers do not push back on it, and it costs him far less than the ask he never makes.
