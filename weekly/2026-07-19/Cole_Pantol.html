Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Cole hands off Option C on 45% of his scoreable calls (10 of 22) and wins repairs on 18% (4 of 22), on the largest scoreable base anyone carried this week. The mechanic behind the ten handoffs is the same one every time and it is the best-executed on the roster: he does not offer the customer a Comfort Advisor, he sends for one from the driveway, names them, gives an arrival time, and stays on site to walk them in. Call 10: I hang out till he gets here. I’ll make sure that I show him everything that’s going on, that way he’s not walking in blind and everybody’s on the same page. Call 21 is the compressed version of the whole move — 841 words, a customer who only wanted a price, a signature that just states I’m okay to get him out here for you, and his name is going to be Rob… be within the hour. His routing on replacement price is correct and never wavers: I just work on the repair side. I don’t quote or give options as far as what a new system costs, but I did put one of them, an option down there for you at the bottom (Call 19). What separates the ten flips from the eight no-moves is not the diagnosis, the report, or the energy analysis — those are identical across both groups — but whether he picks up the phone before the paperwork comes out. On every no-move he offers the third option in words and then reaches for the signature pad. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: the dispatch call happens while the customer is still standing at the unit, not after they have said let me think about it.

Strengths

1. He sends for the Comfort Advisor instead of offering one (Strong)

This is the whole story of the 45%, and it is worth naming precisely because it is a mechanic other technicians can copy. On Call 8 the option is agreed and he leaves the room to make it real: I’m going to grab my tools as well as I’m going to call dispatch just so they can send Dan out here… Dan’s on his way. Once he gets here, do you want me to knock or just bring him on in? Call 10, same day: looks like I do got someone on their way. I’m just gonna go ahead and step out real quick and make them a ticket through dispatch. Call 14: Andrew should be here in a few minutes now, so I’m going to wait out for him, and when he comes in, I’ll just bring him on in. Call 25, with his own phone in SOS mode and no signal, he calls a colleague to raise the ticket for him rather than let the handoff wait. Four separate specialists named by first name across ten flips, an arrival window given every time, and on every one of them he is still on the property when they arrive.

2. Declining to price a replacement, and turning the refusal into the offer (Strong)

The routing is correct on every call where the question comes up, and — this is the part that converts — the refusal never ends the exchange. Call 19: I just work on the repair side. I don’t quote or give options as far as what a new system costs, but I did put one of them, an option down there for you at the bottom. That way you can get all of your information, which would be speaking to one of our comfort advisors about what updating the system costs. Call 13: plus for a new unit, I just work on the repair side. Call 25: I could get someone out here today to give you what those look like. Because I just do the repairs, not with placement. Turnaround time is usually next day. The redirect to a specialist is the correct move under this process and it is this technician’s defining strength; he pairs it with a named person and a timeframe, which is why it lands as help rather than a deflection.

3. Removing the cost and the commitment from the signature (Strong)

He anticipates the objection that signing means buying, and disarms it before it is raised. Call 1: just be a signature at the bottom… $0 value, just stating that you’re okay with having a Comfort Advisor come out. Call 18: it just states that I can have my comfort advisors come out… it’s a zero dollar balance right there. And then I can get them here within 30 to 40 minutes, and they take about an hour of your time. Call 6: that way it’s a free conversation. You don’t have to buy anything, but you can at least see what that information looks like… and then if it doesn’t work, doesn’t work for you guys, you can always fall back on repair. Cost, duration, and the retained fallback, in one breath.

4. Saying plainly when a repair will not hold (Strong)

Call 10, on a compressor reading bad on the windings and overamping: we could put that start device in there, could turn on, but we’re going to be back out here a week, 2 weeks down the road because you’re going to need a new compressor. Call 19, on a system with a refrigerant leak: we can’t just put Freon in there when we know it’s leaking… it’s also like putting air into a tire that has a hole in it. It’s just gonna leak right back out… that would be like putting money into it, but it’d be lighting that money on fire. Both calls produced verified handoffs. This is the sentence that earns the third option, and he reaches for it without being asked.

5. Teaching the refrigerant transition as the reason the maths changed (Strong)

Call 22: 2010, they banned that. It can no longer be manufactured or imported. They moved down to R-410. They banned that the 1st of 2025. And now we’re on the 3rd one, which is A2L. With being on the third one, there’s not a whole lot of R-22 products out there. He then converts the history into a scheduling fact rather than an argument: if we can get a product that’s R-22, it’s usually 6 weeks out, and then, um, that’s if we can get it. A customer who understands that the part may not exist evaluates the specialist visit differently from one who has only been told a price.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Twenty-two scoreable calls, ten of them flipped, and the diagnosis is close to invariant across the whole set — the LRA reading against the data tag, the megohmmeter, the oil on the blower, the energy analysis percentage, the refrigerant history. That is what makes this week unusually legible: the assessment is not the variable.

  • Ready: Neutral. The job history is read on flips and no-moves alike.
  • Welcome: Neutral. The same opening runs on both, near-verbatim.
  • Assessment: Neutral, and Strong on both. Calls 20 and 22 are among the most thorough of the week and neither produced a lead.
  • Decision: Neutral in language, decisive in follow-through. The third option is named on both sides of the line, in almost identical words.
  • Deliver: Decisive. On the ten flips the next action is a phone call; on the eight no-moves it is a signature.
Decisive Gap — the Deliver

Decisive gap — the Deliver. The separation this week is not in what Cole says but in what he does in the sixty seconds after the customer agrees. On Call 8 the customer is mid-anecdote about food storage when Cole stands up: I’m going to call dispatch just so they can send Dan out here — and Dan is on his way before the anecdote finishes. On Call 21, a call lasting 841 words in total, a customer who has already decided they only want a price gets a signature, a name, and be within the hour. He’ll be here. On Call 25, with his work phone in SOS mode and no signal, he calls a colleague to raise the ticket rather than let the handoff slip to a callback. Against that, Call 20 has a better assessment than most of the flips — two compressors over their plate values, a fan motor shedding oil, a documented 12% utility penalty, the option named and financing mentioned — and ends with an email address. Call 2 has the customer’s explicit that’s fair and ends with two signatures. The offer is not the bottleneck; the offer is excellent and it is everywhere. What varies is whether it is converted into a dispatched person while the customer is still in front of the equipment. Ten times this week it was, and every one of those ten is a verified turnover lead.

Beyond the Guide

Staying to brief the specialist rather than leaving a summary

Call 10: I hang out till he gets here. I’ll make sure that I show him everything that’s going on, that way he’s not walking in blind and everybody’s on the same page. Call 13, asked directly whether he waits: I hang out, um, when — till he — when he gets here, I’ll show him around everything. Call 21: I hang out till he gets here anyways. Then I’ll walk around with him. The customer hears that the second visit will not restart the conversation from zero, which removes the main practical objection to accepting it. Coaching implication: worth capturing as a standard, not a personal habit — it is cheap, it is repeated verbatim, and it is attached to every one of his flips.

The energy analysis as a third number alongside now and later

Call 22: we’re overpaying on utilities on that system when it’s running by about 12%. With all of that, we got $2,300 coming up soon, $8,600 coming up down the line, maybe a little bit sooner with that compressor, and we’re overpaying on utilities by about 12%. Call 25: just based on the age and condition of that system, going to be running up that power bill by about 15%. Coaching implication: it reframes doing nothing as a cost rather than a saving, which is exactly the ground the specialist conversation needs. It appears on both flips and no-moves, so it is not by itself the converter — but it is the sentence that makes the converter reasonable.

Refusing a refrigerant top-up on a known leak, and saying why

Call 19: we can’t just put Freon in there when we know it’s leaking. It’s like, at that point, it’s against the EPA regulations. Coaching implication: the customer is told the constraint is external and not a sales position, which is a materially different conversation from one in which the technician appears to be declining the cheap fix by choice.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Make the dispatch call the reflex, not the exception. On all ten flips the option became a ticket within about a minute of agreement. On Calls 2, 6, 16, 20 and 22 the identical offer was followed by the signature pad. Same technician, same week, same words — the only variable is the phone call.
  • Treat “I’ll call you later” and “wait for Harry to call” as no-moves in progress. Calls 4 and 16 both end with a promised inbound call and neither produced anything. Where a callback is genuinely needed, book the specialist visit anyway and cancel it if the customer changes their mind.
  • Answer “I’m too busy” with the forty-minute arrival. Call 6’s customer works all the time; that is the argument for the visit happening now, while the attic is open and the technician is on site.
  • Add a review ask to the two signatures. Twenty-five recordings, no ask, and at least four customers who volunteered thanks unprompted.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priority 1 covers five of the eight no-moves and is a sequencing change rather than a new skill; Priorities 2 and 3 are the two remaining avoidable losses; Priority 4 is upside on calls that already went well.
