Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Across 210 recordings and 145 scoreable calls, the team handed off Option C on 55% of calls and won repairs on 7%, for a combined close rate of 59%. Eighty turnover leads were created and verified in ServiceTitan. All three numbers are down on the week ending 12 July (57%, 9%, 66%) on eighteen fewer scoreable calls. Last week’s finding repeats and sharpens: all thirteen technicians band Assessment Strong, for the third consecutive week, so diagnostic quality explains none of the spread between a 93% handoff rate and a 20% one. What explains it, on this week’s evidence, is narrower than “a time versus an intention”. It is whether the technician dispatches a named person while still standing at the equipment, or describes a service the customer could request later. The two are one phone call apart and they are the entire difference.

Top versus Bottom

Noah Weng handed off 14 of 15 scoreable calls. Andrew Trujillo handed off 2 of 10. Both band Ready, Welcome and Assessment at Strong; both give complete, measured, well-taught diagnoses; both are liked by their customers. The divergence is entirely inside the last two minutes.

  • Noah Weng ends the diagnosis by making the specialist visit the next thing that happens, not the next thing the customer might arrange. Fourteen times in a week, the option became a person with a name and an arrival window.
  • Andrew Trujillo ends the diagnosis by delivering the report. The option exists in the paperwork on calls where it never enters the conversation out loud. His Decision band is the only Weak on any step anywhere this week, and it is the single highest-value coaching target on the roster.
  • Cole Pantol is the useful middle case, because he is both. His ten flips and his eight no-moves carry the same diagnosis, the same energy analysis and the same Option C sentence. On the ten, he leaves the room and calls dispatch. On the eight, he reaches for the signature pad. Same technician, same week, same words.

The Positive-Outcome Predictor Test

Testing the hypothesis directly: does the offer being dispatched rather than described predict the handoff? Across the corpus, calls in which the technician placed a dispatch call, named the arriving specialist, or gave an arrival window while still on the property produced a verified turnover lead at a rate several times that of calls in which the option was presented in the report and left with the customer. The finding holds within technicians as well as between them, which is what makes it actionable: it is not a talent difference, it is a sequencing habit. The strongest single expression of it in the corpus is Cole Pantol’s, and it is reproducible verbatim — I hang out till he gets here. I’ll make sure that I show him everything that’s going on, that way he’s not walking in blind and everybody’s on the same page.

The corollary matters as much: the quality of the offer is not the constraint. The most thorough option presentations of the week — Cole Pantol’s Call 20, Nikko April’s teaching sequences, Benjamin Wyllie’s cost breakdowns — sit on calls that produced nothing. There is no evidence anywhere in this corpus that the team needs a better script.

Price Routing

Recorded here as a strength across the roster, not as a gap. Every technician who was asked what a replacement costs declined to quote it and routed the question to a Comfort Advisor. That is the correct move under this process and it is not coached against anywhere in these reports. The variation is only in what follows the refusal: the technicians at the top of the handoff table attach a named person and a timeframe to it in the same breath, and the technicians at the bottom let the refusal end the exchange.

What To Do This Week

  1. One habit, whole team: dispatch before paperwork. When the customer agrees to see a specialist, the next action is the phone call, not the signature pad. This is a sequencing change, not a new skill, and every technician on the roster already does it on their best calls.
  2. Andrew Trujillo, individually. The only Weak band of the week. Eight no-moves from ten, with Strong assessments behind all of them.
  3. Adopt the warm introduction as a standard. Staying on site to brief and physically introduce the arriving Comfort Advisor is currently one technician’s personal habit. It costs nothing, it is repeated near-verbatim, and it removes the customer’s main practical objection to a second visit.
  4. Fix the job-number gap on Juan Tlatenchi’s recordings before another month of unverifiable work accumulates.
