Strengths

1. The most complete opening block on the roster (Strong)

Call 5 does in six sentences what most technicians spread across the whole visit: it sets the scope, promises photographic evidence of the part of the house the customer never sees, guarantees nothing happens without approval, states the standard being worked to with the actual rating attached, and then hands the customer control of the agenda. Nobody goes up there, so I’ll be able to show you guys what’s going on up there is the line that does the most work — it converts the attic from a place the customer must take on trust into a place they are about to be shown. Coaching value: this block should be transcribed and given to the roster. It is better than anything else in the week’s corpus and it costs thirty seconds.

2. Diagnosing against the manufacturer’s chart, not just the plate (Strong)

Most of the roster reads a single plate value and compares the measurement to it. Andrew goes further and works from the performance table for the ambient conditions on the day — Call 3, this is the chart that your unit uses. So when it’s between 85 and 95 degrees outside, your unit should show about a specific pressure — and then adds a forward-looking threshold rather than a binary pass or fail: when it hits about that 27.5 line, there’s a pretty high likelihood it’ll fail. On Call 1 and Call 8 he measures supply air temperature against the expected split. Coaching value: this is the difference between telling a customer a part is out of range and telling them how long they have, which is the question every customer actually asks.

3. Refusing to fix one thing before understanding the rest (Strong)

Call 2, offered an easy fan-motor swap, he stops to work out why the capacitor keeps draining: the fact that the compressor side’s going out is kind of concerning, mainly because if I swap that out and then swap the compressor’s battery out, and then that battery drains, then I’m just gonna get called back out here… I don’t want to have you replace a fan motor and then have it go out in a few weeks. Call 13 the same instinct: I’m gonna go get you pricing for me to find the leak and then this fan motor so that I’m not just fixing the issue. Coaching value: it costs him the quick repair sale and buys the credibility that makes the upgrade conversation land. Seven of his eleven scoreable calls involved him declining to take the shortest route.

4. Booking the handoff with a time, a name and a failsafe (Strong)

This is why his handoff rate is the highest on the roster. Call 13: he escalates on the phone in front of the customer, then closes on a two-hour window the next morning and names the technician likely to attend. Call 2 he gives an arrival window and a script for chasing it: if you don’t see him within an hour or two, call up the office, say Andrew A said this. Call 6 he goes further and coordinates three trades onto one visit: I’ll just schedule to come out here with the guy on Friday. You’ll have me, drywall guy, equipment specialist. Coaching value: the customer is never left holding a decision. They are left holding an appointment, and on eight of eleven calls a turnover lead exists to prove it.

5. Small preventative work the customer did not ask for (Solid)

Call 1: I saw you have some plastic caps on your refrigerant lines. I usually put brass caps on, that way it doesn’t lose your refrigerant. So the plastic will vibrate itself out and you’ll lose little bits of refrigerant. On the same call he prices the membership honestly against what the customer was about to pay for the visit alone. Coaching value: it is a small thing done unprompted, and it is the kind of detail customers repeat when asked why they stayed with a company.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Spending the Option C moment on a membership upsell (Weak)

Call 3 is the clearest lost flip. The findings are severe and he presents them well — a compressor pulling excess starting amps, a fan motor that has lost its oil, a capacitor at 43 against a 45 rating, low refrigerant, and a coil he flags as possibly irreparable and on backorder (I don’t know if I can repair it, so if it’s irreparable, I have to replace that component, and then it’s gonna be on backorder). The customer asks the buying question directly — well, if you do the whole thing, how much is going to be? — and then signals hesitation: I know it’s important to do it, but let me think about it. What follows is not a booking but a different product: what I kind of like urge you to do is whenever you have that equipment specialist come out, sign up for the maintenance plan. The specialist is referred to as a future certainty rather than something being arranged, and no turnover lead exists for that job. What would have flipped it: the customer had already asked for the number. Call 13 shows what should have happened next — a phone call and a two-hour window.

Gap 2 — Large multi-system properties handled as a list of repairs (Moderate)

Call 10 is a property with four-plus systems, motor start devices testing below specification, fans losing oil, and equipment all dating from the same 2018 build. Andrew identifies the right conclusion privately — I definitely want to talk to him about swapping out that unit — but the customer is never offered a specialist, and the recording captures him struggling to keep the systems straight: I have no idea which of the two condensers outside of the home are associated with which. No turnover lead was created. Call 11 has a milder version: the customer asks which component is the most expensive, which is a buying question in disguise, and receives a technical answer about coils. What would have flipped it: a property with four systems of the same age is the strongest possible case for a comparison visit, and the organisational difficulty is itself the argument for bringing someone who measures for a living.

Gap 3 — Recording coverage is too thin to coach from (Moderate)

Five of his eight verified handoffs come from recordings of under 1,500 words, and one — Call 4, a confirmed flip — is 86 words long, capturing only the first thirty seconds at the condenser. Calls 7, 8 and 9 are similarly truncated. The outcomes are real and verified in ServiceTitan; what is missing is any record of how he got them. What would strengthen it: his best work this week is invisible. On the calls that are fully recorded he is one of the strongest technicians on the roster, and the thin recordings mean neither he nor his manager can see what he did right on the majority of his wins.

Gap 4 — No review ask, on the technician who quotes the rating (Weak)

Call 5 he tells the customer the company sits at 4.9 and is trying to reach 5.0. Fourteen recordings later, no customer has been asked to leave a review. Call 9’s customer is visibly impressed by his equipment and volunteers as much on the recording; Call 1’s customers are thanked repeatedly for their patience and warmth. What would strengthen it: he is already raising the subject of the rating at the start of the visit. Closing the loop at the end is a single sentence and he is better positioned to ask than anyone on the roster.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Eleven scoreable calls, eight of them flips. The pattern is unusually clean because his diagnosis quality does not vary — Call 3, a no-move, contains the single most rigorous piece of measurement work in his week.

  • Ready: Neutral, with one exception: Call 10, his largest no-move, is also the call where he loses track of which system is which.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong wherever the recording captures the opening.
  • Assessment: Neutral, and if anything inverted — the manufacturer-chart work on Call 3 is better than anything on his flips.
  • Decision: The entire difference, and it is about form rather than content. Where the handoff ends in a phone call and a window, it flips. On Call 3 the specialist is a future certainty rather than an appointment; on Calls 10 and 11 nothing is offered.
  • Deliver: Neutral. Signatures, emailed reports and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Andrew already does the hardest part better than most of the roster: he books. Eight of his eleven scoreable calls end with a specialist arranged, a time named and, on two of them, an escalation made in front of the customer. His three losses are the three calls where that mechanism did not fire — on Call 3 because the moment went to a maintenance-plan pitch, on Calls 10 and 11 because the option was never raised. The gap is not skill, confidence or diagnostic authority. It is that the booking is currently triggered by urgency — a hot house, a failed compressor — rather than by the customer asking what the whole thing costs.

Beyond the Guide

Regulatory dates stated precisely, and corrected mid-sentence

Call 1: They no longer put these in starting in 2025… Starting 2026, they’re no longer allowed to manufacture them — or no, other way around, sorry. 2025, they stopped manufacturing. 2026, we can’t install them. So you’re now a refrigerant behind, so you’re going to start dealing with delays on ordering parts. Coaching implication: the self-correction is worth noting positively — he fixes his own error in front of the customer rather than letting it stand. The supply-delay consequence is the part customers react to and he lands it every time.

Explaining what a home warranty will not do, without disparaging it

Call 3: with home warranty, they will wait for it to not work. You call them out, you still pay a premium, you have to pay the guy to replace the battery, and then they get it up and running. But then all of this other stuff is still working harder. Coaching implication: accurate, non-adversarial and directly relevant to why a comparison visit is worth taking. It is also, on that call, the point at which the specialist should have been booked.

Offering himself as the customer’s named technician

Calls 1 and 3 both end with an invitation to request him by name on future visits, tied to the maintenance plan. Coaching implication: it builds genuine continuity and customers respond to it. It is worth confirming with dispatch that request-by-name is something the schedule can actually honour, so the promise holds.

Coordinating other trades into one visit

Call 6: I’ll just schedule to come out here with the guy on Friday. You’ll have me, drywall guy, equipment specialist. Coaching implication: putting the Comfort Advisor into a visit the customer already needed for another reason removes the main objection to the appointment, which is the customer’s time. Worth generalising.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • When the customer asks what the whole thing costs, book before you offer anything else. Call 3 is the week’s only clear loss with a customer who had already asked. The maintenance plan can wait until after the specialist has a date.
  • Treat a multi-system property as a single comparison case. Call 10 had four systems of the same age and produced no lead. The number of systems is the argument.
  • Record the whole call. Five of eight wins are recorded in under 1,500 words and one in 86. His best work this week cannot be reviewed, by him or by anyone else.
  • Close the loop on the rating he already quotes. He opens by telling customers the company is at 4.9 and aiming for 5.0, and then never asks.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for all three no-moves; Priority 3 is what makes the wins coachable; Priority 4 is upside on calls already going well.
