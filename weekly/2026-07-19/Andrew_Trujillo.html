Strengths

1. Diagnosing from the refrigerant side, which nobody else on the roster does (Strong)

Superheat and subcooling appear on Calls 1, 5, 7, 9 and 12, always with the expected range stated before the measured value. Call 1: this superheat here tells us how much is flowing through the coil up here. This other value here, that’s a subcooling, that tells us how much is flowing through the outside unit. With this value being higher, this is telling me that the freon is restricted. Call 5 he reaches a conclusion the electrical readings could not have produced: a 32-degree superheat against a 0.2-degree subcool points him at the metering device rather than at a part. Coaching value: this finds faults that would otherwise be missed and it is a genuine technical asset to the team. It also means that when he says a system is in trouble, he can prove it in a way the customer cannot argue with — which makes the missing handoff below all the more costly.

2. Correctly clearing equipment that is healthy (Strong)

Call 1 he inspects a casita system and reports honestly that there is nothing to sell: everything looked clean. Cap was fine, contactor was good. Compressor wasn’t even over-amping. Coil is made out of aluminum. Call 6 the same: the good news is that I don’t have any immediate repairs I can recommend at this time. Coaching value: under the manager’s doctrine a call with no upgrade potential that ends without a handoff is not a failure, and reading that correctly protects the credibility that makes genuine recommendations land. This judgement is sound and should be affirmed.

3. Refusing to trade safety against price (Strong)

Call 6, on a heat exchanger: at what point do we put aside our safety when it comes to basic functionality and that price? I wouldn’t put any sort of price when it comes to safety… if we get carbon monoxide leaking inside your home, unless you have detectors, you won’t know until you start feeling it. Call 1 he ties it back to his training: when I tell you safety’s first and foremost on my mind… you don’t take any second chances. Coaching value: stated as a principle rather than as leverage, and on Call 6 he applies it to a system he has just cleared of immediate repairs, which is what makes it credible.

4. Absorbing a price challenge without hardening (Solid)

Call 3’s customer says plainly that the quote will be shopped: with a price like that, my husband will be looking at other companies for a different price. I can guarantee you that. Andrew does not defend the number: I’m not here to come and pressure you… what I’m seeing here gives me concern because… I just want to make sure that you guys are going to remain safe and comfortable in here. Call 4 he accepts the customer’s reasoning about waiting without arguing it. Coaching value: both calls stayed open and civil. What neither call did was convert the moment, which is the subject of the gaps below.

5. Catching what a previous visit left undone (Solid)

Call 9 he arrives on what is booked as a maintenance visit and finds the customer expecting a leak to be addressed. He reconstructs the earlier visit — notice that the indoor unit has been leaking water, causing growth inside the attic… notice the compressor is overheating, has internal damage — and identifies the omission: no leak search or repair was ever quoted. Coaching value: he protected the customer and the company from a repeat visit, and that call is one of only two flips in his week. It is worth noting that it flipped precisely because he reopened the whole question rather than working the ticket as written.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — A customer who raised replacement himself was pointed at his home warranty (Weak)

Call 4 is the clearest lost flip of the week. Two systems, oil loss on the office fan, corrosion on both voltage switches, compressors approaching threshold, and equipment on discontinued refrigerant. Andrew reports to a colleague on the recording that he brought up replacement himself. The customer then removes any doubt: I don’t see the economics of putting $15,000, $20,000 into repairs… I officially haven’t priced out a unit other than just what other people have told me. A customer stating that nobody has ever priced a system for him is a Comfort Advisor being requested. What follows instead is a suggestion to phone the home warranty company: why don’t we try giving them a call? That way we can just know definitively. No lead exists for that job. What would have flipped it: the customer had done every part of the work except being handed over.

Gap 2 — The comparison question answered with a repair range (Weak)

Call 12’s customer asks the Option C question in almost the exact words the process uses: if you do like maintenance and swapping out what there is right now as opposed to… the unit having to be replaced, what’s the cost difference? The answer identifies which parts are expensive and gives a repair figure of anywhere from $7,500 upwards of $9,000, and the call ends. Call 8 repeats the shape — cost-wise, what’s urgent? Like if it was you, what would you fix first and then go down the line? is answered with a repair triage and a note that the coils are becoming hard to source. Neither produced a lead. What would have flipped it: the cost difference between repairing and replacing is, by definition, a number only the specialist has. Saying so and booking them takes one sentence.

Gap 3 — Replacement-scale figures given by the technician (Moderate)

This is the same pattern flagged in his previous week and it has not changed. Call 2: compressors, you’re looking at at least about $4,300 just for the shell alone. Call 10: you’re looking at $915 for the parts out here. I know these motors, they go anywhere from $1,300 to $1,200. And the compressors… you’re looking at another about $5,000 at a minimum. Call 12 the $7,500 to $9,000 range. What would strengthen it: the numbers themselves are not the issue and nothing here suggests he is wrong about them. The issue is that a range delivered by the technician closes the conversation, where the same information delivered as that is the specialist’s number and I can have them here today opens it. Every other technician on this roster who holds that line has a handoff rate at least double his.

Gap 4 — Frustration voiced on the customer’s recording (Weak)

Call 6 he steps aside mid-visit to vent to a colleague, with profanity, about the customer’s past equipment decisions, and the recording ends with a further remark about his own performance. Call 9 captures an angry exchange about a lack of support from colleagues while he is inside the customer’s home. What would strengthen it: no customer is heard reacting to either, and neither changes a band in this report. Both are on a company recording during a paid visit, and Call 6’s frustration is audible enough that a manager should know about it — less for the conduct than because it is a technician having a difficult week and saying so.

Gap 5 — No review ask, and half the week unverifiable for a second consecutive week (Moderate)

Four complete visits carry no ServiceTitan job number, following five the previous week. Fourteen recordings contain no request for a review, despite Call 8 ending with the customer thanking him three times and Call 12 ending warmly. What would strengthen it: the job-number problem is a dispatch matter rather than a coaching one, but its effect is real — it is now the second week running in which this technician’s figures rest on a partial record, and that makes both his good weeks and his bad ones harder to read than they should be.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Ten scoreable calls and only two flips, so the interesting comparison this week is not between his wins and his losses but between his diagnosis and his close. The first is the best on the roster in its own way; the second did not happen at all.

  • Ready: Neutral and consistently Strong. File research appears on both outcomes.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong. The approval promise and the thank-you appear regardless of outcome.
  • Assessment: Neutral and consistently Strong — Calls 4 and 12, both no-moves, contain refrigerant-side work no other technician on the roster performs.
  • Decision: Not a differentiator, because it does not appear. On both flips the customer opened the replacement conversation themselves; on all eight no-moves no Comfort Advisor was offered.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The named route back to him and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Andrew has the strongest diagnostic toolkit on this roster and the weakest conversion of it. Across ten scoreable calls he did not once put a Comfort Advisor in front of a customer. His two flips came from customers who took themselves there — one by asking outright whether a new unit would solve everything, and one on a recording too degraded to read. Against that, three separate customers asked what replacement would cost or how it compared, and each received an accurate repair figure and nothing more. The gap is not knowledge, credibility, courtesy or objection handling, all of which are demonstrably present. It is that he is answering the Comfort Advisor’s question himself, and a question answered is a conversation closed.

Observation, not a coaching point: combined with four untitled files, the practical result is that a majority of this technician’s week cannot be reviewed properly. Worth raising with whoever maintains the recording and transcription pipeline, because it is materially affecting how this technician can be coached.

Membership explained by what it removes rather than what it costs

Call 5: if you have a tank water heater, they can do a flush for you as well. And then if you guys have any sort of emergencies, like it’s not cooling in the house, it’s not heating, if I have a leak — you can give us a call, we’ll be right on out, no charge. Coaching implication: he sells the membership well and consistently. The same framing — here is a thing that costs you nothing and removes a risk — is exactly the framing the Option C offer needs, and it is already in his mouth.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Answer the comparison question with a person, not a range. Calls 4, 8 and 12 all contain the customer asking what replacement costs or how it compares. That question belongs to the Comfort Advisor and the correct answer is a name and a time.
  • Stop quoting replacement-scale figures. This was flagged last week and has recurred on three calls. Every technician on this roster with a high handoff rate declines this question in the same breath as offering the specialist.
  • Book a specialist on the strength of the refrigerant-side findings. A 0.2-degree subcool or a 1.7 superheat is a more compelling reason for a comparison visit than any amp reading on the roster, and it is currently being used only to justify a repair.
  • Get job numbers onto the recordings. Nine untitled files across two weeks; his rates rest on half a record.
  • Add a review ask to the close. Fourteen recordings, none.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for the clearest three of the eight no-moves; Priority 3 is the untapped asset; Priorities 4 and 5 are record-keeping and upside.
