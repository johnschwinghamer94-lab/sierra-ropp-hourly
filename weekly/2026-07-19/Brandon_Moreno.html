Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Brandon hands off Option C on 60% of his scoreable calls (6 of 10) and wins repairs on 10% (1 of 10). The headline for this week is a change in behaviour rather than a change in rate: last week his four no-moves all shared one feature, that the free specialist visit was never offered once money was raised. This week he offers it on the no-moves too — Calls 1, 2 and 6 all contain a properly built third option priced at zero. The gap has moved one step later in the call. Where he names a time and takes a signature the call flips (Call 8, let me see if we have that 8 to 10 or 4 to 6… I just need a signature to speak with the equipment specialist. $0 visit because you’re already on the membership); where the offer is left on the page it does not. Call 6 is the clearest instance: he presents the option, then hands the initiative to the customer’s home warranty — if you get any updates, let me know. Call the company. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: every offer ends the way Call 8 ends, with two time windows and a pen.

Strengths

1. Recommending Option C selectively, and saying where it is not needed (Strong)

Call 8 is the best Decision work in the week’s corpus. On a three-system property he recommends the specialist for one system only and states plainly that the others do not warrant it: Some of the other systems I don’t think you need option C for it, but this one I’d recommend that, before even doing this. The reason he gives is the customer’s own exposure rather than the company’s: this is non-refundable. So if we go ahead and put these devices or parts on, and if this compressor fails, now we’re in the hole nearly $2,000 plus either replacing equipment or doing that compressor. Then he closes it — two time windows offered, signature taken, cost stated as zero. Coaching value: a technician who says a customer does not need something is believed when he says they do. This is the version of the handoff that should go to the roster.

2. Rebuilding the whole options conversation for a customer with limited English (Strong)

Call 2 he strips the presentation down without stripping out the substance: Okay, so this is the repairs. It costs a lot of money. Look, $8,900. Yeah, this is old system, not worth it. We have future items, more cost. This is repairs, option A. Option B, repairs, very expensive. Option C, this is $0. This is to speak with the equipment specialist. He’ll give you an estimate, no charge. He then re-explains it a second time in different words rather than assuming the first pass landed. Coaching value: the A/B/C structure survives translation into short sentences, which is a useful proof that the structure itself is doing the work rather than the wording. Worth noting that this call still did not produce a lead, which is discussed below.

3. Showing the customer what cannot be shown by hand (Strong)

Call 4 he uses a thermal camera to make an invisible fault visible, and explains the image before showing it: the area that is dark is cool. The area that is yellow, that’s hot. On the same call he explains why the noise the customer is used to hearing has stopped, using the absence as evidence: usually you hear like a noise, right? That’s from this part. So since this component’s not working right now, that’s why we’re not getting the noise. Calls 7 and 8 use the blood-work frame for the gauge set: we’re basically going to check the vitals on it. It’s kind of like when you get your blood work done, so [you’re] able to see the stuff that we can’t see. Coaching value: every one of these turns a claim into an observation, which is why his diagnoses are rarely argued with.

4. Honesty about the limits of a repair, before taking the money (Strong)

Call 4: we can attempt to repair. That’s over $500. And it’s an attempt. I don’t want to guarantee you put a good part in because after we put it in, we can’t refund it. Call 1 he refuses a top-up on a leaking system and explains the exposure as his own: through EPA standards, I can lose my license. I will not fill that system up if it’s leaking. Call 8 he generalises the same caution into the Option C argument. Coaching value: three separate opportunities to take money for work that might not hold, and three refusals stated up front. This is the foundation the rest of his credibility sits on.

5. Absorbing a customer’s anger at somebody else (Solid)

Call 7’s customer arrives furious about a previous visit — a large sum already spent, a filter dispute, and an unwelcome replacement recommendation delivered badly. Brandon does not join in and does not defend: We’ll get you situated. All right, but folks are good people, and moves straight to measurement. The call ends with a verified turnover lead. Coaching value: the anger was about being told to replace the system, and he still got to a replacement conversation, because he re-established the diagnosis before he raised the option.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The offer is made and then left on the page (Moderate)

This is the week’s defining gap and it is a more advanced problem than last week’s. On Calls 1, 2 and 6 the third option is built correctly, priced at zero and explained — and none of them produces a lead. What is missing in each is the close. Compare Call 8, where he offers two specific windows and takes a signature on the spot, with Call 1, where the offer ends at the guy comes out, he’s able to speak with you and then give you all your information available. Now, what questions do you have for me? The option is described; nothing is arranged. What would strengthen it: the difference between his flips and his no-moves this week is roughly fifteen seconds of diary work. Two windows and a signature, every time.

Gap 2 — Handing the initiative to the customer’s home warranty (Moderate)

Call 6 he correctly identifies that the warranty will not cover the failed component — they don’t even cover the actual, like, expensive part, because that damper, that’s what helps all that air come in — and builds the third option properly, including financing (I’ve seen them as low as like $150 a month). He then hedges it away: that’s always an option, but you just want to see whatever makes sense for you guys, repairs or whatnot, and closes with if you get any updates, let me know. Call the company. The customer leaves waiting on a third party that he has just explained will not help. No lead was created. What would strengthen it: the warranty’s refusal is the argument for the free comparison, not a reason to wait for it.

Gap 3 — A signature asked for and not obtained (Moderate)

Call 2 he does everything right up to the last step, including asking for the close: Option C, this is $0… I just need signature down here and I’ll give them a call and then he’ll come over. No lead exists for that job. On a call conducted in deliberately simplified language against $8,900 of repairs on an old system, the most likely reading is that the request for a signature was the one sentence that did not land. What would strengthen it: on calls where he is already simplifying for comprehension, the close deserves the same treatment he gives the options — said twice, in different words, with the pen visible.

Gap 4 — No review ask anywhere in thirteen recordings (Weak)

Call 9 ends with an extended, warm conversation and a booked return (we’ll see you Monday), and the customer thanks him by name: Thank you, Brandon. Great talk. Call 7’s customer arrives hostile and leaves calm. Neither is asked for a review, and neither is anyone else in the week. What would strengthen it: this is the second consecutive week with no ask across a combined thirty-seven recordings. The close already has a natural pause in it.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Ten scoreable calls. Unlike last week, the presence of the third option no longer separates the outcomes — it is built on nine of ten. What separates them now is whether it is closed.

  • Ready: Neutral and consistently Strong. History and prior repairs are read on both outcomes.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong wherever he opens the call.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 1, a no-move, contains the clearest teaching of the working margin in the week.
  • Decision: No longer separates on presence — the option exists on Calls 1, 2 and 6, all no-moves. It separates entirely on closure: Call 8 names two windows and takes a signature and flips; Calls 1 and 6 describe the option and stop.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The window, the knock and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Last week Brandon lost calls because the offer disappeared when money came up. That has been fixed — every no-move this week contains a properly built, correctly priced third option, including one delivered twice in simplified English. What has not yet followed is the close. On Call 8 he offers a choice between an 8-to-10 and a 4-to-6 window and takes a signature; that call flipped, and so did the five others where an arrangement was made on the visit. On Calls 1, 2 and 6 the customer is left with an accurate description of something they could have, and a pen was never put in front of them. The remaining gap is not the argument, the pricing or the willingness to offer. It is that describing an appointment is not the same as making one.

Beyond the Guide

Reading the membership benefits out rather than asserting the value

Call 9: let me pull this up. I’ll read it line by line… $16 per month, no hidden fees. 15-point cooling system tune-up and inspection, spring and summer, $149 value. Coaching implication: the customer hears the itemisation rather than a claim, which is the same technique that makes his diagnoses land. Worth standardising, and worth checking that the $0 specialist visit is read out as part of the same list — on Call 8 the membership is precisely why the visit costs nothing.

Naming a specific person the customer can book with

Call 9 he leaves the customer with a technician’s name and how to use it: you can put down his name is Rob… you can always schedule with him if you’re like, hey, I want to do the filter or whatnot. He’ll go ahead and book it for you. Coaching implication: continuity of technician is one of the few things a customer values that costs the company nothing, and it converts a service visit into a relationship the office can build on.

Correcting a competitor’s diagnosis without adopting the customer’s anger

Call 7 the customer is explicit and profane about a previous visit and a large sum already spent. Brandon neither agrees nor defends: We’ll get you situated. All right, but folks are good people, then measures and finds the compressor locked at zero amps. Coaching implication: the restraint is the skill. Agreeing would have made the eventual replacement recommendation sound like the previous company’s; refusing to engage let him make it himself.

Recorder artefacts in this week’s files

Three recordings (Calls 10, 11 and 13) carry scrambled speaker attribution, and Call 4 contains a long run of punctuation artefacts mid-transcript. Observation, not a coaching point: the audio evidently captured the visits, but the transcription is degraded enough that some of the options conversation on Call 10 cannot be attributed with confidence. Worth raising with whoever maintains the transcription pipeline.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Close every offer with two windows and a signature. Call 8 is the template and it is already his. Calls 1, 2 and 6 contain the same offer without the close, and account for three of the four no-moves.
  • Treat a home warranty’s refusal as the reason to book, not a reason to wait. Call 6 is the example: he had already established the warranty would not cover the part.
  • Say the close twice on calls where you are already simplifying. Call 2 got the options across in short sentences and the signature request did not land.
  • Add the review ask to the closing knock. Two consecutive weeks, thirty-seven recordings, no ask.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priority 1 accounts for three of the four no-moves; Priority 2 for the fourth; Priorities 3 and 4 are consistency and upside.
