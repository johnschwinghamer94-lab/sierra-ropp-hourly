Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Francisco hands off Option C on 60% of his scoreable calls (9 of 15) and wins repairs on 7% (1 of 15), holding roughly the level of his previous week on a slightly smaller base. The mechanism that produces it is unchanged and still the most portable on the roster: the third option is built, priced at zero, and closed with either a signature or a named window — Call 9, we’ll see you guys on Friday from 10 to 12; Call 4, there’s zero cost to them to come out, but I do need an autograph so that way they can get authorized to come out here. Two things need attention. Call 11, a frozen system with a failed blower motor and a customer collecting three quotes, is the one call where he stopped working the visit — we’ll hold off on my evaluation — and argued the company’s market position instead of building the options. And on Call 17 a replacement figure appears in the recording that is an order of magnitude above every comparable number in his own week; that needs checking rather than assuming. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: finish the evaluation on every call, including the ones where the customer has already said they are shopping — on Call 11 the options sheet was the only thing that would have competed.

Strengths

1. Closing the handoff with a window or a signature (Strong)

This remains the single most copyable habit in the corpus and it survived into a second week. Call 9 he names the slot and confirms what will happen in it: Friday, 10 to 12… so I’ll send you guys the quote over for the thermostat and the compressor, and then we’ll see you guys on Friday from 10 to 12 to go over. Call 12 the same, for a Wednesday. Call 4 he takes the signature and explains exactly why it is needed: there’s zero cost to them to come out, but I do need an autograph so that way they can get authorized to come out here and just give you that information. Coaching value: the customer is never left holding a decision, and the authorisation framing removes any sense that they are committing to a purchase.

2. Advising against the sale when that is the right answer (Strong)

Call 8 is the clearest example of customer-first judgement in the week. The customer needs to sell a condo and the system is failing. Rather than pushing either path, he separates them by circumstance: what I would do if I was in your shoes is I would try to figure out if you’re able to come up with the money first. If you’re able to come up with the money as far as replacing both, the replacement is going to be your better option. But if you can’t — if you’re going to sell the place and you just want it to get fixed, then I would go with the repair for now and just get it sold and be done with it. He also states the constraint plainly: you’re not going to be able to sell this condo unless you fix that HVAC. Call 12 he crosses an option off his own sheet: I’ll cross out this option of restoration cuz I wouldn’t recommend $8,500 unless you really were like, hey, I just want the bare bones minimum stuff. Coaching value: a technician who removes an option is believed about the ones he leaves on the page.

3. The patch-up argument, made from the customer’s own numbers (Strong)

Call 17: my biggest concern is where you start going down the patch-up and trying to catch up with the system. We do 14 today and then either half of this when the compressor, the coils goes out, and then you’re just having to put more and more money onto that unit. Call 2 states the same idea as a caution rather than a pitch: we can definitely do the repairs, there’s no problem with that. It’s just you’ve got to be cautious of what kind of repairs and how much you’re dumping into it, because eventually you’re gonna have to get rid of that unit and you don’t want to have too much money into it already. Coaching value: it frames the specialist visit as protecting money the customer has not yet spent, which is a far easier thing to accept than a recommendation to spend more.

4. Naming poor prior workmanship precisely, and only where it matters (Solid)

Call 5: you’ve had your capacitors changed, but whoever’s done them… they put electrical tape on there, and on the wiring, they just put a bunch of electrical tape, wire nuts, but you have wire that’s already getting exposed. Call 19, in Spanish, he finds a float switch that a previous visitor disconnected and explains what it was there to prevent. Coaching value: each observation is tied to a specific safety or reliability consequence rather than offered as a general complaint about other companies, which is the difference between a finding and a dig.

5. Training a colleague through the report on a live call (Solid)

Call 5 he walks a newer technician through building the A/B/C sheet line by line — which section, what goes in it, where the price sits — while continuing to work the call. Coaching value: the structure he is teaching is the correct one, and the roster’s consistency on A/B/C is partly a product of technicians like this passing it on. Worth recognising, and worth being deliberate about where that teaching happens, since it is being recorded inside customers’ homes.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Stopping the evaluation on the one call that needed it finished (Moderate)

Call 11 is the clearest lost flip. The blower motor is out, the system has frozen, and the equipment is a seven-to-nine-year-old builder-grade unit — he identifies all of it. The customer is openly comparing three companies. Rather than completing the evaluation and putting an options sheet in front of him, Francisco argues position: we’re the biggest company, one of the biggest companies out here, and we have a 4.9 rating on our Google review… we’re going to be the best company out here. But our prices are also going to reflect that. He then offers to stop: we’ll hold off on my evaluation, and then if you want to give us a call back out, then we can come back out and do that. No third option is built and no lead exists. What would have flipped it: a customer holding two other quotes is exactly the customer who benefits from a free comparison visit, and the only thing that competes with a rival’s number is a complete options sheet. Reputation was the weakest available argument and it was the only one made.

Gap 2 — A replacement figure that does not survive comparison with his own week (Moderate)

On Call 17, asked what the coils and compressor would cost, the recording has him answer compressor is $45,000 and coils is about $45,000 to $55,000, followed by I just gave you like a ballpark of like the lowest scenario. Every comparable figure in his own week sits an order of magnitude below that — $5,500 for a compressor on Call 4, $5,200 on the same call’s written report, $5,900 for a compressor and fan motor together on Call 18. What needs to happen: this is flagged rather than judged, because it is equally consistent with a transcription error and with a misstatement on the call. It should be checked against what was actually written on that customer’s report. If the figure was spoken, a customer was given a number ten times too high on a call that then flipped, and that needs correcting with them directly.

Gap 3 — The review ask did not carry over (Moderate)

In the previous week this technician made the only review request in the entire roster’s corpus, and it was good enough to be worth copying. Across nineteen recordings this week there is none — including Call 11, where he cites the company’s 4.9 rating to a customer as a selling point without ever asking that customer to contribute to it, and Call 15, which ends warmly with a paid repair and an emailed receipt. What would strengthen it: nothing about the wording needs changing. It needs attaching to the paperwork so that it fires without being remembered.

Gap 4 — Commission structure and internal talk on the customer’s line (Weak)

Call 5 includes an explanation of how repair commission is banded and why it is structured to discourage certain work, delivered to a colleague during a customer visit. Call 14 carries an extended stretch of crude conversation between technicians, including references to stimulants and to other technicians in terms that would not survive being heard. What would strengthen it: none of this reaches a customer in these recordings and none of it changes a band. The commission discussion in particular is the kind of internal detail that is damaging out of context, and Call 14 reads like the end of a long week — which is worth a manager noticing for its own sake, not only as a recording issue.

Gap 5 — The expectations block is still missing from the front of the call (Moderate)

This was flagged in the previous week and has not changed. Across nineteen recordings the promise that findings will be reported and that nothing happens without approval is largely absent, where most of the roster now runs it as a set block. What would strengthen it: his Decision step is among the best on the roster and his Welcome is among the thinnest. One sentence at the door pre-authorises everything the options sheet later contains.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Fifteen scoreable calls with nine flips. The diagnosis is consistent across the week and the A/B/C sheet is built on almost every call, so the separation is narrower than most technicians’ — it comes down to whether the sheet is finished and closed.

  • Ready: Neutral and consistently Strong. Install year and prior repairs are established on both outcomes.
  • Welcome: Neutral and thin on both. Not a differentiator this week.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 11, a no-move, contains a correct diagnosis that was simply never written up.
  • Decision: Where the difference sits, and it is subtle: the option is offered on the no-moves too (Calls 2 and 18 both contain proper offers that were declined). What distinguishes the flips is a named window or a signature taken on the visit.
  • Deliver: Neutral. Emailed reports, explained fee waivers and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Two of Francisco’s six no-moves are honest losses — on Calls 2 and 18 the third option was built, priced and declined by customers who had reasons of their own. The one that should not have happened is Call 11, where a customer actively comparing three companies was offered a reputation argument and then an offer to stop work. Everything that separates his flips from that call — a completed evaluation, a written sheet, a zero next to Option C, and a window or a signature — was available and was not used. The gap is not the mechanism, which is the best on the roster and held up across two weeks. It is that on the one call where a competing quote was already on the table, he stopped building the only thing that could have answered it.

Beyond the Guide

Working in the customer’s language

Call 19 is conducted in Spanish throughout, including the diagnosis of a disconnected float switch and the explanation of what it was installed to prevent: cuando se llena de agua, flota el switch y corta la unidad… pero alguien vino y nomás lo desconectó. Call 8 he moves between languages to keep everyone in the conversation. Coaching implication: a capability the roster should know it has, and one that removes the most common reason a customer defers a decision.

Explaining why an HVAC system is not a car

Call 8: it’s not like a car. A car, you can keep doing repairs and probably keep it going. HVAC repairs are very random, where like it works and then it doesn’t work. Coaching implication: most technicians on this roster use car analogies to explain components; this is the one that explains why the analogy breaks down, and it addresses the assumption that keeps customers repairing indefinitely.

Condensation volume as a way to explain coil rust

Call 17: those things create about, honestly, 10 to 12 gallons of water per day of how much condensation it builds… over time, that’s how they end up looking. Unfortunately, we can’t do anything to restore it. The only way to fix it is to replace those coils. Coaching implication: a concrete number makes the rust feel inevitable rather than like neglect, which removes the defensiveness that usually follows a coil finding.

Fee waivers explained rather than left unmentioned

Call 15: they actually waived your service fee. So it’s just the 224 and the 39 is waived. Coaching implication: this is the right way to handle a waiver — stated plainly as something the office did, with no implied condition attached. Worth setting as the standard, given that waivers appear across several technicians’ recordings this month with varying framing.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Finish the evaluation and build the sheet, especially when the customer is shopping. Call 11 is the whole cost of not doing so: a competing quote can only be answered with a complete set of options and a free comparison.
  • Check the Call 17 figure against the written report. If a customer was told $45,000 for a compressor, that needs correcting with them directly; if it is a transcription error, the recording pipeline needs looking at.
  • Reattach the review ask to the paperwork. He wrote the best one in the corpus last week and used it once; nineteen recordings later it has not reappeared.
  • Add the expectations block at the door. Second week running with the same gap, behind one of the strongest Decision steps on the roster.

Ranked by effect and by risk. Priority 1 is the week’s one avoidable loss; Priority 2 is a correction that may need making to a customer; Priorities 3 and 4 are consistency items carried over from last week.
