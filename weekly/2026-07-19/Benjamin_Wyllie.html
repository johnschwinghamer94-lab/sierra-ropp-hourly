Strengths

1. The fix from last week, executed (Strong)

Last week’s report identified one specific failure: handoffs finished as intentions rather than appointments, and on Call 18 of that week a customer who wanted a Sierra replacement produced no ServiceTitan record at all. Call 13 this week is the corrected version, and it contains every element that was missing: a stated arrival window (in the next 30-45 minutes), a signature (this is just stating that you’re okay with someone coming out. It’s no cost), a manager called on the spot, and an offer to hand over the diagnosis in person so the customer does not repeat themselves. It produced a verified turnover lead. Coaching value: this should be named explicitly in the coaching session. The behaviour change was made, it worked, and the only thing left is frequency.

2. Letting the customer reach the conclusion (Strong)

Call 1 he lays out a blower motor, a burnt control board, a fan motor leaking oil and a compressor already damaged by liquid return, explains the last one in terms the customer can picture — it’s like the coolant in your engine going back through as a solid when it’s supposed to be a gas, so it’s causing that system to grind — and then stops. His own account of what happened next: she went, oh, that’s just a new system then, isn’t it? I said, well, let me get you the repair cost. He does not agree, he does not push, he offers the repair number she asked about. Coaching value: the customer who says it first does not need convincing, and offering the repair price at that moment is what makes the eventual comparison feel like her decision rather than his sale.

3. Warning that a repair may not fix it, before taking the money (Strong)

Call 11: just because we put that on doesn’t confirm that that compressor is going to turn on based off the numbers that I had last time. So I want to bring that to your attention, that we could give that a try, but you have to pay for the part before we do that. Call 5 he says the same thing to a colleague and then to the customer: just because I put this cap in does not guarantee that this is going to work either. Call 14 he is honest about the limits of what he can see: that’s the hard part when it comes to rust, because I can’t exactly tell how much time it has. I don’t know how deep the rust has gotten. Coaching value: three separate opportunities to sell a part that might not solve the problem, and three warnings given first. This is the behaviour that makes the rest of his diagnosis believed.

4. Asking how long the customer is staying, before recommending anything (Solid)

Call 15: the system that you have is what we know as a builder-grade system… is this a short-term home or a long-term home? Call 6 he applies the same logic to who needs to be in the conversation: that’s why I try to ask, like, can we get him on the phone… when you flip it, do you want your son to be a part of this? Coaching value: both questions shape the recommendation to the customer’s circumstances rather than to the equipment’s age, and the second one is a practical fix for the most common reason a handoff stalls — the decision-maker not being present.

5. Explaining the refrigerant phase-outs without blaming anyone (Solid)

Call 10: we’ve gone through 2 phase-outs now, and we don’t have any control over it, but the government came in and basically said the last stuff is not environmentally friendly anymore and switched us over… It all cools the house the same. But it’s just when they stop producing it and we got to do repairs, it’s expensive. Call 16 adds the practical consequence: parts are obtainable mainly while equipment is still under warranty. Coaching value: stated as circumstance rather than as leverage, which is why customers do not push back on it.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The corrected sequence fires on some calls and not others (Moderate)

Four of the seven no-moves contain findings that plainly warranted a third option and none contains one. Call 9: a compressor at 122 against a 109 maximum, a thermal expansion valve stuck open and a blower motor leaking oil, on builder-grade equipment. Call 15: a compressor with internal damage and fan motor leaking oil. Both of these parts will cost around $7,500, or roughly the cost of the replacement — he says the two figures are comparable and then does not offer the comparison. Call 7 runs to over twenty thousand words, the longest recording in the month’s corpus, and ends with a compressor figure and no specialist. Call 11 the customer is told a repair may not work and is offered no alternative. What would strengthen it: Call 13 shows he can do this in under a minute. The trigger needs to be the findings rather than the mood of the call.

Gap 2 — Writing off a neighbourhood (Weak)

Call 6 records an extended exchange with a colleague, made from the property, in which he disparages the customer personally and then generalises: that’s why I can’t stand this neighborhood… this little community right here is worthless, I don’t think I’ve ever flipped a call here. Call 13’s close carries a similar account of a different customer. What needs to happen: the conduct is a manager matter, but the second half of that sentence is a coaching matter and the more serious of the two. A technician who has decided in advance that an area does not flip will not make the offer there, and Call 6 is a call where the offer was made half-heartedly and the diagnosis was sound. This is worth addressing directly and without heat — it is the kind of belief that becomes self-confirming.

Gap 3 — Pushing where a free comparison was the answer (Moderate)

Call 5’s customer cannot afford a $574 capacitor, and says so. Benjamin’s own account afterwards: I pushed for a flip there without a doubt, but I almost got him. I almost had him… he’s like, we know it’s old. I said, exactly, so might as well start over. No lead was created. What would strengthen it: this is the same note as last week in a different form. A customer who has just said a $574 part is out of reach is the customer for whom a no-cost comparison visit is most useful, and framing it as might as well start over asks them to agree to a larger version of the thing they have just declined. Call 14, on a customer living on Social Security, shows the right framing: I always recommend at least getting that pricing so that you know what you’re up against… but we can also do these immediate ones.

Gap 4 — Numbers becoming confused on the longest calls (Moderate)

Call 7 runs past twenty thousand words and the pricing exchange in the middle of it is not coherent on the recording — several figures are stated, revised and re-revised within a few sentences, and the customer is left restating them back. Whether that is the transcription or the conversation, the effect on a customer who has already said they cannot afford a replacement is the same. What would strengthen it: the written report exists precisely so that the numbers do not have to be held in the air. On the two longest calls of the week the report arrives late and the arithmetic happens out loud first.

Gap 5 — No review ask anywhere in sixteen recordings (Weak)

Second consecutive week, thirty-four recordings, no ask. Call 7 ends with mutual thanks and it was a pleasure meeting you guys; Call 4’s customer is delighted with the visit. What would strengthen it: the sign-off ritual already has two set pieces in it and a third would cost nothing.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Thirteen scoreable calls. The diagnosis quality is uniform across the week — the tolerance arithmetic and the plate comparison appear on wins and losses alike — so the split is entirely about whether a third option is built and closed.

  • Ready: Neutral and consistently Strong. Prior visits are read on both outcomes.
  • Welcome: Neutral and consistently Strong wherever he opens the call.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 9, a no-move, contains as clear a compressor and TXV diagnosis as anything in the week.
  • Decision: The entire difference, and now cleanly so: where the sequence from Call 13 runs, the call flips. On Calls 7, 9, 11 and 15 no third option is built.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The paperwork ritual and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. The change from last week is real and worth stating plainly: when Benjamin makes the offer now, he closes it — a window, a signature, a manager call, and an offer to brief the specialist himself. Call 13 is the proof and it produced a verified lead. What has not changed is how often the offer gets made. Four of his seven no-moves carry findings he himself described as comparable in cost to replacement, and on none of them did a Comfort Advisor get mentioned. On one of those calls he had already concluded, on the recording, that the neighbourhood does not flip. The gap is no longer technique. It is that the offer is still discretionary, and on at least one call the discretion was exercised before the diagnosis was finished.

Beyond the Guide

Explaining system staging to a customer who asked

Call 7: it starts at 60% power, so it helps the compressor not take a lot of damage, and then if it needs more power it’ll go up… And then there’s a multi-stage. Multi-stage starts at 35 and slowly works its way up if it needs to, and it’s super smart. It communicates with the house. Coaching implication: accurate and genuinely interesting to the customer, and it is Comfort Advisor material being delivered for free. On that call it was delivered instead of an appointment rather than alongside one.

The air-in-the-tire analogy for a leaking system

Call 6: we can refill it, but it’s like air in the tire. If there’s a hole in the tire, you can fill it back up, but it’s either going to leak right back out somewhere. Coaching implication: it explains the EPA position and the customer’s own interest in one sentence, without invoking the law at all. Worth standardising across the roster.

Naming what he is not checking, and why

Call 8: I’m not checking the heating components because I don’t want to turn your heating on and get the place super hot. Coaching implication: declaring the limits of the inspection protects both parties and is rare in this corpus. It also sets up an honest reason to return.

Recording quality and length

Call 7 runs to over 21,000 transcribed words and Call 10 to nearly 20,000 — the two longest in the month’s corpus — and both contain long stretches of technician-to-technician troubleshooting recorded inside the customer’s home. Observation: raised as an operational note. Very long calls correlate in this week with worse outcomes and with the numbers becoming confused, and that is worth looking at as a scheduling question rather than only a coaching one.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Make Call 13’s sequence the default. Window, signature, manager call, personal briefing. It is already his and it already works; it needs to fire on findings rather than on instinct.
  • Address the neighbourhood remark directly. Not as a conduct issue but as a working assumption — a decision that an area does not flip removes the offer before the diagnosis is finished.
  • Answer “I can’t afford it” with the free visit, not with a larger proposition. Call 5 is the example, and Call 14 is the correct version of the same conversation.
  • Let the written report carry the numbers on long calls. Call 7’s pricing exchange did not survive being held in the air.
  • Add the review ask to the two signatures. Two weeks, thirty-four recordings, none.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priority 1 accounts for four of the seven no-moves; Priority 2 is upstream of Priority 1 on at least one of them; the rest are consistency and upside.
