Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Robert hands off Option C on 73% of his scoreable calls (8 of 11) and wins repairs on 0% (0 of 11), which by the manager’s doctrine is the strongest possible shape for a week. He runs the most complete handoff sequence on the roster and runs it almost every time: the immediate and future totals are added together out loud, the option is shown on screen priced at zero, a signature is taken, a manager is called, and he waits outside to introduce the specialist in person. Call 4, I can have somebody come out within 30, 45 minutes… here you can see the equipment specialist. It’s no cost to you… then what I can do is wait for him outside to make sure he goes to the right house, and then once he’s here, then I can come introduce him to you. He is also the clearest technician in the corpus about where his job ends: I’m just a tech, so my expertise is like the components, knowing how they operate (Call 7). His single biggest gap is that on Call 10 — two systems, both motor start devices out of range, both fan motors oily, a rusted cooling coil and $8,600 of future work — he names the comparison and the role boundary but never puts the signature page in front of the customer. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: the sequence he executes eight times should not stop at the sentence before the signature.

Strengths

1. Adding the totals out loud and letting the arithmetic argue (Strong)

This is the engine of his week. He does not tell customers to replace anything; he stacks the two numbers and pauses. Call 13: in the future, it will be the motor with $4,116, and then the other components will be $8,600. So $12K… to me, with the age of the equipment and the condition of it, it doesn’t make sense to invest so much money into an older equipment because you replace the blower motor and then 3 months later, 6 months later, the compressor goes out and then the switch goes out. Call 4: putting $2,735 and $5,600 — to me, it doesn’t make sense to put so much money into older equipment. Call 1, on two systems: you’re talking about $15,000 in repairs over time in the future, not knowing when it can go out. Coaching value: the customer reaches the conclusion themselves, which is why the offer that follows reads as help rather than as a pitch. This framing should be standard across the roster.

2. The complete handoff, executed the same way every time (Strong)

Nobody else on the roster runs a sequence this complete. Call 4 contains all of it: the option displayed on the tablet showing $0, the signature taken with a finger in the box, the manager called on the spot, the arrival window stated, and then — the part almost nobody does — what I can do is wait for him outside to make sure he goes to the right house, and then once he’s here, then I can come introduce him to you. Call 7 repeats it for a customer whose husband is on the phone; Call 3 adds the timing (somebody can come out in the next 30 minutes to an hour to get the information to you so you know where you stand); Call 9 pushes it through despite a connectivity failure. Coaching value: the customer never has to re-explain the problem and the specialist never arrives cold. Eight verified leads in a week is the result.

3. Stating where his expertise ends, plainly and repeatedly (Strong)

Asked for replacement pricing he declines every time and gives the same reason. Call 13, asked directly what a new Lennox would cost and which tonnage: that’s the part that’s out of my expertise. I’m just a tech, so I know the components. I can explain to you so you can understand repair costs… The equipment specialist will go over costs with new equipment, 3-tons and all that information. Call 10: my expertise is just normal components and just going over them. I’m really not familiar with options… that’s more for the equipment specialist. Call 7: I’m just a tech, so my expertise is like the components, knowing how they operate. Coaching value: this is the SILO price rule executed better than anywhere else in the corpus, and it is delivered as competence rather than as a limitation — he tells the customer what he does know before saying what he does not.

4. Deliberately keeping the immediate list short (Strong)

Twice this week he moves a genuinely failing component from immediate to future on purpose, and says why. Call 13: because the other motor was leaking oil, typically I put both motors in immediate. I didn’t want to overwhelm you with price, so I did put it for futures. Call 10: I put the motor start because it’s on the fan side… I didn’t want to overwhelm with price. Coaching value: a smaller immediate number keeps the customer in the conversation, and the component is still disclosed rather than hidden. It is also, quietly, what makes the stacked-total argument work — the future column carries the weight and the immediate column stays payable.

5. Taking responsibility for damage outside the scope of the call (Solid)

Call 3 he finds water damage in an attic and does not simply note it: because of everything that’s happening within the attic with the water leaking, we do have a restoration company that we go with. They work with insurance… for me, it matters to me that you guys are taken care of. What’s going on right now, I don’t feel like is completely adequate. On the same call he disables the system so it cannot freeze and leak further overnight. Call 1 explains, without disparagement, what a home warranty will actually do: they don’t typically do repairs unless it’s failed… however long it takes the home warranty to get somebody out to your house, you’re out without AC at that point. Coaching value: both are unpaid work, and both are the reason the customer accepts the recommendation that follows.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — The sequence stops one step short on a heavy two-system call (Moderate)

Call 10 has everything his flips have except the close. Two systems, motor start devices out of range on both fan sides, oil caked around both fan motors, a rusted cooling coil, and future costs he puts at roughly $8,600 and $1,725. He names the comparison — compare and contrast repair versus replacement — and states the role boundary correctly. What never happens is the signature page, the manager call or the arrival window that appear on all eight of his flips. No turnover lead exists for that job. What would have flipped it: nothing needed to be said differently. The last ninety seconds of Call 4 needed to happen.

Gap 2 — Ending a declined offer on “I’ll follow up” (Moderate)

Call 1 is a well-argued no-move: he lays out roughly $3,000 in immediate work across two systems, roughly $13,000 in future work, and frames the option correctly — option C is no charge, there’s no obligation, it’s just kind of see where you stand currently… it’s not to say that you’re doing either or, it’s just to know where you stand in the middle so you can compare and understand. The customer does not take it and the call ends at okay, well, I’ll follow up then. No lead exists. What would strengthen it: a customer who declines the free visit today is the customer to book for a date, not to call back at some point. His own Call 2 line — the membership includes an emergency visit, so the calculus changes — is the kind of reason to give a date rather than an intention.

Gap 3 — The front of the call is the thinnest part of it (Moderate)

Across fourteen recordings the parking question, the permission-to-report question and the no-work-without-approval promise are largely absent, where most of the roster now runs them as a set block. What he does instead — restating the customer’s stated reason for calling before reporting — is good, but it happens at the report rather than at the door. What would strengthen it: his Decision step is the best on the roster and his Welcome is among the weakest. The expectations block pre-authorises everything the options sheet later contains, and it would cost him thirty seconds.

Gap 4 — Internal sales conversation on the customer’s line (Weak)

Call 12 captures an extended discussion with a colleague about approach, conducted during the visit: it’s our job to sell it. I mean, that’s the thing, from a salesman. If you can get us in the door and they know we’re coming to talk to them about a new system, that’s all I care. The content is defensible — it is an argument for being open with customers about why the specialist attends — but it is an internal conversation recorded on a customer visit, and that call produced no lead. What would strengthen it: nothing about the position needs changing. The recorder does.

Gap 5 — No review ask anywhere in fourteen recordings (Weak)

Call 3’s customers receive a restoration referral and a system disabled to protect their ceiling. Call 13 ends on an extended, warm exchange about family. Call 5 ends in a signed replacement order. None of them is asked for a review, and neither is anyone else this week or last. What would strengthen it: he already takes two or three signatures at the close of every call. The ask belongs on the same screen.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Eleven scoreable calls with eight flips. The diagnosis is uniform across the week, and so is the argument — every call, win or loss, gets the stacked-total framing. The separation sits entirely in the last ninety seconds.

  • Ready: Neutral. Service history is used where it exists on both outcomes.
  • Welcome: Neutral and thin on both. Not a differentiator this week.
  • Assessment: Neutral. Call 10, a no-move, contains readings on two full systems and is as complete as any flip.
  • Decision: The entire difference, and it is not about whether the option is offered — it is offered on Call 1 too. It is about whether the signature, the manager call and the arrival window follow. On all eight flips they do; on Calls 1, 10 and 12 they do not.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The property care, the emailed invoice and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Robert is the closest thing on this roster to a solved handoff. He builds the same case every time, states the role boundary better than anyone, and on eight of eleven calls carries it through to a signature, a phone call and an introduction on the driveway. His three losses are the three calls where that final sequence did not run: Call 10 stopped at the comparison, Call 1 stopped at a promise to follow up, Call 12 stopped at a conversation with a colleague. The gap is not the argument, the evidence, the pricing discipline or the customer’s willingness — on Call 1 the customer had heard a $15,000 future and still walked, because nothing was put in front of them to sign.

Beyond the Guide

The Comfort Advisor’s visit captured on the same recording

Call 5’s recording continues past the handoff into the specialist’s presentation — return and supply plenums being rebuilt, variable-speed options compared, and a finance approval completed with a monthly payment agreed. Observation, not technician behaviour: it is the clearest end-to-end record in the corpus of what a warm handoff produces, and it is worth watching for that reason. It is also a reminder when reading these transcripts that one file can contain two roles.

Using the membership’s emergency visit as a reason to decide now

Call 2: if it was today, it would be like an emergency, so there’s no cost for that. So you get one emergency visit a year too. So to me, it just makes sense to do that. Coaching implication: it converts a benefit the customer already pays for into a reason to act on the day, without inventing urgency. Worth standardising.

Refusing to charge refrigerant into a leaking system

Call 6: with refrigerant, there are laws with it. As a company, we don’t add coolant into a unit if there are signs of leaks. If there’s something where the temperature is low and there’s no sign of leak, of course we can top it off… but if there’s something where a leak is evident, he will not. Coaching implication: he draws the line precisely rather than absolutely, which is both more accurate and more credible than a blanket refusal.

Explaining that damage already done cannot be undone

Call 2: if there is restriction, whether it’s the outside condenser or upstairs in the attic, the damage — if it’s been a long time, the damage has already kind of been [done]. There’s nothing you can take back. Coaching implication: customers frequently assume that fixing the cause reverses the wear. Saying otherwise up front prevents the objection that a repair should have restored the system to new, and it is the honest position.

(ranked by impact)

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for two of the three no-moves; Priorities 3 and 4 raise the ceiling on calls already going well.
