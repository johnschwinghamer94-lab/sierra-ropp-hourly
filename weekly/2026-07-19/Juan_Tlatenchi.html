Strengths

1. Measuring airflow, not just electricity (Strong)

Call 12 is the best diagnostic work in the week’s corpus across all thirteen technicians. Faced with a zone system that intermittently stops cooling, he does not reach for a part. He measures static pressure, then changes the number of open zones and measures again, and shows the customer the readings climbing — 0.77 with two zones, 0.91 with one. He then explains the design fault that causes it: what these zone systems come with, it’s called a bypass damper, that basically recirculates the air just to give it more alleviation in the event that these zones are closed off. His conclusion is that the installation is wrong rather than that a component has failed. Coaching value: this is a fault most technicians would have replaced two parts chasing. It is also, incidentally, the strongest possible groundwork for a system conversation, because the problem is the design rather than the age.

2. Diagnosing liquid floodback and pricing it honestly (Strong)

Call 4 he identifies that liquid refrigerant is returning to the compressor, explains the mechanism — by the time it exits, it should exit as a vapor, and it should enter the pump as a vapor. If there’s not enough air flowing through that coil, then that’s where you can have excess amount of liquid still exiting and entering the compressor — names both possible causes, and then prices the uncertainty rather than hiding it: worst case scenario is you have to do both… best case scenario, you only have to do one of these. Say we could just adjust the airflow, so that’ll drop your price drastically. Coaching value: quoting a best case and a worst case, and saying which is which, is the honest way to handle a fault that cannot be fully isolated on the visit. It is rare in this corpus and it should be standard.

3. Declining to price equipment and routing it, every time (Strong)

Call 13: I don’t have pricing for equipment. I have to get someone out here. Call 3 he explains what the specialist actually does and how it differs from his own visit: one of our install team. They’re the equipment specialist. They don’t go in as depth as I do as far as looking at components. Call 6, asked what a new evaporator costs, he says he can get pricing and then flags the real constraint honestly — the equipment is no longer manufactured. Coaching value: this is exactly the SILO position, held consistently across a large corpus, and it is delivered as a division of labour rather than as an inability to help.

4. Trending readings against the previous visit (Solid)

Call 2: this one was drawing 135 on startup, and this time it was 126. So it was worse last time. It could have been a hotter day, or just different conditions in that system. But that’s still definitely out of range. Coaching value: the caveat is what makes it credible — he offers the innocent explanation before drawing the conclusion. Very few technicians volunteer the reason their own evidence might be weaker than it looks.

5. Working in the customer’s language (Solid)

Call 17 is conducted in Spanish throughout, including the refrigerant charge calculation and the framing of options: me voy a fijar allá arriba a ver si se puede ver dónde está la fuga y de ahí pues le puedo dar como diferentes opciones a ver qué es lo que se puede hacer. Call 8 switches languages mid-visit to keep a tenant informed while the owner is on the phone. Coaching value: a capability the roster should know it has, and one that removes the most common reason a customer defers a decision.

Beyond the Guide

Mismatched coils and what they cost in efficiency

Call 13: we always want to see compatible coils because that’s how you actually get the max performance out of this outdoor unit. So it being a 14 — it’s not even operating as a 14-seer just because of the mismatched coil. Coaching implication: this is an argument for a full system that has nothing to do with age or failure, and almost nobody else on the roster makes it. Worth standardising.

Explaining the compressor’s own self-protection

Call 12: these motors come with built in safeties to prevent it from running excessively hot as a way to kind of preserve them for a little longer. So once you shut it off, it cools down. Call 10 the same for the compressor’s thermal overload. Coaching implication: it explains the intermittent fault the customer has actually experienced — it works, then it doesn’t, then it works again — which is the symptom customers most often assume means nothing is wrong.
