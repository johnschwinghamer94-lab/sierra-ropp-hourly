Strengths

1. The opening block, now on every call (Strong)

Last week’s report banded Welcome at Solid for one specific reason: the expectations block landed when he opened a call himself and was skipped entirely on the visits he joined mid-conversation. That has changed. Call 7: this will get an expectation of me today… I’ll go ahead and do a thorough evaluation. Do we have any time constraints we’re working around?… once I’m done with my evaluation, I’ll go to my van for about 15, 20 minutes, write [the report]. Call 9 adds the standard he holds himself to: you don’t have to worry about me doing anything without your approval… overall I’m here for 5-star service. So if you see me doing something you don’t want me to do, I’m not doing it. Coaching value: this is the clearest week-over-week behaviour change anywhere in the month’s corpus, and the handoff rate moved from 75% to 93% alongside it. It should be named as a success.

2. Turning degradation into capacity the customer can picture (Strong)

Call 3 is the best example on the roster of making an abstract fault concrete: right here where it says 36 on the model number, you have a 3-ton unit. Now each unit, 1 ton is 12,000. So right now we should be putting out 36,000 BTUs, and right now we’re only managing part of that — summarised later as the unit has lost 1 ton of cooling capacity due to wear and tear. He also uses a plain operational grade on Calls 1 and 8: out of a 100A, the system is scoring a 50F on operation. Coaching value: a customer who understands they have paid for three tons and are receiving two does not need to be told the system is old. The BTU framing and the letter grade are both worth standardising.

3. The routing sentence, said the same way every time (Strong)

Call 2: I do repairs. I’m not one of the equipment specialists, but once I’m done diagnosing everything, if you guys would like, I can have one out here. Call 10, asked directly what a new unit costs: So I’m just repairs, right… I don’t have our prices on replacement. Call 9 he also declines to speculate about a competitor’s figure rather than disparage it: if you call any AC company in the valley, you’re not going to find a compressor that’s [that price]. So I’m just, I’m just prepared. So I don’t actually have prices on those. Coaching value: this is the SILO price rule executed consistently across twenty-one recordings, and it is the reason the specialist arrives with the customer’s expectations intact.

4. Explaining the refrigerant rules as a constraint on himself (Strong)

Call 9: every time we use Freon, we have to log it. We end up weighing our cylinder, writing down the weight, and then putting it in and checking and reweighing it. And we get checked every week… if I end up getting caught putting Freon in and there’s a leak, then I can lose my license. We can lose our license as a company. Coaching value: most technicians on this roster explain the leak rule as something the EPA imposes on the customer. Explaining it as something audited weekly, with his own licence at stake, is a materially more convincing version and it removes the suspicion that the refusal is a sales tactic.

5. Leaving the property safe, and saying so (Strong)

Call 2: I did leave that disconnect unplugged on the side of the house, so it’s not a fire hazard… that way the breaker doesn’t keep tripping. Call 4 he shuts a system down so a frozen coil cannot keep icing. Call 12 he pulls a disconnect and then makes sure the customer is not stranded by it: if for some odd reason you want to come back out here and you’re like, you know what, I want to give my compressor power again, all you got to do is open this up [and] flip it. Call 20 he gives away a fix rather than sell one: you probably just have a clogged drain line, so just take a shop vac to it and you’ll be good. Coaching value: four separate instances of protecting the customer at his own expense, on the week with the highest handoff rate on the roster. The two are not in tension.

6. Arming customers for their home warranty (Solid)

Call 3: I got you guys all this information so that you can take it to your home warranty and be like, hey, what are you guys gonna do for me? Call 12 he is candid about the limits from his own experience: home warranty is good for like smaller things. I’ve used them for my microwave, my refrigerator… I had a plumbing issue… they didn’t fix the stucco and they didn’t replace the drywall. Coaching value: the personal example makes it advice rather than a pitch, and it positions the comparison visit as something the customer needs in order to negotiate.

It is not a coaching point about technique and nothing in this report’s bands rests on it. It should go to whoever handles conduct, and this report should not be the mechanism by which it is raised with the technician.

Gap 2 — A safety concern reported in passing rather than to a manager (Moderate)

Call 20’s recording ends with him describing the previous evening: I’ve never been fearful in a homeowner’s house before… last night I was. He then describes a customer who intercepted him in the garage after his wife had engaged positively with a replacement conversation. What needs to happen: the account is credible and specific and it was told to a colleague on a recording rather than reported. Whatever else follows, a technician saying he was frightened in a customer’s home should reach a manager through a route that does not depend on somebody reading a transcript weeks later. Worth asking him about directly and worth checking whether a reporting route exists that he would actually use.

Decisive Gap — the Welcome

Decisive gap — the Welcome. For once on this roster the decisive step is not Decision. Noah’s handoff language did not change between his two weeks on file — the routing sentence is word-for-word the same. What changed is that the customer now hears, before any diagnosis exists, that findings will be reported, that nothing happens without approval, and that the standard is five stars. Last week that block was missing from every call he joined mid-visit; this week it is on all of them, and the handoff rate moved from 75% to 93% on nearly double the base. The lesson from this week is that the front of the call is what makes the end of it possible.

Beyond the Guide

Showing the customer a third-party running-cost calculator

Call 7: this is called HVACoperationcost.com… it allows us to plug in what city we live in, how much square feet of your home, how big is your unit. Nevada Energy charges roughly around 11.5 cents for kilowatts per hour. Coaching implication: an independent source removes the suspicion that the efficiency argument is invented, and the customer can re-run it after he leaves. Worth a decision at manager level on whether this is standardised, since it puts a third-party figure alongside Sierra’s own energy-analysis percentage.

Holding the legal line with a tenant

Call 4, with the property owner unreachable and a tenant asking what was found: I’m really not supposed to go over that with you, just legally, because I have to go through everything with her… It’s up to her if she wants to tell you or not. He then documents everything to the owner by email and states the decision she needs to make. Coaching implication: correct, and delivered without making the tenant feel dismissed.

The blood analogy for refrigerant

Call 5: if your compressor is the heart of your system, the Freon that runs through it and gets pumped by it is your blood. When you bleed out, it’s not operating anymore. Coaching implication: the whole roster uses the heart analogy for the compressor; this is the only extension of it that explains why a leak is not a small problem. Worth adding to the standard set.

Bilingual capability

Call 16: I speak a little bit of Spanish and I can let him know what’s going on with the unit, how much it would be to get it back up and running. Coaching implication: he offers it as an option rather than assuming, which is the right way round, and it is the third technician on this roster with the capability.
