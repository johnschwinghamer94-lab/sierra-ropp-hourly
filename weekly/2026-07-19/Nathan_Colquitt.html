Strengths

1. Trending the reading, not just taking it (Strong)

This is the best version of measurement work on the roster and it converts a snapshot into a direction of travel. Call 5: this is from last time. That number we’re seeing, 5.2. So what that shows me is we’re also seeing a decrease in that amount of refrigerant. And with it being a closed system, we should never see any type of drop in that number. He then rules out the innocent explanation himself: it will vary slightly due to temperature outside, before concluding. Call 3 he does the same on compressor amperage and names why it matters that the interval was short. Call 6: it has taken more damage since the time that our last technician was here. Coaching value: a customer who watches a number move over a year does not need to be persuaded about what happens next. This is the strongest possible foundation for an upgrade conversation, and on Calls 3 and 6 it was built and then not used.

2. Showing the work he was paid for (Strong)

Call 4: first thing I’d like to show is that we clean out these coils for you since you’re paying for that service. I like to make sure that it’s known to you. Call 2: I rinsed everything out for you… We got all the leaves out for you this time as well… You see how we got everything cleaned out in there? Call 8 shows the before and after. He also insists on doing it in person rather than over the phone: do you mind if I show you a couple things in person, if that’s cool as well? I just know sometimes on the phone I want to make sure that you know it’s here (Call 4). Coaching value: maintenance work is invisible by nature and customers routinely suspect it was skipped. Showing it costs a minute and it is the reason his membership customers trust the findings that follow.

3. Refusing the mixed-refrigerant shortcut and explaining why (Strong)

Call 2 the customer floats replacing only the outdoor unit. Nathan neither takes the easy sale nor refuses flatly: Some people do try to just replace the outside, which is something if you wanted to do, you could speak to someone about. But the reason we don’t recommend it is because you’d be using two different refrigerants. And they wouldn’t mix well, and it would damage this outdoor unit essentially. On the same call he declines to price a system at all: the reason that we don’t do that is because each home is essentially different, and routes it — they would know the exact pricing on that for you. Coaching value: this is exactly the SILO position, and the technical reason he gives is what makes the routing credible rather than evasive.

4. Using his own history without turning it into a pitch (Solid)

Call 2: prior to my replacement, I was trying to fix this, fix that, fix that, and it was more than what a system ended up costing me by trying to do part by part by part. Call 12 he explains why he will not fit a hard-start device on old equipment even though it would be an easy sale: you put a component in front of the compressor to take that energy, and then eventually that component fails, and it still fails the compressor. So then you’re paying money to get this part. It’s more like a Band-Aid. Call 8 he responds to a customer’s dissatisfaction with the company without defensiveness. Coaching value: in each case he is arguing against the immediate transaction, which is why the eventual recommendation carries weight.

5. Being clear about what he cannot price (Solid)

Call 6: I don’t have a price on that heat exchanger. That was the only thing I wasn’t able to get an exact price for you, because we need to contact the manufacturer. Call 8, on a new thermostat location: I have a price on just the individual thermostat, but I don’t have a price on installing a completely new room of a thermostat… we’d have to run wires through the wall and up into the unit. Call 10 he checks the part number against the actual unit rather than an online listing: online those prices may vary because what I’m seeing for this system isn’t the motor that I was seeing online… I wouldn’t want to put in the wrong motor for you. Coaching value: gaps in the estimate are stated rather than guessed at, which is rarer than it should be.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — A customer asked to compare repair against replacement four times and was never handed over (Weak)

Call 6 is the clearest lost flip in the month’s corpus. The findings support it fully: a compressor whose damage has visibly progressed since the last visit, a fan motor leaking oil, a heat exchanger showing hot spots, and a coil already showing signs of leaking. The customer asks, in escalating clarity: So how much is the fan motor? How much is the compressor? They’re expensive. Then so what does one of those cost? Then the reasoning laid out in full — the possibility is I replace this now, hoping that it’ll keep this thing going… or I replace both… without having to buy a whole new system — and finally the request itself: show me what the cost would be of doing the whole thing… that’s what I’m going to be able to — because you got to compare them all. What follows is a $1,044 fan motor, a compressor over $5,500, refrigerant arithmetic, and an admission that the heat exchanger price is unavailable. No specialist is named, no visit is offered, no lead exists. What would have flipped it: the customer had done the reasoning, reached the conclusion and asked for the comparison out loud. Call 1’s framing — the option comes before spending on the repair — was the exact sentence needed.

Gap 2 — A rising trend identified and left as information (Moderate)

Call 3 he does the hardest part of the job and then stops. The compressor reading has climbed since a recent maintenance visit, the voltage switch is burnt through its brass contact, and the customer already knows about the rust from a previous visit (that’s what he was saying before, there was some rust there, but then you can’t do much to it). His summary is honest — the main one that I’m concerned about is the compressor, because that other reading wasn’t that long ago, and we’re already starting to see increase in numbers — and ends at do you have any questions for me? No third option is built and no lead is created. What would strengthen it: a number that has moved in months is the most persuasive thing he owns. It should end in an appointment, not a question.

Gap 3 — Half the week is unscoreable (Moderate)

Four complete customer visits carry no ServiceTitan job number, including Call 8, where a full options presentation is delivered and formally declined, and Call 10, where a specialist visit is signed for and arranged. Three further recordings capture only arrival. The result is a seven-call denominator on a fourteen-recording week — the worst ratio on the roster. What would strengthen it: this is principally a dispatch and recording issue rather than a coaching one, but the effect is that his best Decision work this week (Call 10’s signature and same-day arrangement) cannot be counted in his rate, and his figures understate him.

Gap 4 — No review ask anywhere in fourteen recordings (Weak)

Call 1 ends with the customer offering him water, an exchange about a haircut, and feel free to call us if you have any questions or anything you need, brother. Call 4 closes with real nice meeting you, brother. I’m really glad that we’re able to go over everything for you. The rapport across this week is the warmest on the roster and it is never converted. What would strengthen it: his standard opening already thanks the customer for having him. The closing equivalent is one sentence.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Seven scoreable calls is a thin base, and the pattern should be read with that caveat. What is unambiguous is that the separation is not diagnostic — Call 6, a no-move, contains the most complete measurement work of his week.

  • Ready: Neutral and consistently Strong. Prior readings are pulled up on flips and no-moves alike.
  • Welcome: Neutral. The parking question and the gratitude opener appear on both outcomes.
  • Assessment: Neutral, and inverted if anything — Calls 3 and 6, both no-moves, contain his best trending and arithmetic.
  • Decision: The entire difference, and it is inconsistent rather than outcome-linked. Call 1 is a no-move with a well-framed offer; Call 6 is a no-move with none at all despite four direct requests.
  • Deliver: Neutral. The verification, the photographs and the absent review ask look the same either way.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Nathan builds the strongest possible case for an upgrade conversation and then does not reliably open one. On Call 3 he showed a customer a compressor reading that had risen in a matter of months and closed with a question. On Call 6 a customer laid out the repair-versus-replace comparison himself, asked for it four times in four different ways, and received an itemised repair estimate with a gap in it. Neither call lacked evidence, rapport or the customer’s willingness — Call 6’s customer was actively trying to be handed over. The gap is that the offer is currently something he remembers to make on some calls rather than something the findings trigger on all of them.

Beyond the Guide

Energy analysis stated as a monthly cost

Call 12: we do what’s called an energy analysis for you. With that compressor pulling that high amperage, I wanted to see how much it’s charging you guys extra for utilities. You guys are overpaying by 10% on utilities every month because that compressor is pulling more electricity than it should. Coaching implication: framing it as a recurring charge rather than a one-off inefficiency is the version customers act on, and he pairs it correctly with the option rather than with a repair.

Explaining why an opened system needs new refrigerant

Call 5: once we open up the system, once air hits that refrigerant, that Freon, it starts to become acidic, and then it’ll literally do the same issue that we’re seeing. It’ll start to eat at the windings of that compressor. Call 2 gives the commercial consequence: anytime we cut the system open, not only do we got to do the compressor, but you got to replace the refrigerant. So that’s why they get so expensive. Coaching implication: this pre-empts the most common objection to a compressor quote — that the refrigerant charge looks like padding — and it is technically correct.

Seasonal pricing mentioned on the specialist’s behalf

Call 2: I know that since we’re not in the middle of the summer, that pricing is fairly decent because once we get in the middle, all the people that supply us our equipment, the price goes up… and I know that they run certain promotions. Coaching implication: the instinct is to help, but it commits the Comfort Advisor to a market position before they have quoted anything, and the seasonal claim is being made in the middle of July. Better handled as a reason to take the appointment now than as a pricing forecast.

Recording the customer’s decline explicitly

Call 8: it looks like — so that means we’re not going with the immediate repairs, the future repairs, or the system update with the buyback plan for you. Coaching implication: stating the decision back before signing is good practice and rare in this corpus. It also gives the office a clean record of a genuine decline as distinct from an unfinished conversation.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Treat a comparison request as the appointment. Call 6’s customer asked four times and finished with because you got to compare them all. That sentence is the booking, and it needs to be answered with a time rather than a total.
  • End a rising trend with an appointment, not a question. Call 3 showed a customer a number that had climbed since a recent visit and closed with do you have any questions for me?
  • Carry Call 1’s framing to every call. Offering the comparison before money goes into a repair is the correct order and it is already his own wording.
  • Get job numbers onto the recordings. Four complete visits this week cannot be scored, including the one where he booked a specialist on a signature.
  • Add a review ask to the close. Fourteen recordings, the warmest customer rapport on the roster, and no ask.

Ranked by effect on turnover leads. Priorities 1 and 2 account for two of the three no-moves; Priority 4 is why his numbers understate his week; Priority 5 is upside.
