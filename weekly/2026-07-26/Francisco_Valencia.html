Bottom Line

The single behavior change that would move the needle most: on every call, the part he can fit today goes on the sheet at Sierra's price before any advice about where else it could be bought.

Strengths

1. Anchoring the upgrade to how long they are staying (Strong)

Francisco does not argue that a system should be replaced; he asks what the customer is planning and lets the arithmetic follow. Call 20: I'm not sure if this is a home you guys plan on staying here for another 2, 3, 4, 5 years. If it is, then repairs might not be the option for you guys, because repairs is more of a trying to keep up with it. Call 19 he uses the same frame to justify equipment tier: Unless you guys are going to be keeping this house forever… that's what we're looking for, like at least the next 20 years. So multi stage. Call 23 he applies it to his own reluctance: we're doing repairs on a 15-year-old system is already hard enough for me to offer. Coaching value: this converts an upgrade pitch into a planning question, which is why four of his seven scoreable calls flipped without a price argument.

2. The "by the book" argument that converts a cost objection (Strong)

Call 22's scorecard bands all four steps Strong and credits the reason directly: Francisco explains that Sierra replaces drain lines and components properly rather than patching with used parts. In the transcript he lays out the real constraint first — we would have to replace the coils and at that point would have to replace your condenser… because that coil is using the R22 refrigerant which they don't make anymore — then gives the customer the honest lead time on the cheaper path: it's a custom size to fit the whole size underneath, so we would have to order that custom metal pan. It could be two or three days. The customer's answer is I guess I should get both so that I know what the difference is going to be. Coaching value: that is the customer asking for Option C themselves, which is the cleanest form the flip takes.

3. Reading prior declined recommendations back to the customer (Strong)

Call 11 he reconstructs the whole history for an elderly customer who cannot remember it: they gave you an estimate for replacing, but you ended up not wanting to replace it. You wanted to just repair it at that time. And then what they charged you was 39.34 for those three items to get replaced. Call 20 he establishes the same ground before diagnosing: How long have you guys been here in this home?… Is this the first kind of an issue you guys had? Coaching value: the guide weights prior declined recommendations heavily in the Ready step and he is the only technician on the roster this week who visibly uses them.

4. Teaching the next technician on live calls (Strong)

Partners ride with him on at least seven recordings — Kevin on Calls 1 and 2, Jose on Calls 3, 4 and 9, Bradley on Call 10 — and the teaching is patient and specific. Call 9: when you close the attic, just use your shoe covers to, like, put them on your hands and then put the cover back on, and make sure that these wires are here before you put that down. It's not pinched. There's, like, a little groove that it has to go into. Call 5 he walks a trainee line by line through the report and invoicing screens. The trainee's own verdict, on Call 3: honestly, I appreciate this. The hands on part, like it gives me a little bit of confidence. Coaching value: he is doing real development work inside his own numbers, and that should be visible to management rather than absorbed.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Francisco has only one no-move against six positive outcomes, so the honest contrast is between his complete calls and his partial ones: Call 22, carded Strong on all four steps with every critical action passed, sits at one end, and Calls 19 and 21 — each carded a positive outcome with one critical action failed — show what is missing when the sequence is rushed.

  • Ready: Solid across the board. He reads history on the wins and the no-move alike — Call 24's mismatched system was identified from the 2022 replacement record — so this step does not discriminate.
  • Welcome: Strong on Call 22, Strong on wins on Call 21. The difference is placement, not content: when the block lands at the door the call carries cleanly, and when it lands late the critical actions start failing.
  • Assessment: Strong on both carded calls that band it. Call 21's dead capacitor is found by measurement and Call 20's 24-year-old R22 system is explained in full — the diagnosis is never the variable.
  • Decision: Strong on Call 22, Solid on Call 21, and this is where the outcomes separate. The specialist offer framed around the stay horizon is present on all four flips and absent from the calls that stall.
  • Deliver: Strong on Call 22, Moderate on Call 21. The review ask is missing on every call, wins included.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same measurement-led diagnosis, same honesty about what Sierra will and will not do — and a wide spread in how complete the call is. On Call 22 he told the customer the coil could not be sourced for R22, gave the real lead time on the cheaper pan, explained the by-the-book replacement standard, and the customer asked for both prices himself; carded Strong on all four steps with every critical action passed. On Call 20 he tied the specialist to the stay horizon — if this is a home you guys plan on staying here for another 2, 3, 4, 5 years… repairs is more of a trying to keep up with it — and closed the repair. On Call 21 he found the dead capacitor by measurement and taught the 8-to-12-year builder-grade lifespan, and the objection handling failed. On Call 16 the same quality of diagnosis ended with let me write up a report for you and no recorded outcome at all. The variable is not the finding: it is whether the sequence runs to the end while the recording is still on.

Beyond the Guide

He is running a training route inside his own numbers

Seven recordings carry a named partner and the teaching is deliberate — Call 9's shoe-covers-as-gloves trick, Call 5's software walkthrough, Call 1's explanation of how a condenser mount is supposed to be fitted. On Call 3 the trainee tells him it is working. Coaching implication: his outcome rates are being produced while developing other technicians, which is worth knowing before comparing him to anyone else on volume.

He defends Sierra's pricing structure without discounting it

Call 7: our prices don't increase in the summer. Our prices only increase when the manufacturer increases it… it's not like, hey, you're going to be paying premium price in the summer because it's busy. It's not like that for us because we have so much work. Coaching implication: this is a strong answer to the seasonal-gouging suspicion several customers on this roster raised, and it is the argument that should be attached to the capacitor price on Call 18 rather than an apology for it.

He walks into a lot of other people's butchery and documents it plainly

Call 1: I'm not sure who did that work, but that's not how [you mount it]. Call 12: six dampers on one system, they're all cut, and a return eliminated. Call 24: a mismatched system where the outdoor unit was replaced in 2022 and the indoor unit left original. Coaching implication: each of these is a pre-qualified system-update conversation; Call 24 is the one where it was pursued.

He sequences the specialist around both decision-makers

Call 24: it's going to be a lot of information. I don't know if your wife wants to be part of it or not, so that way you guys can both hear the different options… As far as like different systems, or if they have payment programs. He then checks the weekend on-call roster rather than promising blindly. Coaching implication: this is better handoff discipline than the card for that call reflects, and it is the habit that should be pulled forward into the calls where the approver is absent.

Recorded audio is carrying material that needs management attention

Call 20's recording continues into a colleague conversation that relays a racist remark attributed to a previous homeowner and includes crude language about a customer. Coaching implication: this is a conduct and recording-hygiene matter for management rather than a coaching item, and it is noted here so it is not lost; it does not affect any band in this report.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Put the part on the sheet at Sierra's price before mentioning any alternative. On Call 18 he told a customer you're better off probably going to go get your own about a capacitor sitting in his van. He already owns the justification — Call 7's our prices only increase when the manufacturer increases it and Call 22's by-the-book standard. The price gets stated with the reason attached, and the customer decides.
  • Run the full opening at the door, every time. Call 9's version is the best on the roster — before you kick me out, just give me a chance to fix it — and on Call 14 the same block arrives after the conversation has already started, on Calls 1, 12 and 15 not at all. His two carded Welcome bands differ for exactly this reason.
  • Finish the objection, not just the explanation. Call 21 is carded with objections failed on a customer who opened frustrated and asked You'll be able to fix it; Call 19 is carded with questions failed. Both still produced outcomes, which means closing those two gaps converts good calls into complete ones rather than rescuing bad ones.

Keeping the training conversation off the customer recording is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — the coaching he is doing is real work, whereas the give-away pricing on Call 18 is money left on the table on a call he had already won.
