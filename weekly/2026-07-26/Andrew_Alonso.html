Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Andrew hands off Option C on 57% of his scoreable calls (4 of 7) and wins repairs on 14% (1 of 7), and his defining strength is handing the decision to the customer rather than taking it from them (Strong) — Call 15: Whatever the issue is, I brought out multiple options. That way you guys tell me what to do. I'm not telling you what to do… I have to get your approval before anything can be done. Is that fair? His single biggest gap is selling the Comfort Advisor visit on its price rather than on its urgency (Strong on wins) — his own manager names it on the recording of Call 7: when you recommend, like, replacements… don't make it sound like that's just like a free option. Try to build some urgency behind it. Call 9 is that gap costing him a call: after finding and tracing a live gas leak for free, quoting a $251 gas stop and lumping roughly $8,600. Actually, I think it was about $9,400 of coming failures, he offers I can call my office right now and we can get somebody out here and the customer answers Oh no, we're not gonna — if none of these are fatal right now. The behaviour behind it is that he frames the specialist as a cost-free convenience, so a customer who has just been told nothing is fatal has no reason to accept. The single behaviour change that would move the needle most: attach the specialist to the consequence he has already proven — the carbon monoxide, the phase-out, the second failing component — instead of to the fact that it is free.

Strengths

1. Refusing to make the decision for the customer (Strong)

Andrew's opening is unusual on this roster because it explicitly hands the customer authorship. Call 8: whatever the issue is, I'm going to build multiple options. I'll let you tell me what to do. I don't like to tell you guys… because I have to have your approval before I finish. Call 16 repeats it and adds the reasoning: It's a lot better if you're telling me what you want done than if I tell you. Call 12: I gotta build you up multiple options. I prefer you guys to tell me what you want done rather than me to tell you. Coaching value: Call 18's scorecard bands all four steps Strong and Call 15's bands Assessment and Deliver Strong; a customer who has been told the choice is theirs from the doorstep does not have to be argued into the specialist visit at the end.

2. Refusing to charge for work he cannot stand behind (Strong)

Call 6, facing a blown fuse he cannot explain: there's only a few things that can pop that big of a fuse, so we're very worried about quoting you to replace a fuse… if I replace that fuse, then I'm charging you, and then it pops again. Because it's something bigger. Call 9 he stops a 208-minute call to chase a live gas leak and declines to bill it: right here I smelled the active gas. I need to go grab some soap bubbles and find out where the gas is coming from because that's a safety hazard. I'm not going to charge you to find out where it is. I just want to make sure that you guys are safe to operate the heater when the winter comes. Coaching value: this is the credibility his Option C conversations run on, and it is the reason he can price nine thousand dollars of future work on the same call without the customer flinching.

3. Building the phase-out into a complete customer-facing briefing (Strong)

Call 2: 2009 EPA said no more R22. 2025 they said no more 410A. Now we have to use an even higher pressure, but in response to that every manufacturer said 30% more per year. Call 5 runs the full three-generation version for a retrofitted system: R407c is the exact same thing as R22, but it's staying near the exact same. That's why they used it… they switched to R410A in 2025. EPA did the exact same thing to 410A that they did with 22. Call 12 he does it for a customer who half-knows it and corrects them gently. Coaching value: this is the strongest structural argument for a system update anyone on the roster has, and he delivers it accurately every time.

4. Teaching by analogy until the customer can repeat it (Strong)

Call 7's five-box model of an air handler; Call 5's thermodynamics framing — in a given space, if pressure goes down, temperature will go down… the systems have lost some of their refrigerant, so the temperature is going down, which is allowing the humidity in the air to condense; Call 6's capacitor walkthrough with both readings against the rating: right here where it says 10 and then 40… the 10 side goes to that fan motor, and it tested at 8.3… the 40 side tested at 31. Both of his banded scorecards single this out — Call 15 for if heat goes up, amps go up, if amps go up, heat goes up, Call 18 for the car-battery and oil-leak comparisons. Coaching value: it is the reason his customers on Calls 6, 15 and 18 accepted a specialist rather than a second opinion.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Selling the specialist as free instead of as urgent (Strong on wins)

His manager identifies this on Call 7's own recording: when you recommend, like, replacements, be like, hey, this last option here is so you can get all your options. Compare and contrast repair pricing for replacement… don't make it sound like that's just like a free option. Try to build some urgency behind it. Call 6 shows the framing in action — that's exactly why this estimate is free. We pay these guys salary and they sign very stringent contracts — and it worked. Call 9 shows it failing: after I can call my office right now the customer answers if none of these are fatal right now and it ends. Impact: Andrew has already produced the urgency argument earlier on both calls (carbon monoxide on Call 7, nine thousand dollars of coming failures on Call 9) and then does not connect it to the offer.

Gap 2 — Completing a maintenance on failing parts with no option at all (Weak)

Call 2 is the clearest no-move in his week. He shows the customer an oil-leaking condenser fan motor — this nastiness is the oil from the motor itself… it's sprayed in a perfect line — and then this is one of the most burnt, burnt power switches I think I've ever seen. Right. You can even see that it's starting to snap apart from wear and tear, on a ten-year-old system he has just briefed on the refrigerant phase-out. When the customer asks How much is that? the answer is I'll get you the price for that at the end. The call ends with I look forward to seeing you next year, man. Impact: the most burnt switch he says he has ever seen produced neither a repair nor a handoff.

Impact: on Call 9 a $9,400 figure that the technician himself corrected mid-sentence was the last number the customer heard before declining.

Gap 5 — Calls at 208 and 249 minutes (Moderate)

Impact: the two calls where he invested most produced the least, and at this length the board cannot absorb many of them.

Gap 6 — The five-star seed planted and never harvested (Weak)

No recording in eighteen contains the ask. Impact: he does the harder half on every call, including on Call 9 where a customer thanked him twice for the free gas-leak work, and never makes the request.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference between Andrew's flips and his no-moves is whether the specialist offer is attached to a consequence: on Call 6 he refused to quote a fuse he could not stand behind and the customer accepted a specialist visit that day; on Call 9 he offered the same visit as a free convenience and was refused in one sentence.

  • Ready: Strong on both sides. He had the warranty and repair history on Call 2 as thoroughly as on Call 16 — this step does not discriminate for him.
  • Welcome: Strong throughout, including on the no-moves. Call 2 opens as well as any call in his week and still ends with I look forward to seeing you next year.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides, and Call 9's — a traced gas leak, a heat exchanger, three failing motors — is the most thorough in his week. That it produced nothing is the clearest evidence the gap sits downstream.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus a free-of-charge framing that fails. Call 6's that's exactly why this estimate is free. We pay these guys salary worked because it followed a refusal to quote; Call 9's identical offer followed a number the customer had already decided was not fatal.
  • Deliver: Strong on wins. He revisits membership properly on Call 9 and both banded scorecards record Deliver Strong; the review ask is absent on wins and no-moves alike.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same warranty homework, same teaching by analogy — and two very different endings. On Call 6 he told the customer he would not quote a fuse he could not stand behind (if I replace that fuse, then I'm charging you, and then it pops again), then offered the free estimate as protection rather than a favour, and a specialist attended that day. On Call 15 and Call 18 the same sequence produced flips carded Strong across the board. On Call 9 he found a live gas leak, fixed the diagnosis for nothing, priced over $8,600. Actually, I think it was about $9,400 of failures — and then offered the specialist as a convenience: if you want, I can call my office right now. The customer's answer was immediate: if none of these are fatal right now. On Call 2, the most burnt power switch he has ever seen produced no option at all. The variable is not diagnosis, trust or explanation, all of which he has on every call — it is whether the specialist offer is tied to the consequence he has already proved, exactly as his own manager told him on Call 7.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Attach the specialist to the consequence, not to the price. Andrew currently offers I can call my office right now (Call 9) with no reason attached, and his manager already told him why that fails. The fix is to name what he has just proven — the carbon monoxide risk on Call 7, the $9,400 of coming failures on Call 9, the phase-out on Call 2 — and then offer the visit as the way to weigh it, exactly as he did on Call 6 when he refused to quote a fuse he could not stand behind.
  • No completed maintenance leaves without an option. Call 2 ended with an oil-leaking fan motor and one of the most burnt, burnt power switches I think I've ever seen and a goodbye. Any recording where he shows a customer a failing component must end with that component priced and the third option offered, not with I'll get you the price for that at the end.
  • Fix the recording setup, then ask for the review. Eleven of eighteen recordings are unusable and his manager flagged the cause on Call 7 (I need you to start recording your calls). Once the evidence exists, the ask costs seconds: he already tells every customer we're at 4.9, we're trying to hit that fifth star and never finishes the sentence.

Tightening call length is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — the depth on Calls 5 and 9 is genuine work, whereas the free-not-urgent framing of the specialist is what separates his four flips from his two no-moves.
