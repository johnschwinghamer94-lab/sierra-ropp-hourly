Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Benjamin hands off Option C on 55% of his scoreable calls (6 of 11) and wins repairs on 18% (2 of 11), on the largest assessable sample on the roster, and his defining strength is talking a customer out of a repair he could have sold (Strong) — Call 22's scorecard credits the flip directly to his refusal of the cheaper option on a twenty-year-old system: you want to burn a lot of money into an older system. His single biggest gap is letting a cost fear stand when the answer is a piece of paper he was about to write anyway (Strong on wins) — Call 29 is carded a no-move with all four critical actions passed, and its fix note is exact: the customer feared the cost without warranty approval, and the estimate should have been reframed as the evidence needed to make the warranty claim. The behaviour behind it is that he presents the numbers well and then waits: on Call 20 the customer asks How much would it be just to replace the whole system? and gets the right answer about the specialist, but that call is still carded with options and objections failed and ends on I have to call the wife and talk to her. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: when a customer's objection is money they may not have to spend — a warranty, a claim, a co-decision-maker — hand them the estimate as the tool for winning that argument rather than as the bill.

Strengths

1. Refusing the repair he could have sold (Strong)

Call 22 is carded Strong on all four steps with every critical action passed, and its scorecard traces the flip to one move: he declined the cheaper repair on a twenty-year-old system with you want to burn a lot of money into an older system, and the honesty is what earned the pivot to replacement. Call 5 carries the same frame in the customer's own words — she tells him I want long term, not just a Band-Aid — after he has set up the most educated best decision for you. Call 16 he does it with financing rather than pressure: how much you want to pay monthly or per paycheck… if you're like, oh, 50 bucks a month, it'll take you long to pay it off. Coaching value: six handoffs from eleven calls is the highest on the roster and this is the mechanism — the customer hears a technician arguing against his own easiest sale.

2. The consent question, asked every time (Strong)

Before he finds anything, he asks permission to raise it: if I have any concerns, do you want me to bring that to your attention or keep it to myself? (Call 2), and the whole frame around it — we're going to do a thorough evaluation of everything. Check for cleanliness, proper function and overall safety. Call 16 adds the reason: usually when one thing's going on, there's multiple things going on. So I want you guys to be aware of that as well. Coaching value: it removes the "he's just upselling" objection before it can form, which is why nine of his eleven scoreable calls reached the Decision step with the customer still engaged.

3. Meeting an awkward fact without judgement (Strong)

Call 27's scorecard singles this out: he asked you guys smoke in the house?, and when the answer was yes he used it as the explanation rather than a reproach — okay, that's caused a lot of damage. So it's very clogged up and not able to breathe. It's not able to do its job. He then looped in the brother who actually decides, by phone, during the visit. The card records the customer going from uncertain to action. Coaching value: the diagnosis, the cause and the decision-maker all get handled inside twenty-three minutes; this is his most efficient call of the week.

4. Explaining electronics in terms of the body (Strong)

Call 8: your control board is like the brain of the whole system… you've got a lot of these hot spots right there. Best way I can put that is like it's nerve damage. On the same call he shows the oil loss on a motor and links it forward: that's what causes it to seize up. Call 21 he describes the progression of a failure the customer can already feel: to get worse and worse and worse to the point where there's none. And you're like, oh, gosh. Coaching value: both scorecards that band Assessment record Strong, and it is the reason his customers accept the specialist rather than a second opinion.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Letting a warranty fear end the call (Strong on wins)

Call 29 is the sharpest coaching item in this report because nothing went wrong technically: all four critical actions pass and it is still a no-move. Its scorecard names the miss exactly — the customer feared the cost without warranty approval, and the estimate should have been reframed as the evidence needed to make the claim. The transcript shows the customer settling into the wait: right now I'm just gonna run it until it stops. Okay. And then I'll call you, and Benjamin agrees: I mean, that's the best way to do it. Contrast Call 2, where he understands warranty companies perfectly — he tells a colleague they deny all their claims — and does not turn that knowledge into a reason to get the paperwork now. Impact: a customer with a live warranty question walked away with a plan to wait for a failure, on a call his own scorecard says he executed correctly.

Gap 2 — The absent co-decision-maker reached after the visit, not during it (Strong on wins)

Call 20 is carded a flip with options and objections failed, and the reason is in the last exchange: I have to call the wife and talk to her, then Benjamin's own question — Where do you think that she would lean towards? — answered with I don't know. When I tell her how much the good's gonna cost you, we're gonna see. Call 27 shows the version that works: the brother who decides is looped in by phone during the visit. Impact: on Call 20 the specialist was offered to someone who had already told him he could not authorise it, and the objection he was left to relay was the price.

Gap 3 — Eighteen of twenty-nine recordings never reach the Decision step (Weak)

Calls 1 through 18, with one exception, end before an option exists — Call 8 with a control board and a seizing motor both explained and nothing priced, Call 16 after financing has been discussed and a coil report promised by email, Call 10 after 141 minutes with farewells and no decision audible. Impact: even with the largest scoreable sample on the roster, more than half his week's Decision-step behaviour is unrecorded, and Calls 8 and 16 in particular look like calls that reached an outcome the recording did not keep.

Gap 4 — One call where the structure collapsed entirely (Weak)

Call 28's scorecard is the only all-fail card in this window: Welcome, Assessment and Deliver Moderate, Decision Weak, and expectations, questions, options and objections all failed, with the outcome unknown. Its fix note describes the cause as a fragmented diagnostic that was hard to follow, and recommends starting with a clear problem statement and the customer's goal. The recording is 50 minutes logged and 396 words captured, and it ends with him phoning another technician. Impact: the same technician who is carded Strong on all four steps on Call 22 is carded Weak on Decision three days later, which points at call structure rather than skill.

Gap 5 — Non-customer audio carrying remarks about customers (Weak)

Several recordings continue into colleague conversation, and on Call 3 and Call 25 that conversation includes derogatory remarks about the customers or people connected to the call, and Call 15 includes disparaging comments about the SILO role itself. Call 6 is 111 minutes logged and 368 words captured, almost all of it shop talk about pay and licensing. Impact: this is a conduct and privacy exposure on the technician with the highest handoff count on the team, and it is also consuming the recording time that would otherwise be capturing his Decision step.

Gap 6 — The five-star seed planted and never harvested (Weak)

His expectations block is complete and it stops at the consent question — no five-star line appears in it, and no review ask appears in any of twenty-nine recordings. Call 11 ends with a genuinely warm exit and a joke about not punching holes in the wall (I did complete the job. I didn't punch any holes in the wall, and you didn't punch me), on a call where the repair was signed and the warranty explained. Impact: the largest volume of positive outcomes on the roster is producing no captured reviews.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference between Benjamin's eight positive outcomes and his three no-moves is whether the person who could say yes was reachable during the visit, and whether the money objection was answered with a document: on Call 27 the decision-maker was brought in by phone and the call flipped, and on Call 29 the customer's warranty question was left for him to resolve alone.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides. Call 26's note-checking is his best example and it produced a flip; Call 28's absence of the same discipline is carded Moderate with every critical action failed.
  • Welcome: Strong on the wins, Solid to Moderate on the calls that stall. The consent question is present on nearly all of them, so what varies is structure rather than warmth.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides — Call 29 is carded with all four critical actions passing and still produced no move, which places the gap squarely at the objection.
  • Decision: Strong on the flips and specifically incomplete on the no-moves. Call 22 carries a refusal of the easy repair and Call 20, though it flipped, is carded with options and objections failed.
  • Deliver: Solid throughout, Strong on Call 22 and Moderate on Call 28, with no review ask on wins or no-moves alike.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same consent question at the door, same ability to make a failing board or a clogged coil comprehensible — and outcomes that separate on one thing. On Call 22 he refused the cheap repair on a twenty-year-old system — you want to burn a lot of money into an older system — and it is carded Strong on all four steps. On Call 27 he asked you guys smoke in the house?, explained the damage without reproach, and got the brother who decides onto the phone during the visit; it flipped. On Call 20 he gave the right answer to How much would it be just to replace the whole system? and it still ends on I have to call the wife and talk to her, carded with options and objections failed. On Call 28 the diagnostic was fragmented and every critical action failed. The variable is not diagnosis or trust — it is whether the person who can say yes is in the conversation and whether the money objection is met with a document.

Beyond the Guide

He understands home warranty companies better than he uses that knowledge

Call 2, to a colleague after a home-warranty customer: deny all their claims. Yeah. But that's not my job, and then the practical read — I've had a few that I've sold because they have home warranty. Call 29's fix note asks for exactly this to be turned outward. Coaching implication: he already knows the claim will probably be denied and that the estimate is what the customer needs to argue with; saying so to the customer is the whole fix.

He escalates upward when a customer needs something he cannot authorise

Call 19 ends with him calling the owner directly — let me call him real quick, see what we can do for you — and Call 28 with him calling another SILO technician for help. Call 8 he has a colleague with him on site (AJ, sorry, I meant to ask, are you able to grab my clipboard?). Coaching implication: he uses the organisation rather than improvising, which is the same instinct a clean Comfort Advisor handoff needs.

He sells the membership as continuity, not a discount

Call 4: have you ever thought about joining the membership where we're constantly coming out here, where it's not 39 each time, but you have the plumbing check — framed around a customer who had just told him smaller companies either went out of business… they're seasonal, they only work during the summertime. Coaching implication: he is the only technician this week who used a competitor's fragility as the membership argument; that belongs in the Deliver step on every call where Option A work is done.

His financing conversation is customer-led

Call 16: how much you want to pay monthly or per paycheck. Really?… if you're like, oh, 50 bucks a month, it'll take you long to pay it off. He starts from the customer's cash flow rather than the total. Coaching implication: this is the answer to Call 20's price objection and it was not used there.

Recording hygiene is a live issue on his route

Call 6 is 111 logged minutes and 368 captured words, nearly all colleague conversation; Calls 3, 15 and 25 all continue past the customer into remarks that should not be on a company recording. Coaching implication: this is a management conversation rather than a coaching item, and it is separately the reason his Decision-step evidence is thinner than his outcome count deserves.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Hand the estimate over as warranty evidence, not as a bill. Call 29 is carded a no-move with every critical action passed because a cost fear went unanswered, and the customer left saying I'm just gonna run it until it stops. He already knows what he needs to say — on Call 2 he described warranty companies as places that deny all their claims. The move is to tell the customer the written estimate is what they use to prove what it would cost out of pocket.
  • Get the co-decision-maker into the call, not into the follow-up. On Call 27 he phoned the brother during the visit and it flipped; on Call 20 he asked Where do you think that she would lean towards? of a customer who had already said I have to call the wife, and that call is carded with options and objections failed. Ask to call them from the driveway.
  • Open with the problem statement and the customer's goal. Call 28's card is the only all-fail in the window and its fix note is explicit about the cause: a fragmented diagnostic with no clear problem statement. His consent question is already excellent; putting "here is what I think is happening, and what are you hoping to get out of today" in front of it would have prevented that call.

Adding the five-star line and the review ask is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — he is converting eight of eleven calls, whereas the unanswered warranty objection on Call 29 is a positive outcome he had already earned and did not collect.
