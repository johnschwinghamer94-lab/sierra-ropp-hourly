Strengths

1. Explaining electrical load in the body's terms (Strong)

Call 8 is the clearest technical explanation in Mario's week and it never uses a technical word the customer has to look up: Works like our heart. The higher the blood pressure from the strokes, heart attacks, none of that fun stuff. When it exceeds. Exceeds 79, we're at 95. He then repeats it for the second unit with the actual figures — The other one, This one's 67, pulling 70. So it's above it still playing more than it should — and adds the supply-chain reality without drama: they stopped manufacturing these parts for these units because they phased out the refrigerant that you have in these units. Call 13's scorecard records the same skill independently, crediting him with connecting the visible symptom (cycling) to root cause in terms the customer could understand and act on. Coaching value: this is the hardest part of earning the right to present and he does it in two languages, which means his flat repair rate is a Decision-step problem, not a credibility problem.

2. Asking the customer what they actually want from the visit (Strong)

He does not assume the work order is the job. Call 6: Anything in particular you want me to take a look at? Anything you're trying to get out of this maintenance? Call 7 he goes further and asks what should happen with what he finds: It's going to be a thorough evaluation. So I'll be checking cleanliness, making sure these units are working properly. Mainly safeties, if anything's out of specs. What would you like me to do with that information? — and the customer answers with a specific deliverable, Give us a print out of that. On Call 4 he establishes the decision chain before he starts: if we see anything, we notice anything, we have to let Derek know first. Coaching value: he consistently finds out who decides and what they want before he touches a unit, which is exactly the setup an option sheet is built on.

3. Parking, permission and expectations on every single call (Strong)

Fourteen out of fourteen recordings open the same way — I parked right there. Is that okay? (Calls 7, 8, 9, 12) — and the expectations block follows in almost identical language each time, including the approval promise and the same-day close: We don't start without your approval. But with an approval we can get things started as soon as today (Calls 5, 6, 10, 14). On Call 3 he checks the parking twice because he is partly across a driveway: I parked. I'm blocking half your driveway. I'm not covering theirs. Is that good? Coaching value: the front of his call needs no work at all, which means coaching time can go entirely to the Decision step.

4. Doing the small things without being asked or paid (Solid)

Call 6, when the customer asks whether filter changes cost extra: I'm already here, ma'am. I'm not gonna charge you for that. Call 9 he takes the old filters away — Take those old filters with me. That way you don't have to worry about — and Call 5, before going into an attic, he protects the room below: I put something on the floor, too. That way all the insulation that's up there doesn't fall… I'll make sure it's all clean by the time I leave, sir. Coaching value: these are exactly the moments the five-star review is earned, and he currently banks the goodwill and never withdraws it.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Sequencing the work instead of presenting the options (Weak)

Call 8 is the defining example. Mario has just told the customer that both compressors exceed their rated draw, that the units are original builder-grade equipment at the end of a Vegas life span, and that to get parts, the major parts, takes about six to eight weeks if they were to break. Then he narrows it himself: But my thing is one fire at a time. Let's see what it takes to take care of the upstairs first. And then we'll put our attention after to the other unit still working. Two dying compressors on obsolete refrigerant is the textbook System Update conversation, and he chose to make it a single-unit repair conversation instead — and then the recording ends before even that reached a price. Impact: the biggest Option C setup of his week was deliberately scoped down to a repair he never quoted.

Gap 2 — Eleven of fourteen recordings never reach a Decision step (Weak)

The dominant fact of Mario's week is absence. Calls 1 through 11 all contain a competent Welcome, most contain a real diagnosis, and every one of them ends with the same sentence in some form — I'll step outside, put everything on paper, give you all the information (Call 6), I'm gonna be in my van doing all the paperwork. What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference between Mario's flips and his no-move is how far he pushed the discovery: on Call 12 he kept asking until the customer laid out three previous technicians and a bypassed damper, and on Call 14 he accepted Not really and went to work.

  • Ready: Solid on both flips and on the no-move — no discrimination. Call 12's history-gathering happens at the door, the same as everywhere else; what differs is how long he stays on it.
  • Welcome: Strong across all three scoreable calls and all eleven others. For Mario this step is genuinely not the variable, which is unusual and worth telling him.
  • Assessment: Strong on both flips, thin on the no-move. Call 12's scorecard credits him with exposing that previous technicians were just bypassing stuff which creates more problems; Call 14's records the opposite instruction about finishing discovery first.
  • Decision: Strong and Solid on the flips with all four critical actions passed; on Call 8, his richest diagnosis of the week, it collapses into one fire at a time. This is where his week is decided.
  • Deliver: Carded Strong on both flips and Solid elsewhere — he always leaves the home well, and never leaves it with a review request.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same faultless opening, same picture-by-picture diagnosis — and three completely different endings. On Call 12 he took the customer's history apart until the real problem surfaced and the call flipped; on Call 13, in Spanish, he explained the startup surge and it flipped again. On Call 14 the customer said Not really to his goal question and the call ended as a no-move with the scorecard note Ensure all discovery questions are asked before presenting options. And on Call 8 — two compressors over spec, obsolete refrigerant, six-to-eight-week parts — he answered the customer's direct price question with I'd have to look at the price book and narrowed the job to one fire at a time. The variable is not rapport, permission or technical explanation, all of which he has on every call: it is whether he keeps going until there is a complete option set on the table.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Two failing components means Option C, not "one fire at a time." On Call 8 Mario had two compressors over rated draw and a refrigerant out of production and chose to sequence the work: Let's see what it takes to take care of the upstairs first. The rule should be mechanical — the second major failure on the same property triggers the full sheet and a call to the office for a Comfort Advisor, before he goes to the van.
  • Say a number in the home. No price appears in any of his fourteen recordings, and on Call 8 a direct question got I'd have to look at the price book. He already asks customers what their power bill runs — how much are you paying? Are you over 200, 300? (Call 6) — so he has the budget context; a spoken range at the unit, while the customer is standing next to him, costs nothing and removes the cold reveal.
  • Do not accept "not really" as the end of discovery. Call 14's no-move is carded exactly this way, and Call 12's flip is what happens when he keeps going — the customer eventually volunteers three previous technicians and a bypassed damper. When the goal question gets a flat answer, follow it with the history question that worked on Call 12 rather than moving to the equipment.

Bringing the right filters and confirming parking are worth a quick consistency reminder, but they are a distant fourth — the front of his call is already the most reliable on the team, whereas eleven recordings ending before an option exists is the whole story of his week.
