Strengths

1. Staying on site to hand the customer over in person (Strong on wins)

Call 18 contains the handoff mechanics exactly as the guide describes them. Cole offers the third option, gets a signature on the options sheet — I just grab a signature off it… Stating that I went over the information — and then does not leave: I usually hang out till he gets here, just so when he gets here, I can show him where everything's at, tell him what's going on… That way he's not walking in blind and everyone's on the same page. He even offers the customer the choice of where he waits (You want me to hang out in here? You want me to hang out in the van?) and leaves the ladder up for the advisor. Coaching value: this is the primary win of the SILO role executed to the letter, and it is why his scorecard on that call bands Deliver Strong.

2. Saying both numbers off the data tag out loud (Strong)

Cole does not tell customers a part is bad; he shows them the ceiling and the reading. Call 17: It has an LRA right here of 68… And when it did start itself up, we're pulling in 99. Call 15: its max rating for what it pulls in when it starts up, as far as power, is 109. The reading that we were getting today was 130. Call 18 he does it for the blower too: It's rated for an 8.1… What I saw while I was up there is right at the max. Call 7 he reports a genuine zero: 9.5 on the amps on the fan side, on the compressor side, we're getting zeros. Coaching value: a measured number against a printed limit is the least arguable evidence in the trade, and he produces it on every demand call without exception.

3. Drawing the entire system before naming a fault (Strong)

Call 3: The way I always explain everything is I always draw the system out. A lot of my homeowners have no idea what's actually up there and how it works. So it's a little bit easier than me just throwing part names at you — then the full circuit, compressor to mixing boxes to cooling coil at about 34 degrees to blower to thermostat. Call 1 repeats it word for word and lands the consequence: see that brown area? So that portion of the coil is rusting. And what rust does is it eats through metal. When it eats through that metal, it's going to cause a leak. Coaching value: the drawing is what makes his rust and phase-out arguments land, and on Call 2 it is why the customer concluded I know we need a new unit before he priced anything.

4. Warning customers off spending money he could have taken (Strong on wins)

Call 15: I hate to see you guys light $3,000 on fire and then go for a permanent solution. Call 18, almost identically: I'd hate to see you guys put $4,000 in. Your compressor goes out and you guys just let $4,000 on fire. Call 2 labels his own immediate repairs honestly before pricing them: I want you to take in mind these are band aids though. Coaching value: this is the sentence that turns his flips from an upsell into advice, and it is the direct reason both of those customers engaged with the specialist rather than resisting one.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — A complete diagnosis that ends in a $39 invoice (Weak)

Call 17 is the most expensive hour in Cole's week because nothing was missing except the ask. He measured the compressor at 99 against 68, explained that R22 was banned in 2010… no longer manufacture it, no longer import it, ballparked upwards 7 to 8,000, showed rust going all the way down the coil and a blower motor whose carpet of dust proves an oil leak — and then closed the call with for today's visit, it's just the $39 for the tune up on the two systems and I'll get everything emailed over. No priced sheet in the home, no specialist, no next date. Impact: it is his only no-move and it came from a call where the diagnosis was as strong as either of his flips.

Gap 2 — Fourteen of eighteen recordings never reach a Decision step (Weak)

Calls 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 16 all contain a competent Welcome and most contain a real presented finding, and every one ends the same way — I can step out, build that report for you, come back in and go over it with you (Call 1), I'm just going to grab my clipboard out of my van (Call 3), go ahead and call Palina and then see what she wants to do (Call 16). Calls 7 and 14 are the most costly: a compressor reading zero amps and a 20-year-old R22 system, neither with a recorded outcome. Impact: his rates rest on four calls out of eighteen, and eleven customers who watched him diagnose their systems have no captured moment where they were asked to decide anything.

Gap 3 — Describing himself out of the replacement conversation (Weak)

It appears on three separate calls in near-identical words. Call 2: these are more questions for like, because I don't do like replacements. I just do repairs to see what's going on. Call 15: I do work on the repair side. Call 17: I just do the repair side. I'm not entirely sure. On Calls 2 and 15 he immediately recovers — I do have guys that come out and give quotes, I would get one of my comfort advisors out here… they give you a price on what it would be to start over fresh with brand new warranties — and both flip. On Call 17 the recovery never comes and the sentence just sits there. Impact: the disclaimer is harmless when it is followed by the specialist and fatal when it is not, and that is precisely the difference between his three flips and his no-move.

Gap 4 — Running repair-versus-replace dispatches "like a normal call" (Moderate)

Call 16's recording captures a colleague asking him directly how he opens those jobs — when you do repair versus replaces, do you say like, at the beginning, hey, this is just the first part of a two part appointment… I'm going to have someone else come out to give replacement pricing? — and Cole's answer is No, I run it like a normal call. That's what I do. Call 16 was a landlord-tenant repair-versus-replace dispatch that the same conversation described as a free flip, and its recording ends with no handoff captured. Impact: on jobs Sierra has already qualified as upgrade conversations, he declines to set the two-visit expectation up front, which leaves the specialist to be introduced cold at the end of a long call.

Gap 5 — Never asking for a review, on a technician customers already praise unprompted (Weak)

The five-star seed is in every expectations block — Above all, we're here to give you a five star experience — and no recording in eighteen contains the ask. Call 13's customer had already done it for him: I called the office today and I told them that you and Mario were outstanding… I wanted them to send a recommendation to your boss. On the same visit she gave him ice packs, two sodas and water bottles. Call 1's customer brought him a Powerade; Call 11's offered ginger ale. Impact: the goodwill exists on nearly every call and is converted only when a customer thinks of it themselves.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference between Cole's three flips and his no-move is whether the sentence I just do the repair side is followed by the specialist: on Calls 2, 15 and 18 it is, and on Call 17 it is not.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides. He knew Call 17's system age and history as well as Call 18's — this step does not discriminate for him at all.
  • Welcome: Strong everywhere, including on the recordings that go nowhere. Call 3, which never reached an option, carries his most complete script of the week.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides, and Call 17's is arguably the best in his week — LRA against measured, R22 phase-out, rust, oil-fouled blower. That it produced nothing is the clearest possible proof the gap is downstream.
  • Decision: Strong on wins versus absent. Calls 2, 15 and 18 all carry a priced immediate, a priced future and the third option; Call 17 has no sheet, no prices in the home and no advisor.
  • Deliver: Strong on wins. He stayed for the advisor on Call 18 and confirmed the next-morning appointment on Call 2; on Call 17 he took payment and emailed a PDF.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same hand-drawn system, same two numbers off the data tag — and two endings that turn on one sentence. On Call 2 he priced the band-aids at 3287, ballparked the heat exchanger at four to six thousand, said so we're already up to almost ten grand and booked the advisor for the next morning; on Call 18 he offered the third option, took a signature and waited on site to brief the advisor in person; on Call 15 he laid out over 10 grand in repairs and the customer asked for the specialist themselves. On Call 17 he measured 99 against a rated 68, explained the R22 ban and a rusting coil just as well, said I just do the repair side. I'm not entirely sure — and then charged $39 for the tune up and left. The variable is not the diagnosis, the drawing or the trust: it is whether the three options get said out loud in the home before he walks to the van.

Beyond the Guide

Customers feed him and then call his manager

Call 13's customer stocked him with ice packs, sodas and water bottles and then said I called the office today and I told them that you and Mario were outstanding… so your boss has heard. Call 1's brought a Powerade mid-attic; Call 11's offered ginger ale twice; Calls 16 and 17 both offered water. Coaching implication: this level of unsolicited goodwill is rare and entirely unharvested — Cole is the technician on this roster for whom a scripted review ask would pay back fastest.

He builds the phase-out argument into a customer-facing script

Call 1: The refrigerant… is called R410. First of 2025 they banned that. It's no longer manufactured, no longer imported to the US. So they came out with a new one called A2L… it's like the wrong gas in your car. Call 17 and Call 14 run the R22 version — banned in 2010… whatever's out there is out there — with a spoken price consequence. Coaching implication: this is a complete and repeatable Option C argument; it currently appears on calls that end in an emailed report as often as on calls that flip.

He does work nobody asked for and does not charge for it

Call 2: I also took a few extra minutes. I did an energy analysis for you. Call 3 he cleaned the outdoor coils on a system he had no concerns about. Call 15 he pushed back on the customer's own proposal to spend $3,000. Coaching implication: the extra work builds exactly the credibility his Decision step needs; it is being spent and not banked.

He deliberately does not pre-frame two-visit appointments

Asked directly by a colleague on Call 16 whether he sets up the two-part expectation on repair-versus-replace dispatches, his answer is No, I run it like a normal call. Coaching implication: this is a considered choice rather than an oversight, which makes it worth discussing rather than correcting by instruction — but it means the advisor is always introduced cold at the end of an already long call.

He handles the EPA constraint as a customer-protection argument

Call 18, asked whether they can just top up the refrigerant: it's against the EPA, breaking the EPA laws to put refrigerant into that system, because it is leaking, followed immediately by an escalation — I also called my manager… if you want to go that route, we could be out here first thing in the morning to at least start the leak search. Coaching implication: refusal plus a same-day alternative plus a manager call is exactly the sequence that produced his best-carded call of the week.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Never end a diagnosed call with an emailed report. On Call 17 Cole had everything — the readings, the ban, the rust, the ballpark — and finished with just the $39 for the tune up and I'll get everything emailed over. The rule is simple: if the evaluation found something, the three options get said out loud in the home and the specialist gets offered before he goes to the van.
  • Attach the advisor to the disclaimer, every time. I just do the repair side is fine on Calls 2 and 15 because it is immediately followed by I would get one of my comfort advisors out here… they give you a price on what it would be to start over fresh with brand new warranties. On Call 17 the same sentence was left hanging. The disclaimer and the offer should be one sentence, not two.
  • Ask for the review he is already being given. The five-star seed is in every opening and the ask is in none of eighteen recordings — while Call 13's customer phoned his manager unprompted and Call 1's brought him a drink. Adding it to the exit costs nothing and he has more banked goodwill than anyone else on the roster.

Pre-framing repair-versus-replace dispatches as two-visit appointments is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — Cole's flips land without it, whereas walking out of a full diagnosis with no option on the table is the single thing that cost him a call this week.
