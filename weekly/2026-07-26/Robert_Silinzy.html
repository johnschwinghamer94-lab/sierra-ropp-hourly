Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Robert hands off Option C on 29% of his scoreable calls (2 of 7) and wins repairs on 29% (2 of 7), and his defining strength is holding a position the customer is pushing him off (Strong on wins) — Call 15, asked to just top up a leaking system: by laws we can't put refrigerant knowing that there's a leak. The refrigerant, you put it in and it's going to come out. So there's laws with the EPA, and when the customer proposed waiting for the off-season, the way that it looks, I'm not sure if it can last. His single biggest gap is that most of his calls never reach the Decision step at all (Weak) — fourteen of twenty-one recordings end with him walking out to start the evaluation, including Call 11, where he had already shown the customer a compressor drawing 96 against an 82 startup limit, a motor start device reading 1 and then 0 against a 45+5 rating, and a voltage switch he described as burning on the sides. The pattern is that his front half is scripted and reliable and his back half is invisible: the seven calls that do reach an outcome are carded across the whole range — two closed, two flipped, three no-moves — with Decision banded Strong on his flip and Moderate on his close. The single behavior change that would move the needle most: the Call 15 sequence — name the constraint, refuse the shortcut, then offer the comparison — applied on every call where he has already found something, rather than only on the ones where the customer pushes first.

Strengths

1. Refusing the shortcut and keeping the customer (Strong on wins)

Call 15's customers wanted the cheap answer twice and got a reason both times. On topping up refrigerant: by laws we can't put refrigerant knowing that there's a leak… there's laws with the EPA. On waiting until winter: the way that it looks, I'm not sure if it can last. On the scale of the job they were imagining: you fix the leak and then you pay thousands of dollars for a leak search repair… A leak search is around $2,000. And rather than leaving them with a refusal, he gave them a route: if you decide that you want to go repair, we can take care of that. If you guys choose that you want to go replacement, we can take care of that. The scorecard credits exactly this — that he didn't fold and walked them through the path so they'd set up the next step rather than leave hanging. Coaching value: this is the complete Decision step and it is already in his repertoire; the coaching is entirely about frequency.

2. Reasoning from a number the customer can check (Strong)

Robert does not assert; he derives. Call 15: your freezing point is 32 degrees. This is below freezing. So that means there's not enough refrigerant in the system. Call 11 he walks three separate readings against three separate ratings — this one reads that to be 45 plus 5 and it's reading 1 and then 0 on the other side, so that one is pretty much completely out, then the amps for like on startup are 82… that one was reading at 96, then the voltage switch: you can see where it's burning on the sides and burns the bottom. Call 13 he catches a mismatch before it does damage: this one is 40 plus 5, and this one is a 45 plus 5. So I don't want to do any more harm… it needs what it needs. Coaching value: both scorecards that band Assessment record Strong, and it is the reason his no-moves are decisions rather than confusions.

3. Teaching airflow in language that sticks (Strong)

Call 16, on a clogged one-inch filter: when it's like that, the unit is running a marathon through a straw. They can't breathe. Call 9 he corrects a habit that would have cost the customer a system: I have some of the vents closed… you don't want to do that. It stops the flow… that can cause damage to your system too, because one floor will be trying to push more than the unit will try to overwork itself. He then teaches thermostat staggering across three floors — you don't want them on at the same time trying to cool down both areas… you want one to be cool, and then this one will be satisfied, and then boom, it'll switch. Coaching value: this is genuine, memorable value delivered free, and it is the goodwill his missing review ask is leaving on the table.

4. Absorbing a customer's distrust without argument (Strong)

Call 1's customer opens with a warning about his own colleagues — it seems like every time somebody… you got to buy a whole new unit and all that. If you'd be honest with me, I'd be great… some of your co-workers always try to — and Robert simply takes it. Call 4's customer describes being charged twelve thousand dollars by an installer who then stopped answering; Robert's reply is definitely sorry that, you know, and he moves to the work. Call 18's scorecard credits the same instinct on a botched prior install: he kept the call moving rather than shifting blame to the prior install, and that call closed. Coaching value: he loses nothing to defensiveness, which is what makes the constraint-based refusals on Call 15 land as honesty rather than sales resistance.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Fourteen of twenty-one recordings end before an option exists (Weak)

Every recording from Calls 1 through 14 ends in the same place: I'll go ahead and get started, I'll go get my tools, I'll go ahead and get started on the outdoor unit. Call 11 is the most costly — a full presented diagnosis with three failing components and a competing company booked for the same morning, and the recording stops as he climbs to the attic. Call 13 stops mid-capacitor-check with the customer's own spare part in hand. Impact: his outcome rates rest on seven calls of twenty-one, and the Decision step evidence for two-thirds of his week simply does not exist.

Gap 2 — Waiting to be pushed before offering the comparison (Strong on wins)

On Call 15 the specialist comparison appears only after the customers have twice asked for something cheaper. Nothing in his scripted opening mentions it: the expectations block promises a report, list of options and stops there on Calls 1, 6, 9, 11, 14 and 16. Call 11 is the clearest cost — the customer volunteers we do have a second company coming… we'll compare the two and Robert's answer positions himself out of the comparison rather than into it: I do repairs, so that's even better… if you want to get information about a possible new unit, if that's what you're looking for. Impact: the comparison he runs well on Call 15 is offered reactively, so on calls where the customer does not push, it never happens.

Gap 3 — No price named anywhere before the report (Moderate)

The only figure Robert says out loud in twenty-one recordings is a competitor-scale estimate on Call 15 — a leak search is around $2,000 — and even that is offered as a caution rather than as his price. On Call 14 the customer asks directly, would it be worth it just getting the compressor?, and gets we'll see. Once I do my maintenance, I'll go over everything with you, and then we'll go from there. Call 8's customer is chasing a $400-odd refund from a capacitor that failed within days and gets no pricing framing at all. Impact: customers on Calls 8, 11 and 14 spent the entire visit with no sense of scale, which is precisely the position from which a competing quote wins.

Gap 4 — The decision-maker identified but not brought into the room (Moderate)

Call 16 is carded a no-move and the reason is visible in the transcript: the person who decides is not present and is only ever going to be phoned afterwards — if I do happen to notice the same thing, I'll call Beth… any work that needs to be done won't be done without her approval. Call 5's homeowner is absent (Are they available today? No, they're not); Call 11's customer leaves for a dental appointment mid-visit. Impact: on three of his seven scoreable and near-scoreable calls the option sheet was always going to be presented to someone who was not there, and no attempt is recorded to get them on the phone during the visit.

Gap 5 — The five-star seed planted and never harvested (Weak)

The seed is in his script on six separate recordings — the goal is to deliver a five star experience. If I'm not meeting that, you stop me — and on Call 9 he improves it: if I'm not meeting that, you stop me, so I can give you six. The customer's reply is you're doing great. No recording in twenty-one contains the ask. Impact: he does the hard half on nearly every call and the ten-second half on none, including on calls where the customer has already told him he is doing well.

Gap 6 — Filter and airflow education crowding out discovery (Moderate)

The one-inch-filter lesson appears near-verbatim on Calls 8, 15 and 16, and the vent and staggering lesson consumes a large part of Call 9. It is good material and on Call 16 it occupies the segment where the three critical questions and the goal question should be — that call is carded a no-move. Compare Call 6, where he does ask, as far as the maintenance today, what are you hoping to accomplish? Impact: the education is a strength that is displacing the questions the guide weights most heavily inside the Assessment step.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The single difference between Robert's positive-outcome calls and his no-moves is whether the person who decides was in the room and got a route offered to them: on Call 15 both owners were present and left with a repair path and a replacement path, and on Call 16 the decision-maker was a phone number to be called later.

  • Ready: Solid on both sides. He asks the repair-history question everywhere; on Call 15 the answer (they just added the Freon) becomes the whole argument, and on the no-moves the same answer goes nowhere.
  • Welcome: Solid on both sides and carded Solid twice. The script is identical on the calls that close and the calls that stall, so it does not discriminate.
  • Assessment: Strong on both sides and carded Strong on both the flip and the close. Call 11's three-component walkthrough is as good as anything in his week and produced no captured outcome at all.
  • Decision: Strong on the flip, Moderate on the close, absent on the no-moves. Call 15's we can have somebody come out and speak with you about replacement… that way you can compare and contrast is the only sentence of its kind in twenty-one recordings.
  • Deliver: Solid throughout — clean exits, honest scheduling, and no review ask on wins or no-moves alike.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same technician, same scripted opening, same habit of reading a number against its rating — and outcomes across the whole range. On Call 15 the customers pushed twice for a cheaper answer and Robert held: by laws we can't put refrigerant knowing that there's a leak, then I'm not sure if it can last, then the route out — we can have somebody come out and speak with you about replacement… you can compare and contrast. Carded a flip with Decision Strong. On Call 18 he found an exposed wire and a blown contactor and dealt with it in the moment; carded closed with Decision Moderate. On Call 11 he presented a compressor at 96 against an 82 limit, a dead motor start device and a burning voltage switch to a customer who told him a second company was coming that morning — and answered I do repairs, so that's even better, then went to the attic and the recording ends. On Call 16 the decision-maker was never in the room. The variable is not diagnosis, which is Strong everywhere: it is whether the comparison gets offered before the customer asks for it.

Beyond the Guide

He fixes Sierra's dispatch problems himself, on the customer's time

Call 1 he discovers he is booked for one address and expected at another, and works it out live: I'm checking for our office and see what's going on there, then it is confirmed. I'll be taking care of both… once I finish with this one, I'll call dispatch and then they'll ship me off across the street. The customer had already been told by someone else that the ticket would be swapped. Coaching implication: he absorbs scheduling failures gracefully and it costs him call time; the routing issue belongs with dispatch rather than in his coaching plan.

He is unusually patient with customers who need time

Call 14, with an elderly customer worried about slowing him down: there's no rush up there. I'm in no rush. I'll take my time… you have plenty of time. Don't worry. Call 9 he invites the customer to shadow him: you can come out and be outside with me if you want. Anytime you want to join in, ask questions. Hey, what is that? I'll explain it to you. Coaching implication: a customer standing beside him at the condenser is the ideal moment for a spoken ballpark, and it is currently the moment he says nothing about money.

He walks into a lot of other companies' failures

Call 4: a twelve-thousand-dollar install that leaked into a bedroom from a mount that they didn't seal very well. Call 8: a capacitor replaced by Sierra and then replaced again days later by a home warranty company, with a refund in dispute. Call 18's scorecard: an exposed wire and blown contactor from a prior install. Coaching implication: these are pre-qualified Option C conversations arriving on his board, and none of the ones captured this week reached one.

He corrects part specifications rather than fitting what is handed to him

Call 13, offered the customer's own spare capacitor: you can't just put any one on the system… it needs what it needs… this one is 40 plus 5 and this one is a 45 plus 5. So I don't want to do any more harm. He then adds the diagnostic implication: if this one is not the correct one, that could have been the reason why it went out. Coaching implication: this is exactly the technical authority that justifies a priced option, on a call whose recording ends before one exists.

His filter lesson has become a signature

Running a marathon through a straw (Call 16) is the most memorable line any technician used on a customer this week, and he pairs it with a practical alternative — we also have 3 inch filters. Those you change every six months. Coaching implication: that is a filter-cabinet upgrade conversation being given away as advice; it belongs on the option sheet as a Future item.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Offer the comparison before the customer asks for it. On Call 15 the specialist appears only after two attempts to buy something cheaper; on Call 11 a customer told him another company was coming that morning and he answered I do repairs, so that's even better. The sentence he already owns — we can have somebody come out and speak with you about replacement, and that way you can compare and contrast — should be offered the moment his own evaluation finds a system at end of life, not held in reserve.
  • Get the decision-maker on the phone during the visit, not after it. Call 16 is carded a no-move with the approver reachable only as I'll call Beth on the way out. Calls 5 and 11 have the same shape. Asking to call the owner while he is standing at the unit with photos changes the option sheet from a message into a conversation.
  • Say a number in the home. Twenty-one recordings contain no Sierra price at all, and on Call 14 a direct question — would it be worth it just getting the compressor? — got we'll see. A spoken range while the readings are still on screen removes the gap into which a competing quote fits.

Trimming the filter and airflow lesson is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — the teaching is genuinely good and appears on his wins as well as his no-moves, whereas fourteen recordings ending before an option exists is the whole story of his week.

  • Commit to one rule for the week: "The moment my own readings say a system is at the end of its life, I offer the comparison and I ask to get the decision-maker on the phone before I leave the unit."
