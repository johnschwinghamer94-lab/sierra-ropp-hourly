Strengths

1. Teaching the customer to read their own failed part (Strong)

Nathan does not announce a diagnosis; he builds it in front of the customer with the part in his hand. On Call 7 he walks the capacitor rating out loud — on this battery it says 40, it says 40 plus 5. So on one side of this battery We should be reading 40, and on the other side of that battery, we should be reading 5… It's a 38 — then spins the condenser fan and narrates why the stop matters: these motors come like factory sealed, so once they start to lose oil… They start to seize up. On Call 4 he does the same with air filtration, ranking the tiers and being explicit about what he would and would not stand behind: there's one that's literally right below what we call medical grade… I stand by that filter. Coaching value: this is the hardest half of earning the right to present and he already owns it — protect it, because it means his Decision gap is a structure problem, not a credibility problem.

2. Getting invited in rather than letting himself in (Strong)

Every recording in the window contains an explicit permission ask, and it is not a formality — customers respond by handing him the house. Call 3: I was just wondering if it was okay for me to park right there for you… I didn't want to block anyone in or anything like that. Call 4, when the customer asks him to move the truck for the garbage collection, he does it without friction: No problem at all. I'm going to move that real quick and I'll be right back for you. Call 6 the customer comes back with ice water unprompted — I'll get you a glass of ice water — and Call 5's customer had the ladder up, the attic open and the gate unlocked before he arrived. Coaching value: the door is never his problem, so coaching time should be spent entirely downstream of it.

3. Naming the approval rule before the customer has to ask for it (Strong)

On the calls where he sets expectations he does the one box that matters most to a wary customer. Call 3: Just so you know, we're not going to do anything without you guys approval today. So if there's anything that you guys want done, I'm not just going to put it in there and be like, here's a bill. Call 7 he frames the whole visit: just to give you a quick rundown of the evaluation for you. So I'm gonna find out what's going on and see why you're not cooling, and then I'll come to you… And then we'll sit down together and, you know, go over every option for you. Coaching value: he has already promised the customer an options conversation out loud — the coaching ask is simply to keep that promise in the Decision step, which is a much smaller change than building the habit from nothing.

4. Absorbing distrust instead of defending against it (Strong)

Call 4's customer opens with a direct accusation about Sierra's own history — Do you remember the change in leadership where everyone was feeling pressured to upsell? — and Nathan does not argue: I completely understand that. She escalates to like, treat me like your great grandmother. Not one ounce of BS, and instead of retreating he answers with a real caveat against his own interest, telling her the filter cabinet is already installed so she should not pay for that work: you already actually have that cabinet up there for that filter that we're speaking of. So that would mean that we wouldn't need to install that, which would be. It's pricey to take apart that and install it, so… I'm going to get the pricing of just the filter, not the entire install for you. Coaching value: he has the temperament for the hardest customers Sierra sends him; the missing piece is what he does with the trust once he has it.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — Pricing a dying system part-by-part instead of building Option C (Weak)

Call 4 is the clearest example in the window: Nathan diagnoses a compressor at end of life, prices it at 6530, and then, unprompted, tells the customer the coil and heat exchanger are also going — it's going to be, you know, once those components fail, it's around 7,500 for them — on a system he has already described as aging. That is roughly fourteen thousand dollars of failure narrated on one visit with no System Update option ever named and no Comfort Advisor mentioned, and the customer is left to draw her own conclusion: Again, it's preventative. Thinking ahead, but it's not today. Impact: this is the single call in his week with the clearest Option C setup and it ended as a no-move because the upgrade conversation was never offered.

Gap 2 — Letting the customer ask the replacement question first (Weak)

The only Comfort Advisor in Nathan's entire week arrives on Call 6 because the customer had already requested the comparison before he got there: I understand you wanted to compare the price of what repairs would be versus replacement… And then we'll have someone come and give you the quote so you know the pricing of what it'd be to repair versus replace. He executes that cleanly. But on Call 4, where the diagnosis was far worse, the closest the customer gets to the topic is asking about a single part — then what's involved with getting that replaced and warrantied? — and Nathan answers it as a warranty question about the compressor, not as an opening. Impact: his one Option C tee-up this week was customer-initiated, which means his handoff rate is currently a function of who dispatch sends him rather than anything he does.

Gap 3 — No option sheet, no Spin It, no Zip It (Weak)

Neither scoreable call contains an A/B/C sheet handed over, a confident price statement followed by silence, or the closing question the guide asks for. On Call 4 the prices arrive one at a time inside an ongoing conversation — Did you just want the price for the compressor, not the… other? — and he keeps talking through the number rather than handing over a sheet and going quiet. Call 7's scorecard records the same thing from the other side: critical actions Options: Fail and Objections: Fail. Impact: without the sheet there is no moment where the customer is asked to choose, so both of his scoreable calls end with the customer deciding not to decide.

Gap 4 — Answering "should I wait?" with a fact instead of a reframe (Moderate)

Call 4 ends on exactly the question the guide's objection-handling is built for — if I wait until it fails, the compressor fails? Is there any difference in the price? — and Nathan's answer is Honestly, no. It is truthful and it is also the end of the conversation, because a customer who hears that waiting costs nothing has just been given permission to wait. Earlier on the same call he had all the material for a better answer already on the table (the coil, the heat exchanger, the refrigerant replacement he himself flagged: whenever we have to open up the system, we have to replace the refrigerant) and did not connect it. Impact: his most honest moment on his biggest call is also the moment that closed it, because the honesty was not attached to a consequence or to an option.

Gap 5 — Ballpark pricing arrives at the end, not up front (Moderate)

Nothing resembling Price Transparency appears early in either scoreable call. On Call 4 the customer has to prompt for it twice — Did you get a price on the compressor while I'm writing my report? and later Let's talk price — and Nathan's response is to go away and build numbers: It's going to take me around 10 to 15 minutes to write that report. On Call 7 no number of any kind appears before the recording ends. Impact: on a 101-minute call the customer sat through the entire diagnosis with no sense of scale, so the $6,530 landed cold and the only conversation left was whether to wait.

Gap 6 — Rapport that runs past its usefulness (Moderate)

His personal connection is genuine but on Call 2 it consumes the recording: several hundred continuous words on day-trading psychology — it's really getting comfortable with being uncomfortable… that seat time, just sitting there and doing it — before he finally says I'm going to get started out here, then I'll come in and touch base with you. On Call 4 the same warmth is well-timed and productive; on Call 2 it is where the Welcome went instead of the Agenda Card. Impact: the rapport is an asset that occasionally spends the time the Agenda Card and the three critical questions needed.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

Nathan has no positive-outcome call in this window, so the honest contrast is not wins versus no-moves — it is the gap between how well he executes the first three SILO steps and how little of that carries into the fourth: on Call 7 he gets the customer to understand a seized fan motor and a weak capacitor and then never converts that understanding into a single option on paper.

  • Ready: Solid on both scoreable calls, no observable difference. History surfaces conversationally rather than from a Pregame — Call 7, You said that was around four years ago he did that? — which is enough for a demand call but leaves prior declined recommendations undiscovered.
  • Welcome: Strong on every recording, wins and no-moves alike. This step does not discriminate for Nathan at all — Call 3's we're not going to do anything without you guys approval today is as good as anything in his week, and Call 3 still produced no decision.
  • Assessment: Strong on both scoreable calls — this is the step that is not the problem. Call 7's this fan wasn't spinning at all demonstration is the high-water mark of his week and it still ended in a no-move, which is exactly why coaching must move downstream.
  • Decision: Weak on both. The single sentence that separates his two no-moves from a different ending is the one he never says — on Call 4 the customer has to supply the structure herself with it's preventative. Thinking ahead, but it's not today.
  • Deliver: Not observable on either scoreable call. Nothing in the window reaches a review ask or a membership revisit, so this step is currently invisible rather than weak.
Decisive Gap — the Decision

Decisive gap — the Decision. Same tech, same warmth, same quality of diagnosis — but the calls stop in two different places. On Call 7 he earns it completely (Whenever we spin a fan motor, it should continue to spin, not come to an abrupt stop) and the scorecard still records Options: Fail and Objections: Fail; on Call 4 he earns it even harder against a hostile opening (Not one ounce of BS) and then converts fourteen thousand dollars of diagnosed failure into a single part price, 6530, with no Option C. The one recording where an upgrade conversation exists at all is Call 6, and there it exists because the customer asked for it first: we'll have someone come and give you the quote so you know the pricing of what it'd be to repair versus replace. The variable is not whether the customer trusts him or understands the diagnosis — they do, on every call — it is whether Option C ever gets built and handed over.

Beyond the Guide

He volunteers future failures without pricing the alternative

On Call 4 Nathan tells the customer about the coil and the heat exchanger even though, as he says himself, they're not going to be on your invoice — he is being transparent about a system's whole condition and getting nothing for it. That instinct is the raw material of an Option C conversation being used as a disclaimer instead. Coaching implication: teach him that the sentence after "these are down the line" is "so let me have a Comfort Advisor price the whole system next to these repairs," and his transparency starts producing handoffs.

Customers hand him the house before he asks

Call 5's customer has staged the entire job in advance — I got the ladder up there. It's open. All you got to take your tools. I got the filter up there, attic, and the side gate is open. There's a hose there, so you don't have to work a lot — and Call 6's brings water twice. Nathan generates unusual goodwill for a first-visit tech. Coaching implication: that goodwill is currently spent on access and comfort rather than on an ask; it is the cheapest 5-star review capital on the team and no recording in the window shows him harvesting it.

The recordings themselves are cutting his week in half

Five of his seven recordings end before any option is presented — Call 6 is logged at 53 minutes but contains 669 words, and Call 2 at 15 minutes captures the Welcome and nothing else. His actual Decision-step behavior is being judged on two calls. Coaching implication: before the next review cycle, confirm his recording setup, because the current evidence base makes his Decision band look thinner than it may be and there is no way to distinguish "did not do it" from "not captured."

He answers price questions by leaving the room

Twice on Call 4 the customer asks for a number and Nathan's reply is a departure — while I'm writing up my report, I'll be able to get the pricing for this exact system for you, then It's going to take me around 10 to 15 minutes to write that report. The customer fills the gap with small talk about his kids, and the momentum from the demonstration is gone by the time he returns. Coaching implication: a ballpark said out loud at the unit — before he leaves to write anything — would keep the pricing conversation attached to the evidence that justifies it.

His technical explanations are better than his commercial ones

The capacitor walkthrough on Call 7 and the filter-tier explanation on Call 4 (there's one that's literally right below what we call medical grade) are genuinely well-constructed teaching. The same man says Honestly, no to a wait-versus-replace question. Coaching implication: he needs scripted language for the commercial half of the call at the same quality he has already built for the technical half — he will use it, because he clearly rehearses.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Turn a multi-component diagnosis into a Comfort Advisor call, on the spot. Right now Nathan narrates the whole failure and prices one piece of it — Call 4, the price for that compressor is 6530 followed by around 7,500 for the coil and heat exchanger. The rule is mechanical: the moment his own diagnosis names two major components, he stops quoting parts, says the system-update option out loud, and calls the office for a Comfort Advisor before the customer speaks. On Call 4 that one sentence was the difference between a no-move and a turnover lead.
  • Build the sheet and then go quiet. He currently talks prices inside conversation — Did you just want the price for the compressor, not the… other? (Call 4) — and Call 7's scorecard marks Options as a fail. He should hand over a written A/B/C sheet, state the prices once, ask which option works best today, and stop talking. His Assessment already earns the right to do this; nothing else needs to change first.
  • Give a ballpark at the unit, before writing anything. Twice on Call 4 the customer had to ask for pricing and twice Nathan left to prepare it (It's going to take me around 10 to 15 minutes to write that report). A range said out loud while the seized motor is still in his hand keeps the number attached to the evidence and removes the sticker-shock moment that ended that call.

His door-step and permission routine is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — parking, permission and the approval promise land on literally every recording in his week, whereas the Decision step is the whole game.
