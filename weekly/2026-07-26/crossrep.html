Bottom Line

Bottom Line

Across 13 technicians and 70 scoreable calls, Option C Handoff rates run from 0% (Nathan Colquitt) to 100% (Noah Weng), and Repair-Won rates from 0% — seven of thirteen techs — to 75% (Joe Mendoza). The behaviour that separates the top third from the bottom third is not the one anyone expects: it is Deliver. The top four techs by Repair-Won rate band Deliver Solid or better — Joe Mendoza escalates and finishes on the same visit, I'm gonna get with the managers right now to get that ordered for you guys so we can see if we can get someone out here today (Mendoza, Call 23) — while the bottom four band it Weak or absent, ending calls with a warm goodbye and nothing agreed: Good luck on your trip. Thank you, brother (Weng, Call 7). The biggest company-wide gap is Decision, banded "Strong on wins" for 10 of 13 techs — the skill exists on the calls that convert and vanishes on the ones that do not, typified by not writing you an estimate for that, but in the future it will (April, Call 18). The single intervention that would move the team's positive-outcome rate most: make the close mechanical — every technician who diagnoses something writes it on a sheet, states a number in the home, and either books the work or books the Comfort Advisor before leaving the property. Nothing in the front of these calls needs fixing.

4 of 13 reports required data-integrity handling — see Appendix.

The Decisive Differentiator

The decisive differentiator: Deliver. It is the only step where the two tiers sit in clearly different band groups, and the difference is whether anything is actually completed before the technician leaves. Joe Mendoza (75% Repair-Won) escalates and finishes on the visit — I'm gonna get with the managers right now to get that ordered for you guys so we can see if we can get someone out here today to take care of that (Mendoza, Call 23) — and Robert Silinzy (29%) closes out cleanly with his own scheduling: once I finish with this one, I'll call dispatch, and then they'll ship me off across the street (Silinzy, Call 1). Company-Wide Pattern Analysis

Training gaps affect most or all techs — fix with group training. Coaching gaps are highly variable — fix with 1:1 sessions. "Strong on wins" patterns are the highest-yield coaching targets — the skill exists, it just isn't applied every time.

Training Gaps

None by the threshold. No SILO step is banded Moderate or Weak by two-thirds or more of the team; the worst-performing step, Deliver, sits at Moderate or Weak for 4 of 12 banded techs. Only 6 of 64 banded cells across the whole dataset fall at Moderate or Weak. This team does not have a knowledge problem.

There is, however, a company-wide behaviour gap that does not show up as a band because it is an absence rather than a weakness: Price Transparency. Across 223 recordings, a spoken ballpark in the customer's home appears in a handful of calls and is deferred to "the van" in the overwhelming majority — I'll get you the prices on that, um, if you just allow me to finish up my evaluation (Trujillo, Call 6), price wise I'm not 100% sure. I'd have to look at the price book (Castro, Call 8), we'll see. Once I do my maintenance, I'll go over everything with you (Silinzy, Call 14). This is the one item on this list that genuinely warrants group training.

Positive-Outcome Predictor Test

Which banded behaviors reliably predict a positive outcome? (The per-tech reports carry no numeric scores, so prediction is tested on bands, not score thresholds.)

Welcome (Critical Action #1, Setting Expectations). Top third bands it Strong (Mendoza, Trujillo), Solid (Silinzy) and Strong on wins (Valencia). Bottom third bands it Strong — all four of them. Techs banding Welcome Strong include both the highest Repair-Won rate on the team (Mendoza, 75%) and four of the seven techs at 0%. Verdict: Not predictive. No band threshold travels with the higher rates; this step is saturated across the team.

Decision (options + objections + the Comfort Advisor handoff). Top third: three Strong on wins and one Strong. Bottom third: three Strong on wins and one Weak. The two techs banding Decision Strong sit at 33% and 18%; the ten banding it Strong on wins range from 75% down to 0%. Verdict: Not predictive on band alone — because almost the whole team occupies the same band. What Decision predicts is not whether a tech converts but how often: the band is identical, and the frequency of applying it is where the rates separate. That is a coaching finding, not a threshold.

Verdict: Predictive — the threshold is Solid or better.

The Deliver Pattern

The Deliver Pattern: Techs who band Deliver as Solid or better win repairs at 75%, 33%, 29% and 18% (Mendoza, Trujillo, Silinzy, Wyllie); techs who band it Moderate or Weak win repairs at 29%, 0%, 0% and 0% (Valencia, April, Weng, Tlatenchi). This is the single most actionable banded finding in the dataset, and it is quote-backed: I'm gonna get with the managers right now to get that ordered for you guys so we can see if we can get someone out here today to take care of that (Mendoza, Call 23) versus for this time, I was able to get approval for waiving your service fee, since it was really just kind of a really quick in and out (Tlatenchi, Call 5).

Stability caveat: this pattern rests on thirteen techs and one crossing case — Francisco Valencia wins repairs at 29% with Deliver banded Moderate. It is a clear direction, not a law, and three of the four techs at 0% with weak Deliver also have the smallest scoreable samples on the roster (2 to 5 calls each). Re-test it next week before building policy on it.

Trujillo and Valencia use them as evidence and both sit in the top third; April reads prior notes aloud but does not convert them into an option; Tlatenchi's customer had to point out that the file existed. Team coaching implication: a guide update making "prior declined recommendations" an explicit Ready-step checkbox, since the two techs who use them are also two of the four highest Repair-Won rates.

Customers are handing technicians the upgrade conversation and it is being declined

Team coaching implication: a targeted coaching conversation with a single mechanical trigger — the customer says the word "replace," the technician says the word "specialist," on the same call.

The #1 Differentiator

Joe Mendoza (75% Repair-Won) gets the part ordered while he is still on site — I'm gonna get with the managers right now to get that ordered for you guys so we can see if we can get someone out here today (Mendoza, Call 23) — while Juan Tlatenchi (0% Repair-Won) exits an evaluation he never performed by removing Sierra's last claim on the job: I was able to get approval for waiving your service fee (Tlatenchi, Call 5). Company-Wide Training Priorities

(fix these in group settings — affects most techs)

  1. Price Transparency — say a number in the home. No SILO step bands Moderate or Weak across two-thirds of the team, but the spoken ballpark is missing from the overwhelming majority of 223 recordings; Mario Castro says no price at all in fourteen recordings and answered a direct question with price wise I'm not 100% sure. Band method: Steps were compared using the five-band Strength Band Key (Strong, Strong on wins, Solid, Moderate, Weak), never numeric scores. Band ordering for ranking and best/worst language, best to worst: Strong, Solid, Strong on wins, Moderate, Weak. "Strong on wins" is treated as the priority coaching band because it marks a skill the tech has but does not apply every time. Verdicts (training gap / coaching gap / team strength) were assigned from band distribution as defined in Sections 5 and 7.

Limitations: This analysis is based on the banded, quote-backed assessments in each tech's per-tech report. Outcome rates reflect in-call decisions only; downstream Comfort Advisor conversion of Option C handoffs is not captured here. Bands are qualitative judgments from each tech's transcripts, not a numeric scale, so cross-tech comparisons describe direction and pattern, not magnitude. Most importantly, several techs' rates rest on very small denominators — Mario Castro on 3 calls, Cole Pantol and Joe Mendoza on 4, Noah Weng, Nathan Colquitt and Juan Tlatenchi on 2 each — so a single call moves those percentages a long way. The scorecard should be read as a map of behaviour, not a league table of performance.
