Strengths

1. Offering the Comfort Advisor as a free comparison, not a sales visit (Strong)

Andrew never asks the customer to consider replacement — he offers them a way to check his own work for nothing. Call 4: I've given you the option of talking to someone for free and seeing that way you can compare, see if it makes more sense to update the system, and the answer is immediate: Cool. Let's. Let's do it. Call 6 frames it as protection against wasted spend: before you guys start putting money in, you know, and they give you this last option so you guys can compare and contrast the repair price and replacement, see what makes sense for the two of you. Call 1 removes the last friction by making it immediate: they can be here within the hour, and Call 2 ends with the visit signed for and free: everything is 79 whatever. 79 and then free for them to come on out. Coaching value: this framing is why he leads the team on handoffs, and it is teachable language that should be lifted straight into the rest of the roster's scripting.

2. Drawing the system before naming the fault (Strong)

On Call 2 he stops mid-diagnosis and builds the customer a map: I'm going to quickly just do a diagram just so we can map out everything that we got going on. So we got your outdoor system, the compressor… those copper lines that extend off of your outside units are going to go directly into your… and only then presents rust, pressure imbalance and metal fatigue against it — This is letting us know that the metal of this fur unit here is aging out… This presents a safety risk because once these pipes crack through, we can bring carbon. The customer's reaction is the proof: Don't ask me to repeat or take the quiz. Coaching value: a customer who can draw their own system does not need to be sold an upgrade — they arrive at it — and this is the mechanism behind his handoff rate.

3. Telling the truth when the truth costs Sierra the job (Strong)

Call 1, on warranty mechanics, he volunteers the rule that works against him: if the compressor's failed, then we can honor the warranty pricing. But if it's not failed and we're looking to get it replaced proactively… we have to charge for out of warranty pricing. Call 12, in a home where another company did the install, he raises it himself: just to be super blunt, you know, why don't you have your other guys… I just want to make sure you guys are protected because, since they installed the system, in terms of warranties, what they're going to be able to cover for you. Call 6 he declines to fit the customer's own part: unfortunately we won't be able to install that part, just given that if any damage is done to the system after we do that install, we will be held liable. Coaching value: Call 11's scorecard traces his close directly to this — a customer burned by a locksmith bought because he said That's super valid and committed to approval before any repair.

4. Going back for the decision-maker instead of writing the lead off (Strong)

Call 6's customer could not decide without her husband, who was at work all day. Rather than leave an estimate in an inbox, Andrew committed to a specific follow-up — I'll follow up with you tonight… I'll do my best to see what I can do to get myself scheduled in so that way I can come on by — and Call 9 is him doing it: a full return visit the next morning, arriving ahead of the specialist to brief the husband himself. I just wanted to take a few moments just before AJ arrived. He's coming from up north. We just want to touch base, see how everything is. He then walks the husband through the $6,700 of repairs, the R22-to-410A phase-out and the limits of home warranties before handing over. Coaching value: that is the handoff executed to the letter of the guide, on his own time, and it is the single best piece of follow-through in the window.

Consistent Gaps

(patterns across many calls)

Gap 1 — No number until the report is written (Moderate)

Andrew's default answer to a price question is to defer it. Call 6, on a part he has already identified and has in the van: And how much is that? — I'll get you the prices on that, um, if you just allow me to finish up my evaluation. Later on the same call, asked directly about replacement: It's hard for me to say. That's a separate department than what I do. Call 2's if it's to replace them, what's that price point? gets a two-minute explanation of single-stage versus multi-stage compressors and no range at all. Contrast Call 9, where he is completely fluent with numbers once they are written down — he had listed that as a $224 repair on that one, we have the $400 quote for the actual motor itself. Impact: customers on Calls 2, 6 and 7 spent between one and three hours with him having no idea of scale, and Call 7's customer had already told him her ceiling: I just don't want that two thousand dollar bill I can't handle that.

Gap 2 — Finding out who decides after the diagnosis instead of before it (Moderate)

He asks the decision-maker question well when he asks it — Call 10: Is there anyone else other than you that will want to have that information in terms of what's going on? — but on Call 6 it surfaces only once the options are on the table, in the customer's words rather than his: my husband usually buys these, then I'll have him call tomorrow when he's off, then she's gonna try and get a hold of the husband, see if he can talk over the phone. The result is Call 9, a second visit on the same job. Impact: a 141-minute call needed a 70-minute follow-up because a question he already knows how to ask was asked ninety minutes too late.

Gap 3 — Objection handling that stalls when the customer's stated goal is "just be truthful" (Strong on wins)

Call 10 is his only no-move and its scorecard is precise about why: Decision Moderate, objections critical action Fail. The customer had been burned before — One person wanted to change our units for no reason — and when Andrew asked what she wanted from the visit, the answer was No. Just be truthful, I guess. His response accepted the frame rather than working it: just sounds like you just at least want to have reliability throughout to make sure. Peace of mind. Compare Call 11, where the same distrust (We just had a locksmith come back and not do what they said they were doing… I'm kind of panicking now) got That's super valid plus a concrete approval commitment, and closed. Impact: the one customer this week who told him what she was afraid of got sympathy instead of a structure, and it is his only call without an outcome.

Gap 4 — The five-star seed is never planted or harvested (Weak)

Andrew's expectations block is the most complete on the team and it is the only one on the roster with no five-star line in it — Call 3's full version runs from equipment tour to written report to options with no mention of the experience standard, and no recording in the window contains a review ask. Cold water?) and one tells him You're always welcome to ask for me from the office is exactly what she wants. Impact: the technician generating the most goodwill and the most positive outcomes on the roster is converting none of it into reviews.

Gap 5 — Calls that run to three hours (Moderate)

The depth is real and it produces outcomes, but Call 2's recording contains long stretches where the customer's television is the loudest thing in the room, and Call 6's estimate build alone is about 15-20 minutes to just write everything up on top of everything else. Impact: at this length the board cannot absorb many of his calls in a day, so his conversion rate is doing more work than his volume — and Calls 3, 5 and 7 all ended before the Decision step, which on a shorter cadence they might not have.

Gap 6 — Personal audio landing on customer recordings (Moderate)

Impact: this is a professionalism exposure on a technician whose customer-facing conduct is otherwise the best-evidenced on the roster, and it should be closed before it lands somewhere it matters.

What Separates Positive Outcomes From No-Moves

The one difference between Andrew's eight positive calls and his single no-move is whether he answered the customer's fear with a commitment or with agreement: on Call 11 That's super valid was followed by a specific promise and the call closed; on Call 10 just be truthful was met with a restatement of her own words and nothing was ever put on the table.

  • Ready: Strong on both sides — he had read the file on Call 10 as thoroughly as on Call 1. This step does not discriminate for him at all.
  • Welcome: Strong on the wins, Solid on the no-move per its scorecard. The consent check Does that sound fair? appears on Calls 1 and 7; on Call 10 the equivalent moment passes without one.
  • Assessment: Strong on both — Call 10's scorecard bands it Strong, which is precisely the point: the diagnosis was never the problem on the call that failed.
  • Decision: Strong versus Moderate. On his wins there is always a named next step with a person attached — they can be here within the hour (Call 1), free for them to come on out… Tomorrow (Call 2); on Call 10 there is no option, no specialist and no date.
  • Deliver: Solid across the board, with the same absence everywhere — no review ask on any call, win or no-move.

On Call 4 the entire captured exchange is the offer and the answer — talking to someone for free… see if it makes more sense to update the system / Cool. Let's. Let's do it. The variable is not diagnosis or trust: it is whether a specific next step with a person and a date attached ever gets offered.

Beyond the Guide

He uses the refrigerant phase-out as the structural case for Option C

On Call 9 he explains it to the husband as a fact about the equipment rather than a sales point: your current systems are essentially outdated because the refrigerant you're running on has been phased out, then walks the history — yours is running on 410A. This was a replacement for the R22 that we were running on since you were my age… around Covid time. Call 4 lands the same argument as value: it's clear that it's running on less efficiency than it was back when it was first manufactured. That means that you're paying more than you should to keep this thing running. Coaching implication: this is an Option C argument that survives any price objection because it is not about price; it belongs in every SILO tech's toolkit.

He preempts home warranties rather than losing to them

Call 9's customer asks How do you feel about home warranties? What do they cover? and Andrew gives a genuinely useful answer about response times, claim terms and refrigerant exclusions — they call them on a Friday or Saturday, they'll say, hey, we'll see you on Monday. And at that point you're like, what do I do for the next couple days? — and the customer volunteers her own thousand-dollar uncovered leak in return. Coaching implication: Calls 3 and 11 in other techs' weeks were lost to warranty companies; Andrew has the counter-argument already built and nobody else is using it.

His follow-through creates second appointments nobody scheduled

Call 6 ends with I'll follow up with you tonight and Call 9 is him back the next morning ahead of the specialist. On Call 11 he leaves his card with You're always welcome to ask for me from the office. They'll flag me down. Coaching implication: he is building a personal request book, which is worth tracking — and it is also unpaid time that ought to be visible to dispatch rather than absorbed by him.

He explains equipment tiers without steering

Call 1: You've got the premium systems that are the very high end. You got the mid range systems and you got the economy systems like what contractors are putting in homes today. That's what you have now… there you get 8 to 12 years out of them. He gives the customer a way to place their own equipment before any specialist arrives. Coaching implication: this primes the Comfort Advisor conversation better than any handoff script — the customer already knows which tier they are in and why it matters.

He asks a better goal question than the guide requires

Call 12: My last question before I get started… beyond the water damage we got going on with those concerns, is there anything expectation wise you have with me, anything you're hoping to gain on my visit for today? Call 3 asks the consent version: Is that information you'd like me to keep to myself, or would you like me to share that with you? Coaching implication: he gets explicit permission to raise concerns before he finds any, which removes the "he's just upselling" objection before it can form — and on Call 10, the one call where the customer's answer was a warning rather than a wish, he did not act on what he was told.

Top 3 Coaching Priorities

(ranked by impact)

  • Give a range at the equipment, before writing anything. Andrew currently answers And how much is that? with if you just allow me to finish up my evaluation (Call 6). He knows his prices cold once they are on paper — he had listed that as a $224 repair (Call 9) — so a spoken range while the customer is standing at the condenser costs him nothing and removes the hour of uncertainty that Call 7's customer spent worrying about that two thousand dollar bill.
  • Establish who signs before the ladder comes out. He asks this well on Call 10 (Is there anyone else other than you that will want to have that information?) and on Call 6 it emerged ninety minutes in, which cost him the whole of Call 9 as a return trip. Making it the last line of his expectations block — which is otherwise the most complete on the team — turns two visits back into one.
  • Answer distrust with a commitment, not a restatement. On Call 11 That's super valid plus a specific approval promise closed a panicking customer; on Call 10 Just be truthful, I guess got just sounds like you just at least want to have reliability and produced his only no-move, carded objections: Fail. The move is the one he already owns — name the fear as legitimate, then attach a concrete thing he will do about it.

Adding the five-star line to his expectations block is worth a quick consistency reminder, but it is a distant fourth — he is already converting eight of nine calls, whereas no review ask means Sierra is getting none of the reputation those outcomes have earned.
