## Top Gaps & Coaching

### Gap 1: Price Objection Not Reframed (Decision step)
When the customer said "That's a lot" to the $2,795 estimate, Ben acknowledged the sticker shock but didn't reconnect to the underlying concern or ask a clarifying question.

**What he could have said:** "I hear you—it's a bigger number than expected. What matters more to you right now: getting the downstairs cooled back up immediately, or taking a step back to see if a full replacement makes more sense financially?"

This would have either (a) earned the repair commitment if immediate cooling was the priority, or (b) positioned the specialist call as the customer's own insight, not a deflection.

### Gap 2: No Competing Options Developed
Repair and replacement were both mentioned, but only repair was priced and detailed. When customer asked directly about replacement cost, Ben deferred entirely instead of saying: "Installation runs $8K–$15K typically depending on the system, but our specialist can give you exact numbers. The tradeoff is the $2,795 gets you cool today, but you'll still be on an 18-year-old unit."

This would have given the customer a real choice framework, not a mystery option.

## Coaching Summary

**To earn more first-call closes on high-dollar estimates:**
1. When price objection lands, ask a clarifying question before restating the value ("What's your priority—immediate fix or long-term solution?")
2. Develop both options (repair vs. replace) with at least rough pricing guidance, so the customer feels they have agency
3. Position the specialist call as *the customer's* smart next step, not your deflection ("You're right to want those numbers side-by-side—our specialist can show you the full replacement picture so you and your wife can make the smartest call.")

**To protect the interaction:**
- Keep vendor calls (to manager, colleagues) off the customer's time; step outside or save for after you leave.
