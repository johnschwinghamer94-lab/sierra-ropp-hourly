## Bands & Evidence

### Welcome: **Strong**
Benjamin opened with empathy and clear expectations. He acknowledged they're a long-standing member ("I know we've been out here many, many times, which I appreciate, you being a member") and then reset expectations despite the repetition: "it's always good to just go over the reminder." 

He walked through the full process: tour of the home, thorough evaluation (cleanliness, function, safety), pictures/videos, report writing, and discussion of any work. Set time constraints: "I'm on no time restraints today, right?" and "Do you need me out of here at any time?"

**Quote:** "I'll write a full report about everything that I saw... we'll discuss that. If there's any work that you want to get done, we can absolutely get that ball rolling today. I won't do anything unless you approve of it."

### Assessment: **Solid**
Benjamin asked the key discovery question early: "What is your main goal from the maintenance today?" and listened to the customer identify filter changes as the immediate need. He also asked permission to explain maintenance timing: "I think this is the first time we've come out here for summer maintenance in about three or four years" — surfacing a gap the customer hadn't expected.

The technical assessment (testing compressor, checking pressures, inspecting coils) was thorough but happened largely alongside a trainee/ride-along, so customer engagement during diagnosis was limited compared to a solo call. He did circle back: "Can you do me a favor? Can you check thermostats and turn them down again, see if they're running?" to involve the customer.

**Gap:** More check-in questions during the inspection (e.g., "Any recent performance changes?" or "Have you noticed any sounds?") would have strengthened the assessment.

### Decision: **Strong**
Benjamin presented the findings in a structured, customer-friendly way. He drew a diagram of the full system to explain how each component works, then identified the specific concerns:
- Compressor pulling 133 amp (over the 120 limit) — "like our hearts work too hard for too long, it's gonna give out"
- Low refrigerant pressure (should be 170s–180s) due to a leak in the coil
- Rust on the evap coil (3+ years without summer maintenance)
- R410A refrigerant now banned — replacement coils no longer available

He presented options clearly:
1. **Leak search and repair:** $2,777
2. **Compressor replacement (if that fails):** $5,733 + $7,500 evap coil = $12,000+
3. **Full system replacement:** pricing TBD via install specialist visit

When the customer hesitated ("That's a lot of money" / "We may be selling next year"), Benjamin didn't back down on urgency but pivoted smoothly to Option C: "Which is why I did give you the option to speak to one of our install specialists. Just about what that price looks like. So you can compare and contrast."

He reconnected repeatedly: "What are your questions about that?" and addressed objections with empathy: "I know you said you want to think about it, so it's..." acknowledging her need to decide without pressure.

**Quote:** "So with that leak going on, it's basically... there's no oil or no blood going to our heart or oil going to the engine, so causes a lot further damage."

### Deliver: **Solid**
Benjamin's technical explanation was thorough and well-paced. He used the system diagram, explained each component's role (compressor = heart, condenser fan = cooling the heart, evap coil = refrigerant flow, blower motor = air circulation), and connected findings to real consequences ("You are actually overpaying by about 15% on your utility bills every single month").

He did NOT explicitly ask for a 5-star review at the end. The call closed with a focus on the next steps (install specialist consultation) and friendly personal chat (baby on the way, moving back to California, rescue dog story). 

**Gap:** A simple ask—"If we get to work with you on this, would you be open to leaving us a review?"—would close the delivery loop.
