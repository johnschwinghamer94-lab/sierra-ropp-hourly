## Coaching Tip

**FIX —** When the customer said "$3,000 does seem, from a common sense perspective, a little off," that was the moment to ask "What's your biggest concern here?" instead of launching into equipment cost justifications—this reconnects emotionally to their doubt and opens the door to repositioning value rather than defending pricing with facts.

## Supporting Details

**What went well:**
- Diagnostic excellence: Joe's use of gauges and pressure readings to identify non-condensables was textbook assessment
- Technical depth: Explained installation/installation practices and why the original contractor should warranty this
- Empathy early: "It's got to be frustrating to be in this heat without any air conditioner actually working"

**The gap:**
- **Objection handling:** Customer's price objection ("$3,000 is crazy... I can get a floor unit for $500... friend says $1,000 possible") was a soft handoff moment. Instead of reconnecting to the underlying fear ("this seems expensive/implausible"), Joe shifted to lecturing about BTU requirements and equipment costs. This defensive posture cost the moment. A reconnecting question like "Walk me through why the $1,000 option appeals to you—is it mostly the price, or are you questioning whether the repair is worth it?" would have kept the customer engaged and potentially uncovered whether they wanted a maintenance membership or a second opinion call-back with the installer.

**Why it matters for the next call:**
- When a customer's objection touches on price or feasibility, resist the urge to "prove them wrong" with facts. Ask first. You'll either surface a real concern you can address, or confirm they're just shopping—and you'll do it while keeping the relationship warm instead of adversarial.
