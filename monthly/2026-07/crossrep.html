Final report for the month

This is the FINAL July 2026 rollup. It replaces any month-to-date version previously published at this path. Bands are merged from the four weekly rollups inside the month; close rates and flip counts come from ServiceTitan and override the weekly reports’ own tallies in both directions.

Bottom Line

Sierra’s SILO team closed July at a 61% team close rate, with 428 Option C flips created off 706 calls run. The month’s shape is consistent across almost every page: the front of the call is in good health and the back of it is not. Assessment banded Strong for thirteen of fourteen technicians and Welcome for eleven, and Welcome was the single most improved step on the roster — four technicians moved it from Solid to Strong during the month, a fifth reached Strong in the back half, and none moved back. Against that, not one technician on the roster asked for a five-star review with any consistency in any week of July, and the closing week recorded technician after technician whose recordings never reached the Decision step at all. The primary win — the Option C flip — is being executed well by the top third and skipped entirely on the calls that stall.

Top Team-Wide Recurring Gaps

1. The five-star review is planted and never harvested — all 14 technicians, all 4 weeks

This is not a tendency, it is the state of the team. Every single per-rep page in July carries a review-ask gap, and most carry it in every week with a running count attached: planted ten times and harvested zero (Benjamin Wyllie), no review ask anywhere in twenty-four recordings (Brandon Moreno), a third consecutive period (Nikko April), never asking for a review, on a technician customers already praise unprompted (Cole Pantol). Two exceptions are worth naming because they are the only ones: Dustin Romine harvested twice out of six planted in the week ending Jul 12, and Brandon Moreno harvested once out of sixteen in the closing week. Nothing else on this list is as cheap to fix or as universally ignored.

2. Recordings that never reach the Decision step — 8 of 14 technicians in the closing week alone

The week ending Jul 26 produced the same finding on page after page: eighteen of twenty-nine recordings (Benjamin Wyllie), twenty-four of twenty-eight (Joe Mendoza), eighteen of twenty-five (Francisco Valencia), fourteen of twenty-one (Robert Silinzy), fourteen of eighteen (Cole Pantol), thirteen of eighteen (Nikko April), eleven of fourteen (Mario Castro), nine of sixteen (Brandon Moreno). Some of this is recording discipline and some of it is calls genuinely ending at diagnosis, and the two need separating before August — but on any reading, the majority of Sierra’s recorded SILO calls in the last week of July ended without a decision being put to the customer.

3. Technicians doing the Comfort Advisor’s job instead of handing it over — 6 technicians

Andrew Trujillo, Benjamin Wyllie, Brandon Moreno, Francisco Valencia, Juan Tlatenchi and Mario Castro all had weeks flagged for quoting replacement scope, running repair-versus-replace maths, or qualifying a replacement budget themselves. This is the mirror image of the price rule, not a violation of it: routing a price question to the specialist is the correct move and is credited as such throughout. The gap is the technician absorbing work that creates the lead somewhere else.

4. Conceding the call to a home warranty company — 5 technicians

Brandon Moreno (twice, in consecutive weeks), Joe Mendoza, Nikko April, Nathan Colquitt and Andrew Trujillo all recorded a call where a customer’s home warranty ended the conversation. In every case the correct answer was the same and free: offer the no-cost Comfort Advisor comparison and let the customer decide with both numbers in front of them.

5. Non-customer audio on customer recordings — 9 technicians, including conduct issues

Nine of fourteen pages carry a version of this: personal calls, commission and shop talk, internal sales conversation, and in several cases remarks about the customer whose job the recording is attached to. Mario Castro’s opening week captured a racial slur; Benjamin Wyllie, Cole Pantol and Juan Tlatenchi each had derogatory remarks about customers captured in separate weeks. This is a conduct and privacy exposure before it is a coaching topic and it should not be handled inside a sales coaching session.

6. Recordings that cannot be matched to a ServiceTitan job — 3 technicians

Juan Tlatenchi carried this in three consecutive weeks — not one recording matchable in any of them — and Nathan Colquitt had five unmatched recordings in the week ending Jul 12, three of which contained a booked handoff. Cole Pantol had one scorecard that disagreed with ServiceTitan outright. The cost is direct: it makes real converted work invisible, and it is the single reason Juan’s weekly pages understated him all month.

7. Findings delivered without a priced option attached — 7 technicians

Andrew Alonso completing a maintenance on failing parts with no option at all; Mario Castro with no number said out loud, ever; Nathan Colquitt with no option sheet, no Spin It and no Zip It; Noah Weng with no sheet on any recording in the month; Nikko April refusing to write down what he had diagnosed. The diagnosis is right — Assessment is the roster’s strongest step — and it repeatedly arrives with nothing for the customer to act on.

Notable Individual Trends

Juan Tlatenchi — the month’s largest correction, and it runs in his favour

His weekly reports tallied roughly a third of his calls as converting and one week could not be verified at all, because for three consecutive weeks not a single recording of his carried a matchable job number. ServiceTitan credits him with an upper-half close rate for July. Under the locked rule that truth overrides transcript impression in both directions, the ServiceTitan figure stands and his weekly pages have been understating him materially all month. The record-keeping failure is still a real and serious gap — but it is a record-keeping gap, not a performance one.

Mario Castro and Robert Silinzy — corrected downward

Both read better in transcript than in ServiceTitan. Their weekly tallies suggested upper-half close rates; ServiceTitan places both in the lower half for July. The same rule applies in this direction: the transcripts show good calls, and the leads were not created at the rate those calls implied.

Noah Weng — the roster’s flip specialist and nothing else

The highest close rate at Sierra in July by a clear margin, and zero repairs won across the entire month. His Deliver band was Weak in the first and last weeks of July. He is simultaneously the reference example for how to execute the primary win and the clearest case of what happens when the option sheet is never built.

Andrew Trujillo — the sharpest single-week recovery on the roster

His Decision band fell Strong on wins → Moderate → Weak across the first three weeks, bottoming out in the week ending Jul 19 with the fewest handoffs of his month. In the closing week it returned to Strong, his handoffs roughly tripled on a comparable sample, and he recorded his first repair conversions of the month.

Nathan Colquitt — the only declining Decision band that ended at Weak

Moderate → Strong on wins → Moderate → Weak, on the smallest graded sample of any four-week technician, with three separate weeks recording a customer raising the replacement question and not being handed to an advisor. He finishes tied at the bottom of the close-rate table.

Nikko April — Strong front of call, bottom of the table

Ready and Assessment Strong in every week and Welcome improved to Strong and held, yet he finishes tied last on close rate. Three consecutive weeks flagged him routing the customer away from Sierra — an emailed link instead of a visit, the do-it-yourself route, then coaching the customer to buy elsewhere. This is the one trend on the roster that should be addressed as a conversation about intent rather than technique.

Francisco Valencia — the roster’s most stable Assessment, and a slipping close

Strong Assessment in every week without exception and the second-highest close rate of the month, but Decision and Deliver both declined into the closing week, which recorded eighteen of twenty-five recordings never reaching a decision.

Andrew Alonso — the fastest responder to coaching

Joined the coaching set in the week ending Jul 19 and posted the third-highest close rate of the month. Deliver moved Solid → Strong on wins and Ready Solid → Strong in a single week. His limiting factor is recording coverage — eleven of eighteen recordings unusable in the closing week, some because they were never recorded.

Cole Pantol — the front of the call fixed, the back of it unchanged

The Agenda Card appeared in one of twenty-two recordings in the opening week; Welcome reached Strong by the week ending Jul 19 and held, and Assessment moved Solid → Strong and held. Decision banded Strong on wins in all four weeks without moving once, on the largest graded sample at Sierra.

Management Action Summary

  • Conduct items, handled outside coaching and this week. Recordings captured a racial slur (Mario Castro, week ending Jul 5) and derogatory remarks about customers (Benjamin Wyllie, Cole Pantol, Juan Tlatenchi, in separate weeks). Nine of fourteen technicians had non-customer audio on customer recordings. This belongs with a manager, not in a sales coaching session, and several of these were first raised in early July and were still recurring in the closing week.
  • Job numbers on recordings — a reporting integrity problem, not a technician problem. Juan Tlatenchi had three consecutive weeks with nothing matchable and Nathan Colquitt lost three booked handoffs to unmatched recordings. Until this is fixed, weekly close rates will keep understating real performance and coaching will keep being aimed at the wrong thing.
  • Recording coverage. Andrew Alonso had eleven of eighteen recordings unusable in the closing week, some of them because they were not recorded. Multiple technicians had half a week unscoreable. The grading is only as good as the capture.
  • One team-wide behaviour to mandate: the review ask. Every technician plants the five-star seed in the Welcome and essentially nobody harvests it. This is a scripted ten seconds at the close and it is the highest-yield, lowest-effort change available to the team in August.
  • Separate ended-early recordings from ended-early calls. The closing week’s "never reached the Decision step" counts are the single largest finding of the month and they currently conflate two different problems with two different fixes.
  • Route replacement pricing, and say so. Six technicians did the Comfort Advisor’s pricing work themselves in at least one week. The team does not do price transparency by design; the coaching point is to hand the question over and book the visit, never to quote more.

What To Do In August

  • 1. Make the review ask non-optional. Fourteen of fourteen pages, four of four weeks. Script it into the close and audit it weekly. Use Dustin Romine’s week ending Jul 12 — six planted, two harvested — as the working example, because it is the only one the roster produced.
  • 2. Get every call to a decision. Whether the fix is recorder discipline or the Decision step itself, the closing week says the majority of recorded SILO calls ended without a choice put to the customer. Diagnose which of the two it is per technician before coaching either.
  • 3. Build the option sheet on every call, including the flips. Assessment is roster-wide Strong and the diagnosis repeatedly arrives with nothing written down. Noah Weng’s month — the best close rate at Sierra and zero repairs won — is the exact shape of this cost.
  • 4. Fix job-number capture before the next monthly rollup. The August numbers will be as wrong as July’s weekly numbers were for Juan Tlatenchi until this is closed.
  • 5. Handle the conduct items now, separately. Several were raised in the first week of July and were still present in the last.

Method and Coverage

  • What this is. A rollup of the four weekly SILO rollups inside July, merged with ServiceTitan ground truth. Calls already graded by a weekly report were not re-graded; bands are merged and trended, not re-derived from raw transcripts.
  • Bands. Five word bands only — Strong, Strong on wins, Solid, Moderate, Weak. The month band for a step is the band that recurred most often across that technician’s graded weeks, and every per-rep page shows the full week-by-week sequence so a modal band never conceals a moving one. No SILO step is scored numerically anywhere in this report.
  • Close rate. The only performance number used. Taken from ServiceTitan via tgl_truth — leads created off calls run — aggregated per technician across every available day of July. It overrides the weekly reports’ own tallies in both directions, which this month moved Juan Tlatenchi materially up and Mario Castro and Robert Silinzy down.
  • ServiceTitan data gap. tgl_truth files exist for Jul 1 through Jul 28 only. Jul 29, 30 and 31 have no ServiceTitan truth data at all. Those three days are not counted as zero — they are outside the close-rate window, so every close rate in this report covers 28 of July’s 31 days.
  • Weeks covered. Four weekly rollups contributed: weeks ending Jul 5, Jul 12, Jul 19 and Jul 26. Jul 27–31 has no weekly report and was not graded. Daily plans exist through Jul 30 and transcripts through Jul 31, but no banded grading was produced for those days and none has been invented here.
  • Straddling week. The week ending Jul 5 begins Jun 29, so its graded-call counts include two June days, per the spec rule that a straddling week counts for both months it touches.
  • Roster coverage. Fourteen technicians appear. Andrew Alonso first appears in the week ending Jul 19; Dustin Romine appears only in the weeks ending Jul 5 and Jul 12. Jaden Szelestey and Alex Yakovchuk are on the SILO roster but produced no graded calls in July and have no page.
  • Price rule. Under the locked SILO price rule no technician is banded down or given a gap for declining to quote or break down replacement pricing; routing a price question to the Comfort Advisor is credited as correct process. Pricing findings in this report are the opposite failure — technicians doing the advisor’s pricing work themselves.
